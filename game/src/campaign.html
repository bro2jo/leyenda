The Line and the Hand

The Chronicle, played. Fourteen missions, from the morning at Harrow Ford to the night on the mill bridge. The people, the places and the things are the page's own. Most missions are a scene as the page tells it; three are fights told larger than the page tells them, and each of those says so. He starts with the Grace in him and loses it; after that his reach on the board follows the Knots, and his cards carry the rank each Art had in that chapter.

How it differs from a run

  1. A main menu. Go on with the next mission, or go to the journey (the missions as a road, with a spur for each fight told larger than the page), the road, the fire, the cards, the company, or settings.
  2. The road. Once he is on the road in Book II, the menu opens a run of the game itself: three crossings and three captains, fought with the deck he has in the campaign and the pack from the fire, with the game's own camp between battles. His best Renown is kept. Nothing on the road is the Chronicle's.
  3. Growing is done with cards. Hollis, Ash and Wren earn a mark for every fight they take and another for a foe sent off, in missions and on the road. Marks buy ranks, and each rank is yours to place: choose one card of that fighter's own hand, and every copy of it rises, to a third rank at most. Darrow's Arts rise only when the page says so.
  4. Words between friends. Three fights with someone beside him make a bond: that friend's lent card comes already risen from then on, and a line of theirs from the page is kept on the company screen. Eight make a second: he starts with block when they stand with him.
  5. Something to reach. A lit diamond on the board is worth walking to: a card to the top of his pile, a moment's rest. Each one found is a star for the fire, once.
  6. A map longer than the board. The Thousand Steps are three boards long. Bring the whole company down and the view follows; what lies ahead comes onto the board as it comes into sight.
  7. Set it down, pick it up. The board is kept whenever it is your move. Close the page mid-mission or mid-road and the menu offers to pick it up again.
  8. How it went. The telling ends with the fight's own account: who fought, what each dealt, who was sent off, what was found. Tap one foe on the board to see where he alone can strike.
  9. Every mission has its own aim, written under the board: reach a tile, hold for so many turns, walk a stretch, stay on a stone. There is no captain to bring down.
  10. He fights from where he is put. In the House of Menders he moves one step or none. A lit ring marks where someone has to get to; a green one, what must be kept.
  11. Not every opponent is a man. A river, a stair or the cold is fought as a clash: what you deal is ground covered, and its answer is what it costs him.
  12. The deck is the chapter's. Whoever stands beside him lends a card. In the page's own scenes nothing else goes in.
  13. The fire. Every star is a star to spend. At the fire it buys camp cards, up to three, and that pack goes with him into the three fights told larger than the page. A card can be put back for its stars at any time.
  14. Cards are found as he goes. Each mission told leaves one card at the fire, shown when the mission ends; stars open the last two. The cards screen keeps everything he has held, the Arts sealed since the ford, and a place for each card still to find.
  15. He is told what is happening. A mission's card lists what is new: who is beside him, an Art learned or risen, what his body can do now, what he meets and its habit. Over the board, who is with him and the story's own beats are said as they happen, and a rule is explained the first time it matters, once. A tap sends any of it on.
  16. Three stars a mission: one for finishing it, two for doing it the way the page did.

    Pictures to make, with prompts

    For each: a full figure for the clash and a square portrait for the map, filed with game/tools/file_art.py figure|token NAME FILE under the name shown. Words in quotes are the page's; the rest is only enough to get a first picture, and yours to change. Until theirs are made, the sergeant wears the swordsman's picture and the two boys the pikeman's.

    Shared: put this after every figure prompt

    Style: painterly fantasy concept art in the hand-painted style of the attached reference image: visible, elegant brushwork; believable weathered medieval wool, linen, leather and steel with detailed stitching; soft, warm, natural light; grounded historical fantasy. Not a photograph, not a 3D render, not a cartoon.

Composition and output: vertical 2:3 portrait (1024 x 1536). One man only, his whole figure from the top of his head to his feet with a comfortable margin on every side. He is turned three-quarters toward the left edge of the picture, as if facing an opponent who stands just out of frame on the left. Background: one solid, flat chroma blue (#0000FF) filling the whole frame edge to edge: no gradient, no texture, no glow, no vignette. No floor, no ground shadow, no frame, no text, no watermark. Nothing he wears or holds is blue, and the blue casts no light or reflection on him.

Plain dark slate-grey background, no text, no frame. Same painterly style, same clothes and gear, same light.

    A full-body character illustration of a levy sergeant. As the page has him: "a broad man in a buff coat with a voice like a barn door slammed and a sergeant's stick under his arm." He holds the stick, not a sword.

    The boy with the pitchfork pitchfork

    A full-body character illustration of a levy boy of sixteen or so, one of the boys with borrowed pikes, who has brought a hay pitchfork instead and holds it out in front of him with both hands, unsure of it.

    The boy with the horn hornboy

    A full-body character illustration of a levy boy of fifteen or so with a muster horn on a cord round his neck, a borrowed pike too long for him in one hand and the horn half raised in the other.

    A grey cloak greycloak

    A full-body character illustration of one of the Confessor's men: a plain, patient man in a long grey cloak, a list of names folded in his belt. The page gives him the cloak and the walk of a man "walking a market looking for one stall"; nothing else.

    The lymer lymer

    A full-body illustration of a tracking hound on a long leash, the leash running out of the frame to the left. As the page has it: "a heavy, low-built hound, loose in the skin, with a nose like a spade and ears to its jaw, the kind of dog a man keeps for following another man who has gone."

    A rider in a dark coat darkcoat

    A full-body character illustration of a rider on foot beside where his horse would be, in a long dark riding coat, gloved, one hand as if on a rein. The page says only "four riders in dark coats, new since first light."

    A walker walker

    A full-body character illustration of a man walking with his eyes on nothing. As the page has him: "He wore mail under a surcoat whose colours had washed out past reading, and there was frost in his beard." Empty hands.

The Unkneeling · a side game · playtest build five

The Line and the Hand

Move the company on the map, pick a fight, and play it as a hand of cards. This build is about how it looks, sounds and moves: one set of drawn icons, a proper card frame, the ground behind each fight, moves walked tile by tile, a curtain between scenes, a camp that shows your purse, and music under it all. One new rule: a fighter can fall back through the friend behind him.

How to play

  1. On the map, tap one of the company, then a lit tile to move. A move can be taken back until that fighter does something else. A fighter with a friend right behind him can fall back through that friend to the tile beyond, marked with an arrow: it is his whole move. Tap anyone to see what is in force on them; tap an effect to read it.
  2. To fight, tap a foe ringed in red, read the forecast, and engage. Once a battle Darrow can open from two tiles with a throw: thrown cards only, one less breath, and most foes cannot answer.
  3. Rae does not fight. Select her and tap a hurt friend beside her, ringed in green, to mend them and set aside a Wound. A fighter with Rae beside them is lent her hand.
  4. At the table, tap a card to read it and tap again to play, or drag it up onto the field. His answer and its number sit above his head.
  5. End the hand and he answers once. Block you kept becomes guard; breath you kept is held.
  6. Bring down the captain to win the battle, before night falls on turn 30. Every man felled pays a spoil, and one more if Darrow's own hand felled him.
  7. At camp, spend spoils on cards, mending, honing or leaving a card behind. What you do not spend counts toward your Renown.

New in this build

  1. Falling back through a friend. A fighter with a friend right behind him can step through to the open tile beyond. Straight back only, through one friend only, and it is his whole move, so it gets a hurt man out of a ford or off the bridge without opening a way round the line. The foes cannot do it. Measured: on Standard the careful test player wins 93 runs in 100 with the rule and 93 without. On Hard it was worth about 8, so Hard's foes now strike 2 harder than Standard's, not 1, which puts Hard at 77.
  2. Moves are walked. Yours and theirs go tile by tile along the path, with a little dust, instead of sliding straight across.
  3. A curtain between scenes. It crosses the screen with the name of what comes next: the next ground, the camp after a win, the end of a run. The meeting before a clash shows both faces.
  4. The camp shows what you have. The purse, Darrow's health and what a night's rest gives, and your Renown as it stands, which falls as you spend. Every price is on its button, red when you cannot pay; spoils leaving the purse are shown. The next ground is drawn small beside March on.
  5. Music. Your two tracks are in: one under the map and the camp, one at the card table, fading across when the scene changes.
  6. One look. Every icon is drawn in the same hand, and each tree has its own shape as well as its colour: a shield for Footing, a sword for Blade, an arc for Cast. Cards have a frame, a breath gem and a tree gem. Each fight has the ground it is fought on behind it. Blows leave a cut; heavy ones shake the field.
  7. The camp's cards are stronger, and fewer. In the last round of testing no camp card was clearly worth buying. Eighteen are left, most of them improved. Measured on Hard over 150 runs a card, the plain deck with no camp at all won 81 runs in 100; one copy of Dig In or Blood Up added 7 or 8, most of the others a little or nothing. Cards that pay spoils or mend cannot show their worth in that test, which has no camp to spend in.
  8. Hammerfall has a reason to exist. It deals 14, pins him, and halves his answer for the hand.
  9. Pressing on costs less. In a clash of several rounds he now strikes 2 harder each round you press, not 4. Over 60 runs that moved the careful test player from 58 runs won in 100 to 62, which is inside the noise: in that mode the losses come on the third ground, from the wear of two battles, more than from this cost.

What testing changed

  1. The cards flashed because they were being rebuilt. Playing one card threw away the rest of the hand and made it again, twice, pictures and all. Every tap on the map did the same to all the tokens. Now a card or token is made once and kept: the hand closes up when a card leaves, and tokens slide to their new tile.
  2. Darrow's throw from two tiles was the best way to win, by a long way. With it the look-ahead player won 93 runs in 100; without it, 54. It let him fight all battle with nobody able to answer, and his sword cards went unplayed. He now has one opening throw a battle.
  3. Broken halves an answer instead of cancelling it. Before, the foe could not answer in three hands out of four, so block hardly mattered.
  4. The danger moved to where you can do something about it. Foes hit 2 softer on their own turn and 2 harder in their answer, which a block card can stop.
  5. The third battle is less of a cliff. Its foes no longer get a second step up in strength on top of the banner and the captain's temper.
  6. Rae tends three times a battle. Unlimited, the right play was to stall and heal to full before every captain.
  7. A battle ends at nightfall on turn 30, and the captain stops waiting on turn 8. Without these a fight could be drawn out for ever by running away.
  8. Spoils are scarcer, with two reasons to earn them well. A foe felled by Darrow's own hand pays one more, and each turn under 10 in a battle won is worth a point of Renown.
  9. Smaller things. A move can be taken back. End the turn says how many of yours can still act. There is a difficulty setting and a button to start a new run. A refused card shakes in place instead of jumping to the corner.

What is new, and where it came from

  1. A camp between battles. Five cards on offer, one of each, bought with spoils. From Dominion, where a shared supply of limited piles is bought from each turn and the cards join your deck.
  2. Leaving a card behind. Dominion calls it trashing and Slay the Spire sells it at the merchant: a smaller deck draws its best cards more often.
  3. Spoils against Renown. In Dominion the cards that score do nothing in play, so every purchase is a choice between a stronger deck and a higher score. Here, spoils you keep are your score.
  4. Wounds. Dominion's Curses and the Spire's status cards: dead cards an enemy puts in your deck. The crossbowman's bolt does it.
  5. Stances. The Spire's Power cards: played once, in force until the fight ends.
  6. Foes with one habit each. Tough is Runeterra's keyword for taking less from every blow. The banner, the flurry and the captain's temper each ask for a different answer.
  7. Three grounds. Two fords, a road through a wood, and a bridge. Fire Emblem's lesson: the same units play differently when the ground changes.
  8. Leftover damage and friends who strike. Follow Through is Runeterra's Overwhelm turned sideways. Call the Company pays you for standing the company around one man.

Style: painterly fantasy concept art in the hand-painted style of the attached reference image: visible, elegant brushwork; believable weathered medieval wool, linen, leather and steel with detailed stitching; soft, warm, natural light; grounded historical fantasy. Not a photograph, not a 3D render, not a cartoon.

Composition and output: vertical 2:3 portrait (1024 x 1536). One man only, his whole figure from the top of his head to his feet with a comfortable margin on every side. He is turned three-quarters toward the left edge of the picture, as if facing an opponent who stands just out of frame on the left. Background: one solid, flat chroma blue (#0000FF) filling the whole frame edge to edge: no gradient, no texture, no glow, no vignette. No floor, no ground shadow, no frame, no text, no watermark. Nothing he wears or holds is blue, and the blue casts no light or reflection on him.

What to watch for while you play

  1. Do you fight with Darrow, or hide him? The best test player still leans on the companions and keeps him back, because losing him ends the run. If you find yourself doing the same, his cards need to be worth the risk.
  2. Do you block? His answer now lands in about half of all hands. If you never play a block card, or always do, the numbers are off.
  3. Is Standard too hard on a first run? Careless play wins about two runs in five. Try Gentle first if it stings.
  4. Is the camp a real choice? Spoils are tight now: a card or a mend, rarely both.
  5. Does anything still flash, jump or stutter? Say where, and whether it is the map or the card table.

How it was tested

The rules, apart from the screen

629 rule checks, including one for every card that it does what its text says. 4,000 random games with every card, health total and effect accounted for after each move.

The screen

130 browser checks at phone, small-phone and desktop sizes, and 24 whole runs through the real buttons. A separate count confirms that a tap on the map or a card played rebuilds nothing on screen.

Three test players

One looks ahead: it tries each fight on a copy of the game, plays the hand out several ways and keeps the best. It wins 93 runs in 100 on Standard and 77 on Hard. One plays sensibly but carelessly and wins 40. One only chases the captain and wins 35.

What they cannot tell you

Whether it is fun, and how it feels under a real thumb. A person will play Darrow more boldly than the careful player does, and that is the run I most want to hear about.

Others who mixed a map with cards

Fights in Tight Spaces

One fighter on a small grid against several. The cards are the moves themselves, including stepping and shoving, and enemies show what they will do next so you can push one into another's line of fire.

Card Hunter

A party of three on a board. Each character's deck of 36 comes from their class and what they carry, and you play one card at a time to move or strike.

Metal Gear Acid

Any card can be spent for its effect or simply to move. A card's cost is time: the more you spend, the longer until that character acts again.

Gloomhaven

Two cards a turn, the top half of one and the bottom of the other. No dice; a small deck of modifiers does that job.

All four make the cards the moves. This design keeps movement free, the Fire Emblem way, and uses cards only for the blow, which is why a clash can be a single hand. Card Hunter's idea that the deck is what the character carries is the same as the deck being the sheet.

The deck as it stands

Darrow's deck is the sheet: one card for each Art, its rank on the card, plus plain Strikes and Guards. The last row is what the company lends, plays, and offers between battles. Ten of his thirteen cards already have a picture.

The rest of the design

The company

Each companion has a small deck of their own and plays a hand of three on two breath. Hollis hits hard and moves little. Ash is fast and pins a man in place. Wren reaches two tiles and marks a foe so the next friend has more breath against him.

A run

Three battles, each a little harder. Darrow's health carries from one to the next; the company returns whole. After a win, take one of three cards for the rest of the run, mend, or take nothing so the deck stays lean. Inspiration is three for the whole run.

What the sheet does

Arts
The cards. A new rank is a better card.
Knots
How far he moves, and which cards are still sealed.
HP, Might
How long he stands and how hard the plain cards hit.
Ember
His breath each clash.
Inspiration
A new hand.
Regard
Works as a support rank: how good a companion's lent card is.

Nothing is kept

A run lends cards and ends. An Art, a rank or a point of Might only ever comes from the Ledger. The figure in the clash is a look from the Wardrobe, so what he wears there could be what stands on the field.

## Your training rules mostly pass the evidence test

*Background evidence for a recreational-competitive ultimate handler at week 11 after ACL reconstruction with a patellar-tendon (BPTB) graft, in Phase 4 of a criteria-based plan. Your PT and surgeon outrank everything here. This report is your lowest-priority source, and nothing in it is a new exercise, load, dose or date. Where the evidence points at a possible change, it becomes a question for Tuesday (last section).*

## Bottom line

- **Your best-supported rule is "RPE 7–8, never to failure."** Meta-analyses find it builds the same strength as training to failure, which costs recovery time and blunts jump and sprint gains. Your step sizes, reps-before-load and "one change at a time" are sensible convention that no trial has tested.
- **Your "3–4 in reserve" is probably more conservative than it feels.** People underestimate their reps left by about one on average, which errs on the safe side for a healing athlete.
- **The upper-body blocks and the every-4th-week deload are reasonable, not superior.** Hard, progressive sets twice a week matter more than the periodization model. The 20% velocity-loss cutoff is well supported. The 10% cutoff for power points the right way, but no trial isolates it.
- **Your week is already a high/low design.** At least 48 h between knee days matches the 48–72 h recovery convention. Easy cycling on separate days barely interferes with lifting, but it won't build running tolerance.
- **The Phase 4 exit gate is stricter than most published running criteria** (typically ~12 weeks, ~80% quad symmetry and pain-free single-leg hopping).
- **Quad strength is the test most tied to reinjury, but symmetry scores can flatter** if the other leg weakened too. On Tuesday, ask which device is used and whether there's a body-weight-normalized number.
- **Reinjury risk falls with time *and* with passing criteria.** Nine months is the evidence's floor. By your plan's week count that's about late May 2027, where your plan already puts game speed.
- **For tracking:** log load × reps × reps in reserve, session effort × minutes, every throw, every jump contact and the morning check. Skip ACWR.

**Evidence labels.** Most full texts couldn't be opened, so unmarked figures come from abstracts or index records. Three labels flag weaker sources:

- **(secondhand)**: taken from a summary that cites the paper; check the original before quoting.
- **(not re-checked)**: a well-known paper cited from the researcher's memory, with a DOI link.
- **Convention**: textbook or coaching practice with no trial behind the specific rule.

Even the main ACL rehab guideline rates most of its own recommendations as very low certainty ([Aspetar CPG 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36731908/)).

## Stopping short of failure is your best-backed rule

| Your rule | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Main lifts RPE 7–8, never failure | Several meta-analyses | Strongly supported |
| Loads picked by feel, not % | Meta-analysis + trial | Supported |
| New loads at 3–4 in reserve | Studies of RIR accuracy | Supported; conservative |
| Steps ~2.5–10% or one increment | Textbook heuristic | Convention |
| Reps first, then load | One trial (not re-checked) | Supported |
| One variable at a time | None | Convention |
| Unclean morning reverses changes | Monitoring review | Supported in spirit; rule is convention |

**RPE** is a 1–10 effort rating. **RIR (reps in reserve)** is how many clean reps you could still have done, so RPE 8 ≈ 2 RIR.

**Failure adds little strength and costs recovery.** A 55-study analysis found **strength gains similar across a wide range of RIR**, with muscle growth rising modestly closer to failure ([Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)). Another found **non-failure training better for strength when volume wasn't matched** ([Grgic 2022](https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662)). The 2026 ACSM position stand, an overview of 137 reviews, lists failure training among the variables with **inconsistent effects** ([ACSM 2026](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3873753)). The costs fall on exactly what a cutter needs. Volume-matched failure sets delayed recovery of jump height for up to 48 h ([Morán-Navarro 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3725-7), not re-checked). Grinding deeper into sets went with **smaller jump and sprint gains and no extra strength** ([Jukic 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/f7e501b3-00b2-4694-bd3a-364a552f9c45)).

**Choosing loads by feel works, though the calls run conservative.** Across 15 studies, autoregulated loading produced **one-rep-max (1RM) gains similar to fixed percentages** ([Hickmott 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/)). Trained lifters did slightly better with RPE-chosen loads ([Helms 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/pdf)). The catch is accuracy. Across 12 studies, lifters **underestimated their reserve by about one rep**, with wide individual spread, and they were least accurate far from failure ([Halperin 2022](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)). A set logged "3 in reserve" at 8–10 reps probably had 4 or more. A bar-speed device or video can calibrate this without ever testing failure.

**The rest is convention, and nothing contradicts it.**

- **Step sizes.** Your 2.5–10% steps match the textbook heuristic of ~2.5–5% upper body and ~5–10% lower body. NSCA's "2-for-2" trigger adds load after the target is beaten by two reps in two straight sessions. Both reach us only through study guides, and no trial has compared increment sizes ([CSCS study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cscs/program-design/resistance-training-program-design)).
- **Reps before load.** One trial found adding reps gave similar growth and broadly similar strength to adding load ([Plotkin 2022](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14142), not re-checked). That matters with light dumbbells, where one step can exceed 10%.
- **One change at a time.** Never tested. Its value is diagnostic: when the knee reacts, you know which change did it.

**The morning check.** A 56-study review found self-reported wellness tracks training load more sensitively than objective markers ([Saw 2016](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/5/281.short)). Athletes also commonly back off when joint aches rise ([Rogerson 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10948666/)). Generic monitoring advice weighs multi-day trends more heavily than one bad morning. Your rule, where one unclean morning reverses the session's changes, is stricter, and that suits a healing knee. The researchers found no trial of swelling-response rules, so the exact rule is clinical convention and stays the PT's.

## Upper-body blocks are sound, but the order isn't magic

**Periodization model.** A 35-study, volume-matched meta-analysis found periodized training beat non-periodized training for 1RM by a **small margin (effect size ≈0.3)**, with no difference in muscle growth. Day-to-day or week-to-week variation beat a straight linear climb **only in trained lifters**, and too few studies existed to judge block periodization at all ([Moesgaard 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)). ACSM 2026 rates periodization as inconsistent too ([ACSM 2026 release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)). What reliably mattered:

- heavier loads (≥80% 1RM) for strength;
- 2–3 sets per exercise;
- the lift placed early in the session;
- at least 2 sessions a week;
- 30–70% 1RM moved fast for power, which is the speed-bench idea.

Your 3×8–10 → 4×6–8 → 4×3–5 → power sequence, twice a week, sits inside that. Extra sets show **steeper diminishing returns for strength** than for muscle growth, while frequency helps strength specifically ([Pelland 2026](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); effect sizes not retrievable).

**Deloads.** In a survey of 246 strength athletes, deloads lasted **about 6 days, every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks**, and were mostly planned. Athletes cut sets and reps, kept their training days, lightened the load slightly and left more in reserve ([Rogerson 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10948666/)). Coaches describe 5–7 days every 4–6 weeks ([Bell 2022](https://www.pubmed.gov/36619355/)). Every 4th week is the frequent end, which is reasonable while surgery recovery shares the budget. The only RCT tested a full week *off*, which cost some lower-body strength gain and changed nothing else ([Coleman 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/)). That thin evidence favors a lighter week over a week off, and that is how your plan already defines the deload that starts 11/15: sets cut 30–40%, loads held.

**The two-complete-weeks rule** for advancing a block has no trial behind it. Maintenance data show why a missed session costs little. One heavy session a week held strength and 40-m sprint for 12 weeks in pro soccer players, while one every other week did not ([Rønnestad 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21873897/)). Strength held for months on a small dose **as long as intensity was kept** ([Spiering 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972)). A missed day leaves nothing to cram.

**Velocity cutoffs.** VBT (velocity-based training) uses a bar-speed sensor. Velocity loss (VL) is the percentage drop from a set's fastest rep. In an 8-week squat trial, stopping sets at 20% VL instead of 40% ([Pareja-Blanco 2017](https://accedacris.ulpgc.es/handle/10553/35727?locale=en)):

- gave **equal strength with ~40% fewer reps**;
- **improved jump height more (+9.5% vs +3.5%)**;
- preserved the fastest muscle-fiber type, which 40% VL depleted.

A meta-analysis agrees that VL doesn't change strength gains, while higher VL costs jump and sprint ([Jukic 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/f7e501b3-00b2-4694-bd3a-364a552f9c45)). Two limits apply. Nearly all these trials used squat and bench in trained young men with **4-minute rests** ([Pareja-Blanco 2020](https://rio.upo.es/entities/publication/6c8a1311-ebf1-43a8-9dbc-f02495a9f316/full)). Shorter rests make any cutoff arrive sooner. On rest periods themselves:

- about 3 minutes beat 1 minute for strength ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272), not re-checked);
- for muscle growth, little is gained past ~90 seconds ([Singer 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)).

**Triphasic** (the eccentric → isometric → concentric sequence your off-season plan cites) is a **coaching model with almost no outcome data**. A 2023 volleyball study noted that no prior research had evaluated it in elite athletes ([Springer 2023](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s12662-023-00877-8)). One basketball conference abstract found a *worse* jump result than with traditional training ([IJESAB](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol2/iss5/74)). Eccentric training itself is well supported for strength ([Douglas 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0628-4), not re-checked). Longer lowering phases gave similar or more strength, while shorter ones favored jumping ([Amdi & King 2025](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-40/does-eccentric-speed-impact-your-gains/), secondhand). So slow-eccentric phases may dull jumping for a while.

## Your week already runs on a high/low rhythm

**High/low** comes from the sprint coach Charlie Francis. It alternates hard days (sprints, jumps, heavy lifts) with easy ones, with 48–72 h between hard days. It reaches us through practitioner summaries, not trials ([Rugby Renegade](https://rugbyrenegade.com/high-low-periodisation-for-rugby/)), but recovery data agree with it. Plyometric guidance spaces hard sessions 48–72 h apart ([NSCA study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/nsca-cpt/mobility-bodyweight-performance/plyometrics)). Soccer performance markers recover in about 72 h after a match ([Silva 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Silva+2018+acute+and+residual+soccer+match-related+fatigue+meta-analysis), not re-checked). Four knee days never back to back, with upper, bike and throwing in between, is this layout.

**The bike barely touches your lifting.** A 43-study meta-analysis found **no interference with maximal strength or muscle growth**, only a small blunting of explosive strength, **mainly when both were done in the same session** rather than 3 h or more apart ([Schumann 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)). Rugby players gained more with 24 h between strength and aerobic sessions than with 0 or 6 h ([Robineau 2016](https://hal-univ-bourgogne.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01409685)). An older analysis found running interfered and cycling didn't ([Wilson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)). The limit is that cycling carries heart-and-lung fitness over to running, but not running economy or impact tolerance ([Tanaka 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Tanaka+1994+cross-training+transfer+VO2max+cycling+running+swimming), not re-checked). Those return only with running.

**Throwing has no ultimate-specific evidence.** No study measures throwing volume, arm-injury rates or a return-to-throwing plan in ultimate. The principle borrowed from baseball's interval-throwing programs is to **count every throw, warm-ups included, and build volume before distance or intensity** ([interval-throwing review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10837817/)). Pitch-count numbers shouldn't transfer: a 175 g disc stresses the arm far less per throw, and baseball's own workload ratios are contested. A disc golf survey linked forehand throwing to elbow complaints, which is weak evidence ([Musculoskeletal Key](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/ultimate-frisbee/)). Once throws add pivots, step-outs or movement, they become knee loading, and that's the PT's call.

**Session order and warm-ups.** The standard order runs warm-up → jumps/speed → heavy strength → accessories → conditioning. It comes from NSCA teaching, which also says to end a jump set when landings degrade ([CSCS notes](https://www.ptpioneer.com/personal-training/certifications/nsca-cscs/cscs-chapter-18/)). The hard data cover only part of that order. Lifting before endurance work gave **~7% more lower-body strength** than the reverse ([Eddens 2018](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-intra-session-exercise-sequence-on-the-interference-e/)). No trial has tested jumps-before-lifting long term.

Your knee sessions already follow this order: impact work comes right after the warm-up, before the first lift, and stops while landings are still clean.

For warm-ups, the FIFA 11+ injury-prevention routine cut football injuries (pooled risk ratio 0.75; 0.61 for 11+ alone, a subgroup) ([Thorborg 2017](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/49470)). Strength-based prevention programs cut injuries to about a third, and stretching showed no effect ([Lauersen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092538), not re-checked). No prevention trial exists in ultimate.

## The knee gate sits at the strict end of the literature

A scoping review of 201 studies found a **median clearance to run of 12 weeks (range 5–39)**. Fewer than 1 in 5 studies used any clinical, strength or performance criterion ([Rambaud 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29720478/)). The Aspetar guideline proposes **~80% quad symmetry plus pain-free single-leg hopping**, built on limited research and clinical judgment (secondhand) ([Aspetar CPG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36731908/)). Its journal says 12 weeks alone "appears inadequate" ([Aspetar Journal](https://journal.aspetar.com/en/journals/volume-12-targeted-topic-rehabilitation-after-acl-injury/the-latest-guidance-on-return-to-run-after-acl-reconstruction)). Your gate covers all of that and adds hamstring and hip thresholds. No published glute threshold was found, and nothing suggests the gate is too strict.

**Quad strength and LSI.** LSI (limb symmetry index) is the operated leg's score as a percentage of the other leg's. In the Delaware-Oslo cohort, quad symmetry was the only single criterion that predicted reinjury: **33% reinjured below 90% vs 12.5% at or above** (secondhand) ([Grindem 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/302594763_Simple_decision_rules_can_reduce_reinjury_risk_by_84_after_ACL_reconstruction_The_Delaware-Oslo_ACL_cohort_study)). BPTB grafts leave **bigger quad deficits and smaller hamstring deficits** than hamstring grafts ([Xergia 2011](https://www.springermedicine.com/the-influence-of-graft-choice-on-isokinetic-muscle-strength-4-24/20605864)), so the quad number matters most for you. Hamstring-to-quad balance still matters: each 10% drop in the ratio went with roughly ten times the graft-rupture risk (secondhand) ([Kyritsis 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303465632_Likelihood_of_ACL_graft_rupture_Not_meeting_six_clinical_discharge_criteria_before_return_to_sport_is_associated_with_a_four_times_greater_risk_of_rupture)).

Symmetry scores have two pitfalls:

- **The other leg weakens too.** Patients at 90% symmetry at 6 months would fail against the other leg's *pre-surgery* numbers ([Wellsandt 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315720674_Limb_Symmetry_Indexes_Can_Overestimate_Knee_Function_After_Anterior_Cruciate_Ligament_Injury), secondhand).
- **The bar takes a long time to clear.** At 5–7 months, 85% of patients missed either a symmetry or an absolute criterion ([PubMed 36094575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36094575)).

A common absolute benchmark is **~3.0 Nm/kg on an isometric test** ([IJSPT](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/166786-isokinetic-quadriceps-and-hamstring-peak-torque-in-athletes-aged-10-50-preoperative-uninvolved-limb-reference-values-for-acl-reconstruction)). The device used also changes what the number means. Your 90.4% at week 8 clears the 70–80% running thresholds, but the ≥90% return-to-sport bar is a later, separate question. Blood-flow-restriction training has moderate-certainty evidence for quad strength ([Hu 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23259671261440196)). It's a question for the PT if quad gains ever stall, not now.

**The impact ladder.** It matches the early, two-legged, vertical end of the most-cited post-ACL plyometric framework ([Buckthorpe & Della Villa 2021](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/23549-recommendations-for-plyometric-training-after-acl-reconstruction-a-clinical-commentary)). That framework counts foot contacts and progresses along four lines:

- two legs → one leg;
- longer → shorter ground contacts;
- higher entry speed;
- vertical → horizontal → multidirectional, with shallow step-cuts later.

Your ceiling of about 48 contacts a session (2 × 3 × 8) is well below healthy-athlete ranges, by design. In healthy volleyball players, lower jump volumes worked about as well as higher ones ([Ramirez-Campillo 2020](https://jssm.org/jssm-19-489.xml-abst)).

**Today's filmed drop jump** is worth more than a distance number, because **symmetric hop distance doesn't mean normal landing mechanics** ([JOSPT 2018](https://www.jospt.org/doi/full/10.2519/jospt.2018.7817)). No validated drop-jump scoring cutoff turned up, and no study validates single-leg pogos as a running test. "Acceptable" and "pain-free" are the PT's clinical judgments.

**The graft site.** BPTB's characteristic complaint is front-of-knee pain from the harvest, scarring and the infrapatellar nerve ([WJO 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12836189)). No study links jump dosing to it. Pain during jumping and the next morning is still the symptom clinicians plausibly watch as impacts rise.

**Open-chain extension.** "Open-chain" means the foot is free, as on a knee-extension machine. A meta-analysis found **no difference in laxity, strength or function** between open- and closed-chain quad work, including patellar-tendon graft subgroups. The authors still advised caution in the first 12 weeks, and the evidence covers only ~200 people at low-to-moderate quality ([Perriman 2018](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2018.7656)). A 2024 review found open-chain work equal or better for laxity ([Pamboris 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1416690/full)). Nothing retrieved covers step size or when full-range loading should start, so the one-plate rule is purely the PT's.

## After the gate: criteria, then chaos, then nine months

| Your plan | Published criteria-based guides | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 5 running, not before wk 13 (11/17) | Median clearance 12 wk; 12–16 common | Fits; criteria do the work |
| Phase 6 (~late Dec–Feb): speed, plyo, cutting, Nordics | Between running and the 9-month floor, criteria-paced; no sourced month ranges | Fits |
| Phase 7 (~late Feb–May): reactive plyo, agility, max velocity | Unpredictable ("chaos") work comes last | Fits |
| Phase 8 (~late May 2027): game speed, small-sided games | Return to sport ≥9 months plus criteria | 9 months ≈ late May |

**Running.** Guides start with walk–jog intervals on flat, predictable ground, which is weak evidence ([Physio Network](https://www.physio-network.com/blog/acl-rehab-when-can-i-run-again/)). **No trial shows how to progress running volume or speed after ACL reconstruction.** One guideline gates each step of running → sprinting → plyometrics → agility → cutting → competition ([Brinlee 2022](https://www.ovid.com/journals/sphe/abstract/10.1177/19417381211056873~acl-reconstruction-rehabilitation-clinical-data-biologic?redirectionsource=fulltextview)).

**Cutting.** On-field frameworks run in five stages: linear movement, multidirectional movement, sport skills, sport movements and modified practice. Each stage adds unpredictability along the **control–chaos continuum** ([Bioengineering 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/bioengineering12111229)), and progression is mostly gated by criteria ([scoping review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42688941/)). The intensity dial is **cut angle × approach speed** ([Dos'Santos 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6132493/)). In women's league ultimate, players made ~**1.5 cuts a minute, mostly at shallow 30–90° angles**, and more competitive teams cut faster with less knee bend ([Slaughter 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7696432/)). Sharp cuts are a small slice of the game and come last in most progressions. Dedicated deceleration programs are scarce ([deceleration review 2024](https://researchoutput.csu.edu.au/files/573899577/464718415_published_article.pdf)). No study describes the handler's pivot or the lunge to break the mark.

**What ultimate demands.** Data from one 12-player study show handlers walking more and sprinting and braking less than cutters ([positional study](https://www.academia.edu/100622340/External_match_loads_imposed_upon_Ultimate_Frisbee_players_A_comparison_between_playing_positions)). Competitive games averaged **~82% of max heart rate, with over 40% of time above 90%**. High-intensity running fell **43–47% in the last 9 minutes of each half** ([Krustrup & Mohr 2015](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/FullText/2015/12000/Physical_Demands_in_Competitive_Ultimate_Frisbee.16.aspx)).

**Injuries.** Pro ultimate logged **33 injuries per 1,000 player-exposures, 72% in the lower limb**, led by **thigh strains (12.7%)** and **ankle sprains (11.4%)**, and more often in second halves ([Hess 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895593/)). Layouts raised injury risk ~1.7× ([systematic review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7767421/)). Nordic hamstring programs roughly halve hamstring injuries in other sports ([van Dyk 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=van+Dyk+2019+Nordic+hamstring+exercise+meta-analysis+BJSM), not re-checked), which fits their place in Phase 6.

**Conditioning tests.** In ultimate players, Yo-Yo IR1 tracked sprint distance (r = 0.74) and IR2 tracked late-half high-intensity running (r = 0.69) ([Krustrup & Mohr](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276461963_Physical_Demands_in_Competitive_Ultimate_Frisbee)). The 30-15 IFT is reliable, and one 0.5 km/h stage counts as a real change ([Frontiers 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2016.00510/full)), but it is **not validated in ultimate**. All of these tests involve 180° turns, so they belong after cutting is cleared.

**Time and criteria together.** Three findings set the 9-month floor:

- In Delaware-Oslo, reinjury fell **51% for each month return was delayed, up to 9 months**. Athletes who passed criteria reinjured at **5.6% vs 38.2%** for those who didn't (secondhand) ([Grindem 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/302594763_Simple_decision_rules_can_reduce_reinjury_risk_by_84_after_ACL_reconstruction_The_Delaware-Oslo_ACL_cohort_study)).
- Young athletes returning before 9 months had **~7× the rate of a new ACL injury** ([Beischer 2020](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2020.9071)).
- An international consensus says to abandon time-only decisions ([Panther 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32647735/)).

Second ACL injuries average **~15%, and ~23% in young athletes who return**, split between graft and other knee ([Wiggins 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26772611/)). These are mostly under-25 data. In adolescents the other knee's risk overtakes the graft's over time ([Webster & Feller 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23259671251317490)), which is why prevention work continues after return. Psychological readiness counts too, but no single cutoff holds. In one study, *high* readiness scores at 3 months went with more second injuries (secondhand) ([Ueda 2024](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/sms/resource/pt/mdl-38584989)).

## What to log this week, and what to skip

Most of this is already in your logs: session minutes and effort (session load is computed from them), every set's load and reps, throws with the pivot foot, and the morning check. The one item not yet captured is reps in reserve; say it when you report a set and it goes in the notes.

| Log | Why | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Each set: load × reps × RIR (+ bar speed if you have a device) | Runs reps-before-load and the RPE rule; RIR calls run ~1 rep conservative | Meta-analyses |
| Session effort (0–10, ~30 min after) × minutes, every session incl. bike and throwing | Whole-week load; watch week-to-week change | Widely used ([Foster 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Foster+2001+new+approach+to+monitoring+exercise+training), not re-checked) |
| Morning check (as now) | Self-report tracks load better than objective markers | Systematic review |
| Every throw, warm-ups included; backhand/forehand; planted/pivot | Baseline for any change; pivots load the knee | Borrowed from baseball; weak |
| Jump contacts, landing quality, next-morning front-of-knee pain | Ladder volume; BPTB graft site | Convention / inference |

**Skip ACWR.** The acute:chronic workload ratio is this week's load divided by the average of the last ~4 weeks. Its apparent link to injury is largely a statistical artifact ([Impellizzeri 2020](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/141898)). The same "association" appears even when chronic load is swapped for random numbers ([Impellizzeri 2021](https://iris.univr.it/retrieve/e34cfb98-e922-4c2f-aab5-18583ab7e31b/Impellizzeri_What%20Role%20Do%20Chronic%20Workloads%20Play_SportMed_2021.pdf)). Plain week-to-week change does the job, though no validated threshold exists.

**Wait on a weekly jump test.** Team sports often add one, and averaging several countermovement jumps is more sensitive than taking the best ([Claudino 2017](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2538)). For you it's still a jump, so it waits for the PT.

**Treat red flags as stop signs, not data.** Sudden swelling, giving way, loss of extension, calf pain or fever mean stop and call the surgical team or PT.

## Conclusion

Across all four research areas the pattern is an inversion. The plan's plainest rules carry the most evidence: stop short of failure, space hard days, keep intensity when volume drops, stay consistent. Its named methods and specific tests carry the least: high/low, triphasic, the 30-15 IFT for ultimate, single-leg pogos as a test, and exact step sizes. Nothing contradicts those methods, so there's no reason to drop them. It does mean the program's real protection comes from its gates and its morning check, not from clever periodization.

The biggest open question right now is measurement, not programming. Which device produced the 90.4%? What counts as an "acceptable" landing? How many throws actually happen in a week? The literature can't answer any of that, but Tuesday's visit and this week's log can. After that, the risk shifts from tissue healing to the move from control to chaos: fast, shallow cuts, late-half fatigue and hamstring strains. Through that stretch, the evidence protects the knee with time and criteria together, never one alone.

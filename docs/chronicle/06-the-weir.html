Chapter 6The Weir

Scene 1Three Fingers and a Thumb

The last two fingers of his right hand had not come back.

The frost had gone out of the rest of him by mid-morning, the first morning since the mountain that it had, and he had walked at Hob’s head up the mill tracks waiting for those two to follow. They had not. When he closed his fist on the stick they came last and stopped short, a hair off the palm, and stayed there.

At noon Maelis would read the Knots, a week since the Steps. She would put two fingers either side of the knee and shut her eyes, and when she opened them she would look at his hands, because she always looked at hands. He had until noon.

He worked them as he walked. Fist, open, fist, on the stick, from one gate to the next, as she had once made him work a thigh that would not wake. By the third gate they were warmer and no quicker. By the fourth he had moved the stick to his left hand and put the right inside his coat, against his ribs, where it could be warm and nobody’s business.

The tracks climbed between hedges higher than the cart, two ruts and a crown of frozen grass. When the sun found the hedge-tops the frost came off them in drops, and the drops found Hollis on the tailboard, who told each one what he thought of it. Rae drove with the rag still in the bell at her hip from Coldmere, and the only sound from her all morning was the leather of the lines. He had chosen the quiet road at the fork, over the one she had looked at longer, and he owed her the hearing of her silence about it. He let her keep it.

Twice he looked back down the track toward Coldmere, where a miller’s man had said a rider was asking after a captain with a stick, and saw only hedge.

Behind the cart Maelis walked with her roll on her shoulder and her eyes on the road.

The tracks came down at noon to a brook over brown stones, with a slab laid across for feet and a ford beside it for wheels, and a bank where the sun had got in and the grass was green instead of white. Rae stopped the oxen at the water with the word from her belly, the first of her voice Darrow had had all day, and it was for Hob and Dun and nobody else.

“Sit,” said Maelis.

He sat on the tailboard with the leg out along the boards and his right hand inside his coat. She put the pipe in her teeth, set two fingers either side of the knee and shut her eyes, and the company found things to do a little way off.

It took as long as it took. Then she opened her eyes.

“It’s walking. Walk it. Till I say.”

He began to get down.

“The other one,” said Maelis, and held out her hand.

She had seen him put it away at the third gate. She had walked behind him all morning with her eyes on the road and let him.

“You’d do better hiding a limp,” she said.

He gave her the hand.

She turned it palm up on her knee, pressed the last two fingers flat with her thumb and let them go. They came back up late, behind the others, like men late to a muster who have heard the horn and are coming at a walk.

“Close it.”

He closed it. They stopped short.

She pinched the little finger at the last joint, hard. “Feel that?”

“Late.”

“Late.” She let go. “The frost went into those two at the kennel grate, and what frost takes that deep it keeps. They’ve come back as far as they’ll come. Slow, and half there. It’ll be a different grip on a sword from now on, and on anything else you mean to hold.” She took the pipe out of her teeth. “I’ll not dress it. A Mender who lies to a patient buries him.”

Down at the water nobody was talking.

“I’d have taken that about the knee,” he said.

“I know you would. You’ve been waiting for it since the river.”

“Yes.”

“The knee I bound with your own sinew and a thread it cost somebody their life to learn. There’s no thread for a finger. It’s not the hand you had. You’ve a season to learn the one you’ve got.”

He got down off the tailboard and took the stick in the hand he had always taken it in. It turned in the slack of the two fingers, and the ferrule went where it liked on the frozen ground. He moved his hand up the stick and took it high, in the thumb and the first two fingers, with the other two laid along the wood, and leaned on it, and the stick stayed where he had put it.

“There,” said Maelis.

“That’s how my grandmother held a spoon.”

“Did she drop it?”

“Never once.”

“Then hold the spoon.”

“Give me the sword,” he said.

She brought the oilcloth bundle out from under the salt and unwrapped the hilt and a hand’s breadth of dull blade, with the one word cut near the hilt that he had laughed at on the mountain.

He took it high, three fingers and a thumb, the other two along the leather with nothing to do. The sword sat farther back in his hand, and lighter. He cut the air once over the stream, slowly, and the point went where the wrist went and stopped there. His old hand, out of long habit, would have sent it a span past the mark.

“Well?” said Maelis.

“It’s a smaller sword than it was. And it’s quicker in the stop.” He watched the two idle fingers ride along the hilt. “I’d sooner have the fingers.”

“So would I.”

“There’s a grey in Coldmere I’ll want a rein for in the spring.”

“A rein wants two good fingers, and you’ve three. Ask me about a saddle in the spring, and not before.” She held out her hand for the sword.

He kept it a moment longer.

“But I’ll take the stop,” he said.

“About time,” said Hollis.

He had come up on the beech leg with a hand on the mule’s neck for a rail, and he looked at the hand on the hilt as a farrier looks at a foot.

“Every man with ten fingers holds a sword like he’s strangling a goose, Captain. You did. I watched you do it in the cloister and let you, because you’d have argued.” He flicked the two slack fingers with a nail. “The bind’s in the first two and the thumb. It always was. Those two are for the pommel and for showing the ladies, and they’re what puts a cut a span past where you wanted it and your guard open behind it. The Seated Blade never had a use for your little finger. Now you’ve none for it either. That’s not a loss. That’s an argument finished.”

“You’ve been waiting to say that since the river.”

“I’ve been waiting to say that since I was thirty. I never had a man with the right hand for it.” He set the hilt a finger’s breadth higher in Darrow’s grip. “Fight from where you’re put, I told you. That goes for the hand and all.”

“Fingers that work: eight,” said Wren, from the water, writing. “That’s a better count than Ser Hollis’s legs.”

“Leave my legs out of your book.”

“They’re in it under furniture.”

Darrow laughed, the whole of it, and a bird went out of the far hedge in a hurry. He gave Maelis the sword, and she put it back under the salt.

Rae said the word that moved the oxen, and the cart went down into the ford with the water loud on its wheels. Darrow took the slab with the stick high in the new grip, and Maelis stood at the far end of it, where he could see her, and watched him take every step.

On the far side he turned round.

“Good,” said Maelis.

He looked past her, down the track toward Coldmere, as he had twice that morning. Hedge, and a strip of sky above it, and nothing on the sky yet.

BetweenNot the Carter

“Are you still angry about the river road?”

He had got himself up onto the box to ask it, which took the rail, the new grip and longer than he liked, and he asked it before he had his breath back before he could think better of it. Rae moved along the board to give him the side away from the wheel. Behind them Wren had gone to sleep in the sacks with the ledger under her cheek, and Maelis walked a dozen yards back, where the hedges kept the talk. Hollis sat on the tailboard with his eyes shut, which proved nothing.

“Aye,” said Rae.

She let it sit. Hob found a rut, and the cart rolled through it and out.

He had sat out a reeve’s lantern beside her. He could sit out this.

“Some,” she said, at the next gate. “Not all of me.”

“Which part?”

“The part that knows that road.” She tipped her head east, where there was only hedge. “I’ve carted salt to the farms down it since I could hold the lines. Their dogs know my bell. There’s a boy on that road rides my tailboard from the mill to the bend every harvest and won’t sit still on it, and that shoe in the ditch was his size.” She looked at the oxen. “And I took my cart past the end of it with the bell stopped. A carter with no bell is a thief till she proves otherwise. I went by them like one.”

Darrow had his answer ready, and he gave it, because it was true.

“There was a herald’s man on that road with my name by heart, and the Mender’s, and Hollis’s. Your cart’s the one a grey cloak crossed the green to ask you about. Every farm on that road would have had us in its yard by dark. They’re safer for the cart that didn’t come.”

“That’s true,” said Rae.

He waited for the rest of it.

“It’s still a road I didn’t drive.” She shifted the lines in her hands. “That’s not the part, though.”

“What’s the part?”

The cart went a long way up the track before she said it, and she said it with her eyes on the road.

“I was glad,” she said. “At the fork. When you said north.” The bell at her hip swung on its rag and made no sound. “I’ve carried for everybody on this road since I could hold the lines. Everybody or nobody, that’s the trade. You don’t pick. And you said north, for your own, and I was glad of it, and I turned the oxen glad.” She looked at him then. “That’s what I’ve been chewing on since the fork. Not you.”

He had climbed up to take her anger off her. Anger would have been easy to carry. This was heavier, and it was hers, and he sat with it while the hedge went by.

“One true thing,” said Rae. “That was mine.”

He gave her his.

“I chose north for the herald’s man. Not only for him.” He turned the stick over on his knees. “I watched that grey cloak walk across the green to you and say his piece, and watched you answer him, and I couldn’t hear a word of it from the wall. I’d not have you asked again. So I picked the road with nobody on it to ask.”

“You chose it for me.”

“Some of it.”

“And said not a word of that at the fork.”

“You gave me the road.”

“I gave you the road.” Rae shook the lines, and Hob leaned into the yoke. “I never gave you the carter.”

It went in where she had aimed it. She had told him in her uncle’s yard, in other words, that on her road he would be first at nothing, and he had agreed and meant it. Then at the fork he had decided for her in his head and called it a choice of roads.

“No,” he said. “You didn’t.”

“Next fork, then.” Her eyes were back on the road. “You say why first. Out loud. Then I’ll say aye, or I won’t.”

“Next fork. Why first.”

“Aye.”

From the tailboard, with his eyes still shut, Hollis said, “Is that the end of it? Only there’s been a drop of hedge working down my collar since the ford, and I’ve waited all day for somebody on this cart to be speaking so I could complain to them.”

“Complain to the mule,” said Rae.

“I did,” said Hollis. “The mule agrees with me. Where’s the joy in that?”

Scene 2Two Carts Out of One

Ash saw it first. She stood up in the sacks with the splint braced against the salt and looked back down the track the way they had come, and the hair rose along her spine from her neck to her tail, and she made no sound at all.

Darrow turned on the box.

The mill tracks had come out at dusk onto an open shoulder of hill, where the hedges gave out into stone walls and the sky had gone the colour of a cold kettle. Behind them the land went down in long folds toward Coldmere, and on the last fold, a mile back and high against what was left of the light, stood a rider. One. A light horse, long in the leg, its legs plain against the sky.

“Stop them,” Darrow said.

Rae stopped the oxen. On the skyline the rider stopped too. Darrow counted ten. Rae said the other word and the cart went on, and when he looked back the rider was moving again, at a walk, keeping the mile exactly, as if it had been measured out and paid for.

“That’s your long-legged horse,” said Hollis from the tailboard, low. “The miller’s man’s.”

“Shod to go fast,” said Darrow, “and walking. Nobody rides a horse like that to walk.”

Wren was up on her knees in the sacks, staring past the rider at the empty folds behind it. “Riders: one,” she said. “Column: none. No dust, no smoke-cart, no lances. Is one better or worse?”

“Worse,” said Hollis. “Forty you hear coming, and forty want something simple. One wants something particular, or they’d have sent forty.”

“Riders behind us, one,” said Wren, writing, “which is worse than forty, according to Ser Hollis.”

Maelis looked at the skyline, then at the light going off it, then at the knee. “We’ll want a wall tonight,” she said.

The track came off the shoulder into a dip where a spring crossed it, and the ground there went soft and black under the frost. A lane went off left between walls toward a farm with one lit window. The mill tracks went on ahead, up over a run of bare stone. Down in the dip the shoulder hid them from the rider, and the rider from them.

Rae slowed the oxen. Darrow felt her not look at him.

“That’s a fork,” he said.

“It is.”

“Why first, then.” He put his hand flat on the stick. “The tracks go on to a mill, and a mill’s a place with people in it who’ll lie for a carter. The lane goes up to one farm and a dog. I’d keep the tracks.”

“Aye,” said Rae. Then: “He can have the lane, though. Everybody off.”

Darrow got down and went up onto the stones with the stick, because his legs were the slowest thing in the company and his eyes were not, and stood where he could see the top of the shoulder against the sky.

A mile at a walk was a quarter of an hour. He had known that since he could sit a horse. They had spent some of it coming down the hill, and out of sight the rider could not see them stop, and would keep walking.

He began to count.

Rae drove the cart up the lane into the soft ground, loaded, and the wheels went in to the felloes. At the farm gate the dog came to the bars and gave its opinion, and went on giving it, and every bark went down the valley toward the shoulder. She brought the oxen round in the gateway with a hand on each nose.

“Half gone,” said Darrow.

“Then stop telling me,” said Rae, and drove back down on the turf of the verge, where the ground held and kept nothing.

At the spring she had them pile half the load on the near side, Hollis swearing, Wren counting sacks, Maelis telling both of them to be quiet, until the cart leaned on its near wheel like a drunk on a friend. She took it through the black mud on a fresh line, a hand’s breadth off the first, the near wheel cutting deep and the off wheel barely touching.

The top of the shoulder was only a black line now against a sky going the same colour. Up the lane the dog was still at it.

“Rae.”

“I know.”

The cart came up out of the mud onto the stones. The wheels rang once and left nothing behind them, and the company went up the stony track after it without being told, over the next rise and down the far side.

There Rae stopped and had them put the load back even. Then she said it once, for all of them.

“A man reading that dip sees two carts come down off the hill and go two ways. One’s heavy and went up the lane, plain as a signpost. The other’s lopsided and light and went over the stones, and the stones won’t tell him anything.” She took up the goad. “Which would you follow?”

“The plain one,” said Darrow.

“Everybody does.”

“Carts on the road ahead of us, by their tracks: two,” said Wren, writing. “Carts on the road: one. I’m in it.” She looked up. “That’s the best lie I’ve seen since I came off the mountain, and I’ve been travelling with Ser Hollis.”

“It’s a road,” said Rae. “Roads say what you tell them.”

“I’ve never once lied to you,” said Hollis.

“You told the miller’s man it was a bear.”

“That was improving it.”

They made camp in the lee of a field wall, with the hill between them and the dip. Rae said a small fire would not show from below, and Maelis looked at the hill and agreed.

Hollis came along the wall on the beech leg with his hat in his hand and looked at the height of it, the stones on its top, the frost, as he had looked at the well at Coldmere. Then he looked at the Captain. Then he reached over and set the hat down on the far side, crown down in the frost, where nobody on this side could see it.

“That’s a wall,” said Hollis, as if a bet had been settled.

Darrow went to the end of the wall and looked back the way they had come. The dip was dark, and the farm’s window, and the folds beyond it. He had counted to the end of the quarter hour on the stones and well past it, and nothing had come over the shoulder. Nothing stood on the skyline now.

That was either good or the opposite, and there was no knowing which from here.

BetweenOver the Wall

“You’ve been not looking at these since the fire was lit,” said Hollis, and rattled the stones in his lap. “Come and not look at them from here.”

Darrow came. Hollis sat with his back to the wall and the beech leg out along the frost, and handed a stone up to him without looking, as a man hands up a cup.

“You saw me put the hat over,” said Hollis. “Where is it?”

“Two paces out from the third stone with moss on it.”

“Then that’s where you throw. Not at the wall. Not at the hat. You can’t see the hat. You throw at the place the stone has to come down, and you let the wall be the wall.”

Darrow stood at the wall with his feet planted. The far side was already dark. Somewhere back over the shoulder a rider who could not see them either was likely doing the same sum.

The stone sat in three fingers and a thumb, and the other two lay along it and did nothing.

“Let go with the first two,” said Hollis. “The others never did anything but hold on too long.”

He threw. It went up into the last of the light and over, and came down a long way off in the field beyond with a rattle.

“Grass,” said Hollis. “Long. You threw at the hat you wanted, not the one you’ve got. Again.”

The second clacked off the top stones and came back to his own feet.

“Wall. That one was frightened of the wall.”

The third went over and landed with a soft sound just past it.

“Short. Better. You’ve stopped being proud of your arm.”

His hands were stupid with the cold, the right one worst. He blew on it, and took the fourth stone, and stopped looking at the wall. He looked at the place two paces out from the third mossy stone, which he could not see, and let the first two fingers open, and let the others do nothing at all.

There was no clack and no rattle. There was a small sound, like a coin dropped into a purse.

“Hat,” said Hollis.

“You can’t know that.”

“Forty years of hearing where things land, Captain.”

Darrow leaned on the wall and looked over. In the crown of the hat, pale on the dark felt, sat his stone.

“That’s Overwall,” said Hollis. “Over what’s in your way and down on what’s behind it.”

“You were right about the fingers.” Darrow turned his hand over in the firelight. “It went off cleaner without them.”

“I’m right about most things. Nobody pays me for it.” Hollis rattled the stones and held one up. “Again. Once is luck.”

Scene 3A Captain With a Stick

“A rider on a long-legged horse,” the drover was saying, “asking after a captain with a stick.”

Darrow was in the taproom door with the stick in his hand.

Nobody had looked round yet. The inn at the top of the mill tracks had one room for everything, low and brown with smoke, and a dozen hill people sat along its benches with their boots to the fire and their eyes on the drover, because the drover had the floor and knew it. Darrow leaned the stick in the corner behind the door, among the coats and the crooks, and walked to the end of the nearest bench without it. The knee took the room and said nothing about it. It was the slowest he had ever crossed a room, and not one head turned.

“Three nights back, at the landing,” said the drover. He was a big man with a dog asleep under his chair. “Stood the whole room a round and drank water himself. Good coin, too, none of it clipped. Asked after this captain, and a carter with two oxen, and a woman Mender, one after the other, like a man reading off a list.”

“What did he want with them?” somebody asked.

“Didn’t say.” The drover drank. “Asked how far a man with a stick walks in a day. That’s what I can’t get past. Not where he’d gone. How far he walks.”

Under the table Darrow’s right hand had closed on nothing. The last two fingers came last, as they did now.

Rae had come in behind him with her hood still up. She sat down on the drover’s bench as though she had been coming to sit there all evening.

“And what did you tell him?”

“Told him I’d never seen a captain in my life, nor wanted to.” The drover looked at her properly. “You’re Thorne’s. The bell-cart.”

“I’m Thorne’s.”

“They’re saying at the landing your cart came down that mountain with the captain on it.”

“My cart came down that mountain with salt on it,” said Rae, “and it’ll go back up with flour. Ask the reeve at the hamlet what’s on my cart. He asked me at his gate, and I told him.”

The drover laughed, and the room laughed with him, and somebody asked about the price of wool, and the rider went out of the talk the way weather does. Darrow sat with his back to the wall and his hand open on his knee and did not look at the corner behind the door until it was time to go out to the cart.

The family was in the yard.

A farm cart stood by the trough with its bedding roped on top and a grandmother sitting on the bedding, wrapped to the eyes. Three children were being kept still beside it. Their ox stood with one hind foot held off the ground, the hock swollen hot under the lantern, and the man of them stood at its head with his cap in both hands, waiting for somebody to come out.

Darrow had seen bedding roped on carts like that once before, across a river, and he had looked at the spears instead.

Rae looked at the ox. Then she looked at the children’s feet, and Darrow watched her do it. The middle one, a boy who would not keep still even now, wore one boot. His other foot was wrapped in sacking and tied round the ankle with twine.

She said nothing about the foot. She went to the ox and ran her hand down the leg to the hock, and the ox flinched, and she took the hand away.

“Done till spring,” she said.

“I know it.” The man turned his cap round. “We’d not ask. Only it’s the Thorne cart, and the road says the Thorne cart carries for anybody.” He looked past her, at the company coming out of the inn door: the old knight on the beech leg, the girl with the ledger, the small grey woman with the roll on her shoulder, and Darrow last, with the stick. “And they’re saying there’s a captain goes with it now, who stood at a gate up the mountain and wouldn’t kneel.”

Nobody in the yard said anything to that.

“We came off the river farms when the levy came,” the man said. “Up the side lanes, not east with the rest. My wife’s people are in the village over the hill. If we could have the loan of your near ox as far as there, we’d walk him back to you ourselves by tomorrow night.”

Maelis had the boy up on the mounting block and the sacking off his foot. She looked at it in the lantern light and began to wrap it again, properly, without a word to anyone.

“Hob’s mine to lend,” said Rae. She put her hand on Hob’s neck, and then she looked at Darrow. “The day’s yours.”

“At a lame ox’s pace,” said Wren, very quietly, “the village is tomorrow noon. At ours, it’s tonight.”

“With a rider somewhere behind us,” said Hollis, “who wants to know how far you walk in a day.”

The man stood in the lantern light with his cap in his hands and waited, as people on a road wait for a cart to make up its mind.

BetweenThe Rag

“It’s your ox,” Darrow said.

Rae looked round from Hob’s neck.

“I could argue it either way and win both times,” he said, “and that’s no way to decide anything. It’s your ox and your road. Whatever you say, the company stands behind it. All of us.”

The yard heard that. Hollis heard it, and shut his mouth on whatever he had been about to say. The man with the cap looked from the Captain to the carter, as though he had been told the wrong one was in charge.

“You’ll not take it back at the first hill,” said Rae.

“No.”

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked at the boy on the mounting block with the Mender’s linen on his foot, and at the grandmother on the bedding, and she said it out loud, to all of them, so that nobody would have to guess.

“Hob goes over the hill. The cart goes with him, and we go with the cart, at his pace.” She turned to the man. “First light. Not before. The lame one walks behind, unladen, and if he lies down I’ll not be the one to tell your wife.”

The man began to thank her. She told him to thank the ox.

“Rae,” said Hollis. He said it gently, which in Hollis was the warning. “There’s a rider behind us who’s asked the road how far the Captain walks in a day. And tomorrow the answer’s half.”

“I know it.”

“Then you know what you’re spending.”

“A day,” said Rae. “He’s had three days of us already, and all he’s done is look.” She put her hand flat on Hob’s shoulder. “These have got a grandmother on the bedding and a boy in one boot. I drove past their road with my bell stopped. I’ll not drive past them twice.”

Hollis turned to Darrow.

“You heard her,” said Darrow.

Hollis looked at him for a long breath, at the Captain who had once decided everything for sixty men before they had their boots on, and something in his face gave up the argument and was not sorry to.

“Then somebody find that lad a boot,” he said, “before the Mender’s linen goes through on the stones.” He sat down on the edge of the trough with a grunt, worked at the strap of his pack, and brought out a boot. A right boot, big as a bucket, the leather still good. “Carried it down a mountain. Never knew why.” He held it out to the boy. “Stuff it with straw. Your foot’ll grow into it by about the time I grow back into mine.”

The boy took it in both arms, as though it might be taken back.

Rae stayed out with the oxen when the others went in. Darrow found her at the cart in the dark of the yard, with her back to the inn’s light and her hands at her belt.

She was working at the knot that held the rag in the bell.

He thought of the shoulder of the hill behind them, and of a rider a mile back on a still night, and how far a bell carries over frost. He had said whatever she decided. He kept his hands on the stick.

The knot came loose. She pulled the rag out and put it in her pocket, and turned toward him, and the bell at her hip rang once in the cold, plain and dark, for the first time since Coldmere.

“You said whatever I decided,” said Rae.

“I did.”

“Then that’s decided too.” She let the bell hang. “A carter carries for anybody. And she lets the road hear her doing it.”

Scene 4Six Buttons

The bell rang all morning, and every time it rang Darrow looked back.

They had gone over the hill at first light at the lame ox’s pace: Hob in the river family’s traces with the grandmother up on the bedding, Rae’s cart behind on Dun and the mule, who had opinions about it, and the lame ox last of all, unladen, its hock bound. The boy walked at Hob’s head in Hollis’s right boot stuffed with straw, fell over in it twice, and refused to ride. Rae had the rag in her pocket. At every jolt the bell at her hip went out over the frost and away down the valley, and Darrow looked back down the track at the sound of it and saw empty road.

The rider was not behind them.

At noon the track ran beside a water-meadow, flat and white, its grass stiff with frost, a frozen ditch down the middle of it like a seam. On the far side, at the edge of a stand of bare alders, a horse stood with its rider on it.

They were close enough to count the buttons on the coat. Darrow counted them: six, dark, down the front of a coat cut close for riding. The horse was long in the leg and stood without shifting a hoof. The rider sat it the same way, slight and upright, hood back, hands quiet on the reins. A woman, with her hair cut short as a page’s.

She was looking at him. Not at the cart, or the family, or the bell. At him.

Rae stopped the oxen. She needed no telling.

Darrow was already off the track.

He had not decided it. He was walking out onto the meadow with the stick, toward her, over the frozen tussocks, before anyone had said a word. The cold had been in him since morning, in his hands and his chest, and it went out of him now, and he knew that warmth. The last time he had felt it there had been a horse under him and a river in front.

Twenty paces out, the ground told him what he was doing.

The meadow was flat as a table from his feet to the alders, and the ditch ran across it between him and her, frozen over and deep enough to take a man to the knee. A man on foot crossing it with a stick would be in the open the whole way, and would come to the ditch, and have to go down into it and up the other side with his eyes on his feet. She had put her horse where every step he took toward her was a step onto her ground. She sat where she was. She had no need to move.

He stopped.

He planted the stick in the frost beside his right foot and took it high, three fingers and a thumb, and stood square, and let her look. If she wanted him, she could get down off that horse and cross the ditch herself.

She looked a long time. The wind came across the meadow and found his collar, and behind him the mule said something about it. Her hands stayed quiet on the reins.

Then she turned the long-legged horse, unhurried, and walked it into the alders, and the meadow was empty, and he was a man standing in the middle of it with a stick.

He walked back. It was further than it had been going out.

“That’s a duelist,” said Hollis, low, when he reached the track. “Did you see her hands? Never moved. A duelist doesn’t move her hands till it’s worth her while.”

“She could have closed with us any time since the shoulder,” said Maelis. “A hunter who could have closed and didn’t is waiting for something.” She looked at the meadow, then at him, and let the twenty paces go unsaid, which was worse than saying them.

Wren was in the cart with the ledger shut on her knees. Darrow went to the tailboard.

“I should have told you first,” he said.

Her face had gone the colour of the frost. She opened the ledger and found the page with her thumb, and did not write.

“Times the Captain has told me first: two,” she said. “Times he hasn’t: one.”

Rae said the word and the oxen leaned into the yoke, and the bell began again. In the sacks Ash stood with her forefeet on the tailboard and looked at the alders, and went on looking after the alders were gone.

BetweenThe Wrong Question

“It’s salt,” said Rae, before he had sat down. “Mostly.”

He sat down anyway, on the shaft beside her, with the stick across his knees. They had left the river family at a door in the village behind them, the grandmother still talking, and Hob was back in his own traces and had said nothing about any of it. The others were at the fire under the barn wall. Out here there was only the cart, and the frost, and the sack at her feet.

“It goes to the mill at the end of these tracks,” she said. “It always goes there. Somebody at the mill takes it in, and gives me back a sack of flour that weighs a hair more or a hair less than flour, and I carry that where it’s going.”

“Where’s it going?”

“Down the road, to people on the coast who pay for what the road knows. Who’s on it, and which way, and what’s on their carts.” She did not look at him. “There’s a lamp for every road, they say down there. I carry for the one on this road.”

He turned that over and found the sharp edge of it at once, as he always found the edge of a thing first.

“Is my name in that sack?”

“I don’t know,” said Rae. “I’ve never opened it. I’ve never opened one.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.” She put her heel on the sack’s neck, as though it might go somewhere. “And it’s the wrong question.”

“What’s the right one?”

She looked at him then.

“What I was carrying for them up your mountain.”

The fire cracked over by the wall. Hollis said something there and Wren laughed at it.

“Ask it, then,” said Darrow.

“Not tonight.” She held his eye. “I meant to tell you this before the mill, and I have. That other’s a longer road, and I’ll drive it when I can drive it right. Ask me some night when there’s nobody on the hill behind us.”

He could have pressed. He knew how. He had pressed men for less in tents with the rain coming in, and had the answer by the second candle. He sat with his hands on the stick and did not.

“You’d have found it anyway,” said Rae. “Wren weighs sacks for the joy of it.”

“Wren weighs everything.”

“That’s one,” she said. “One true thing. That’s mine.”

So he gave her his.

“On the meadow,” he said. “For twenty paces I thought it might be him. On that horse. I wanted it to be him so badly I’d have crossed the ditch on my knees to see his face.”

Rae was quiet a moment.

“It wasn’t.”

“No. She sits a horse nearly as well. Nobody sits one better than him.”

“Then that’s one each,” said Rae, and pushed the sack under the cart with her heel, out of the frost, where it would keep till the mill.

Scene 5Under the Forms

He had been nailed to the bell-post twice.

The post stood by itself at a fork in the mill tracks, a day short of the mill, with nothing near it but a frozen trough and the wind. The first paper was the Crown’s: another copy of the writ they had read at the hamlet a week ago, under its blob of blue wax, his name alone at the top in letters a size bigger than the rest. The second was nailed under it. It was small and square and very white, and someone had driven a nail into each of its corners, the four of them exactly the same distance in from the edge.

To the captain of the Ninth Lance, who walks with a stick.

Under the forms. Come to the weir below the mill at the end of these tracks, at first light on the second morning after this is nailed, which is to say not tomorrow. Come alone. I will be alone. There is a question. It will take less time to ask than this has taken to write.

There was no name under it.

“Four nails,” said Wren, who had read it over his shoulder. “Spaced to the hair. She measured.” Her voice had gone careful. “Nobody measures nails.”

Darrow looked at the writing, because the writing was what there was to look at. Every letter stood upright and cut clean, a stroke and then a stop, nothing joined to the next; there was no flourish anywhere, and no blot. Whoever wrote it had written it standing, and taken her time, and crossed nothing out.

The script came up faintly behind his palms and read the paper as it read a face. There was no lie in it, and no more in it than it said, and it said exactly what it meant: the weir, the hour, alone, a question. It was the most frightening letter he had ever been sent.

He took it down, nail by nail, and put it inside his coat.

They argued it at the fire that night in the lee of a wall, all of them at once, the loudest the company had been since the road began.

“It’s under the forms,” said Hollis. “You go. That’s all there is to it.” He said it to the fire and then, because that was cowardly, to Darrow. “When a knight sends word under the forms, the one who sends names the ground and the one who comes picks where on it he stands. A question’s asked before any blade’s drawn, and nobody on the bank lifts a hand while it’s being answered. She’s offered you that. A writ wouldn’t. A Confessor wouldn’t. You don’t spit on it.”

“You don’t walk into it either,” said Wren. “We’ve two nights and a day. We could be two valleys off by her first light, and she’d be stood on a weir with her question and nobody to ask it of.” She was counting it off on her fingers, which she never needed to do. “That isn’t running. That’s arithmetic.”

“She found us beside a meadow when she was meant to be behind us,” said Rae. “She’ll find us two valleys off.” She was mending a strap by the fire with the awl, and kept her eyes on it. “I’m at the mill tomorrow night with the sack. I’ll read that weir before anybody stands on it. Ice, stones, where the water’s under. Then you decide.”

“Then we get there first,” said Wren, who was never beaten for long. “We walk tonight, all five of us, and we’re stood on her weir a day before she is. She named the ground. She never said we couldn’t be standing on it.”

“That’s spitting on it with your boots on,” said Hollis.

“And his hands?” said Maelis.

They all looked at her.

“Four days,” she said. “He’s had that grip four days. You don’t take a new grip onto ice against a woman who spaces her nails to the hair.” She looked at Darrow across the fire. “I’m not telling you not to go. I’m telling you what you’d be going with. If it’s a question she wants answered, it can be answered by another mouth.”

“Mine,” said Wren at once. “She’ll not waste a blade on a girl with a ledger. I’ll take her your answer, and ask her two things back: who sent her, and why she let you walk off that meadow.”

Darrow sat with the paper against his ribs.

A year ago he would have stood up halfway through Hollis and said what the Ninth would do, and the Ninth would have been saddled before Wren had her ledger open. Tonight he let all four of them run it to the end, and found he wanted to hear how it came out.

“She wants a question answered,” he said. He turned the stick across his knees. “Every one of you’s right.”

“That’s no use to anybody,” said Hollis.

“No,” said Darrow. “It isn’t.”

Rae bit her thread off short and put the awl away. “Tomorrow night, then,” she said. “I’ll tell you what the water’s doing.” Nobody argued with that, and nobody slept for a long time.

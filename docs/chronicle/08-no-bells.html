BOOK III — THE RUNNING DARK

Chapter 8No Bells

Previously

At the ring in the frozen marsh Darrow read what the stones are for, Wren read the line he could not, and Maelis said yes.

Scene 1The Cold Lamp

The trees came right down to the river and stopped there, as if they had been told to.

They were old trees, older than any in the Lowmarch: black trunks wider than the cart, going up out of the snow without a branch for twice a man’s height and then spreading so close overhead that the light came down between them grey and green and slow. The snow under them lay smooth and deep and had not been walked on. Nothing moved. Ash stood at the edge with one forefoot lifted and her nose working, and the hair along her spine went up and down again, and her tail went round once, which Darrow had never seen her do at a wood before.

Wren was the one who noticed.

“Listen,” she said.

They listened. The river ticked behind them under its snow. Somewhere a branch let go of its load with a soft sound like a sigh. Nothing else.

“There’s no bell,” Wren said. “There’s always a bell. Every village on the Holloway rang something by now, a chapel, a gate, a cow. I’ve had a column for bells since the mountain.” She looked at the trees as if they owed her money. “Nothing.”

Darrow looked back the way they had come.

The Wend lay white and empty between its banks, with their own tracks going across it in a long untidy line: the cart’s two ruts, the oxen’s prints, and one set that went straight and long-strided and then stopped short of the bank and stood about a while. On the far side, where the marsh came down to the river, five horses stood in a row at the edge of the ice. None of them had moved. Behind them, over the reeds, a thin grey thread of smoke went straight up into the still air and did not come any nearer.

His feet were cold through both boots, and for once that was the only thing about his legs he had to think about.

The first thing of the clans’ they saw was a lamp.

It stood at the tree line a few paces from where the cart had come up off the ice: a post of grey wood as tall as a man, with an iron arm at the top and an iron lamp hung from it, shuttered on three sides and open toward the river. It was cold. There was old soot on the glass and snow on the lid, and nobody had lit it for days. Below the arm, tied round the post on a leather thong, hung a little black chip of something, no bigger than a thumbnail, turning slowly in no wind at all.

Wren went straight to it, the way she went to anything nobody had counted yet, and put out her hand.

Rae had her by the wrist before her fingers got there.

She did not pull. She held on, and let Wren look at her, and then let go.

“You don’t touch what’s hung on a lamp,” Rae said. “Not here.”

Wren rubbed her wrist, more for show than anything. “Whose is it?”

“Not ours.”

Darrow came up beside the post on the stick. The chip was black and glassy, and it took the grey forest light and gave none of it back; but one edge of it was dull and pitted, scorched. He had seen a thing like it once before, no bigger than a barleycorn, on a cloth in Maelis’s hand at the Grey Cot, taken out from under a lad’s tongue. He had called that a chip off a field-stone. He did not call this anything. He looked at Maelis, and Maelis was looking at the chip, and her face had the look it got at a wound before she touched it.

Nobody said anything about it. Rae turned her back on the post.

“There’ll be one at every way in,” she said. “My gran said. A lamp at every door, and a door is anywhere they say it is.”

“Then whose door is this one?” said Hollis from the tailboard.

Rae looked at the trees. Her face did something Darrow could not read, and he had been reading her face for a month.

“Greth,” she said.

She said it from the belly, low, the way she spoke to the oxen, and then said nothing more, like someone who has set down a load and found it heavier than she reckoned.

“What’s that?” said Wren.

“The grey country.” Rae’s eyes stayed on the trees. “What my gran called it. East of the river.” Then, plainly: “She’d not have liked me saying it here.”

The wind came through the tops of the trees, a long way up, and didn’t come down.

He had led them over the ice. It had been the best thing his legs had done in a year, and the joy of it was still in him under the cold, wanting to be used: there was a ridge to the north under the trees, an easy slope, the obvious line, and every habit he had ever had said go up it first and see.

He looked at Rae instead.

“Which way?”

She had been waiting to be asked; he saw that, and saw her decide something.

“My gran came out by the water,” she said. “There’s a stream comes down to the river a bit north of here, under the trees. In winter it’s the road. You don’t walk the snow under a wood like this if there’s ice to take a cart.” She took Hob’s head. “She came out by it, the year she came over. We go in by it.”

“You know the way?”

“I know the way she told it.” Rae looked at him straight. “That’s not the same, and I know it isn’t. But it’s the way I’ve got.”

“Then it’s the way,” said Darrow.

She went first, with Hob and Dun, along the edge of the trees to where a stream came down out of the forest frozen and white and wide enough for a cart, its banks steep with snow and the trees leaning in over it from both sides like people at a door. She walked the cart up onto it without a word. Hollis swore at the angle and held on. Maelis walked at the tailboard.

Darrow came last, with Wren, and Ash ranging ahead of everybody, and the cold lamp behind them at the edge of the river with its black chip turning on its thong in no wind at all.

Scene 2Bread on a Stump

The stream ran up into the forest all day, and the forest closed over it.

By noon the banks were higher than the cart and the trees met overhead, and the light came down through them the colour of weak ale. Nothing moved but the company. The snow on the ice took every sound and gave nothing back: not the oxen, not the wheels, not Hollis, who was swearing at the cold under his breath from the tailboard and could not hear himself do it.

Twice they crossed ski-tracks. Two long grooves, side by side, came down the bank out of the trees, crossed the ice ahead of the cart and went up the other bank and away. The second time Darrow walked up to where they started, and they did not start anywhere. They began in the middle of the snow on the slope, as if whoever made them had dropped out of a tree onto his skis, and they ended in the middle of the snow on the other side the same way.

“They’re beside us,” said Rae, without turning round. “Have been since the river.”

“Where?”

“If you could see them, they’d not be any good at it.”

In the middle of the afternoon they came round a bend and found the second lamp.

It stood on the right bank on a post like the first, iron arm and iron lamp shuttered on three sides; but this one was lit. In full daylight, under the trees, a small steady flame burned behind the glass, and somebody had trimmed it that morning, because the glass was clean. Below the post, on a cut stump, lay a round loaf of dark bread on a square of red cloth, with the snow brushed off the cloth all round it.

Everybody stopped to look at the lamp.

Ash went to the stump.

By the time Wren said “Is that—” the loaf was gone, and Ash was standing on the ice in front of them with the red cloth in her mouth and crumbs in her beard, wagging, enormously pleased.

Rae went white.

“That was tal,” she said.

“That was bread,” said Hollis.

“It was a gift laid down before an ask.” Rae was looking at the stump as if something had died on it. “My gran’s word. You put a thing down at a door before you ask anything of the house, or the asking’s an insult, and they’ll not answer it. Somebody laid that down for somebody, at their own lamp, and we’ve come up their road and eaten it.” She turned on Ash, and then did not seem to know what to say to a dog. “Something goes down in its place before dark. Or we’re thieves at a door. And this is their door.”

Ash dropped the red cloth at Darrow’s feet, as if that settled it.

It did not settle it. They argued about it for most of an hour, standing on the ice under the lit lamp in the cold, five people and a dog and a pair of oxen who wanted to know why they had stopped.

“Salt,” said Maelis, and then, when Wren looked hopefully at her roll: “Not my salt. That salt’s for wounds.”

“A knife,” said Hollis. “A good knife. That’s a gift where I come from.”

“Where you come from you’d be giving a stranger a knife at his own door,” said Rae. “Think about it.”

Hollis thought about it. “Not a knife, then.”

Wren offered a pencil. She had two. Rae looked at her, and Wren said “It’s a good pencil,” and put it back.

Darrow had a hand inside his coat on the letters before he knew he had done it, and took it out again. Nothing in there was his to give. He looked instead up the bank at the trees, where the ski-tracks went, and the old itch came up in him: go and find them, follow the groove until it ends at a man, and say it to his face, the dog ate your bread, I’m sorry, what do you want for it. It would be quick. It would be honest. He would be first.

He looked at Rae.

“It’s your gran’s word,” he said. “You say what goes down.”

Rae looked at him a moment. Then she went to the cart without answering and came back with one of the three round loaves the miller’s wife had put on it at the reeds, and the red cloth Ash had dropped, which she shook out and folded square.

“Bread for bread,” she said. “Same size or bigger. That’s what she taught me.”

She laid the cloth on the stump where the snow had been brushed off, and the loaf on the cloth, and put her hand flat on top of it for a moment, palm down, and took it away.

Nobody said anything for a little while. Then Hollis said, “That’s a good loaf, that,” in the voice of a man watching good money go into a well, and the oxen shifted, and they went on.

They made their fire at dusk in the lee of the bank, close enough to the lamp to see it and far enough not to seem to be watching it. Rae banked it with three turns of a stick, the same three as every night, and would not say where she had learned them. Maelis had his right boot off before he sat down, as she had every night since the mill, and looked at the toes, and pressed them, and gave the foot back. “Pink,” she said: the first good word she had given it. The warmth got into Darrow’s bones and stayed there for once, all the way down, and he sat with his boots off and let it.

Wren came and sat by him with the ledger on her knees.

She waited, which she had got better at. She had written her sum in that book the night before the ring and left it where he could see it, and he had not answered it, and she had come out across the ice anyway, at the back, with a lamp.

The script came up while he was looking at the fire.

It came as it always came, pale, on both palms at once, and the same with his eyes shut, over the two slow fingers of the right and the third beside them that had come back to him since the ring; and it was not the same. The lines that had sat on his skin all autumn like frost on a window sat lower now and steadier, like iron that has been through the fire and the water and come out harder. The word under his name had changed. He looked at it a long time.

Then he turned to Wren.

“It’s changed,” he said. “Just now. The word under my name. It’s said Kindled since the House. Now it says Tempered.” He told her how it sat on his hands, as near as he could put it. “You’re the first. Nobody else has it.”

Wren looked at his hands, where there was nothing to see. Then she opened the ledger to the line from the night before the ring, Times he has told me what they said: none, struck out none, and wrote one. Then she turned to his column, under Told first. Four., and wrote Five., and drew a line under it, the way she closed a day.

“Thank you,” she said, to the book.

A long way off in the trees, somebody began to sing.

It was a man’s voice, and then more than one, a slow tune with a turn in the middle of it that went down where you expected it to go up. It came and went with the wind in the tops of the trees. Hollis, across the fire, had gone still, with his cup halfway up.

“You know that,” said Darrow.

“No,” said Hollis. He put the cup down. “Half of it.” He did not say from where, and from his face, nobody was going to ask him tonight.

The singing stopped. The lamp on the bank burned on by itself.

In the morning the loaf on the stump was gone, and the red cloth with it. In the snow beside the stump there was one ski-track, coming and going.

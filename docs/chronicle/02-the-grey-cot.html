Chapter 2The Grey Cot

Previously

In the hour before dusk the grey cloaks climbed to the gate, and Confessor Ivo Marrant read his list to the House, Darrow's name first. Two of his men came through the wicket before Old Mercy rang, late, and Darrow's shoulder could not shut it. The Prior spoke the law of names, and Marrant made his camp on the lower terrace to wait for the Captain of the Ninth to come down and kneel. Darrow went to the bars and gave him his name and his face.

BetweenThe Count

He did not go to the hall. Not yet. The low wall-walk ran off the court without a stair, which was the whole of Maelis's reason for ever allowing it, and Wren was at the far end of it with her elbows on the parapet and her ledger shut, looking down at the terrace.

"Five," she said, before he reached her. "Round the fire. One more at the winch-house. That's six of them, and eleven men in that court and you, and I had a number for you, Captain. Twelve. One in twelve. They'd have had to read every face." She still had not looked at him. "Now it's one in one."

"I know."

"A hundred and twelve paces from the gatehouse stair to the tower door. I ran them tonight so the bell would ring before anyone found you, and then you walked up to the bars and told them where you were." She was talking faster. "So. Count the lamps." She tipped her head at the hall across the court, where a novice was going along the windows with a taper. "Count them while I tell you exactly how stupid that was. Lose the count and you owe me a chit."

He counted. She talked: the paces, the rope, Pell's hands on it, how many grey cloaks it takes to hold a wicket and how many captains it takes to undo a whole morning's lie. He kept the lamps.

"Nine," he said, when the taper went out.

Her lips moved along the windows and stopped where his had. Then she looked at him, for the first time since the bars.

"Next time you mean to be brave," Wren said, "tell me first. So I don't run."

"I'll tell you first."

"You won't." She opened the ledger, wrote one line, and turned it to the last of the light so he could read it: Times the Captain has promised to tell me first: 1.

Then she shut the book on it.

BetweenThe Kept

The chapel door stood open on his way across the court, with one candle burning inside, and the Prior was there alone in the front pew with her hands in her sleeves. Her lips were moving. He stopped in the doorway, and they stopped.

"Come in, Captain," said Mother Ione. "You'll let the cold in either way."

He came in and stood at the end of her pew, because it was low and the knee had had enough of low things for one day.

"You should be angry with me."

"Should I?"

"I buried your story at your own gate. You let it go down the Steps for me this morning, and held the bell all day so the gate would look as if it kept nobody, and then I went to the bars and told him where I was."

"Whose story was it, Captain?" She did not wait for him. "You gave it to the lay brothers. I only let them carry it. A lie told for a man is his to bury, when he's done with it, isn't it?"

"That isn't an answer."

"No. It's a question. They're cheaper at my age."

He tried another way in. "What were you saying, when I came in?"

She looked at the candle a while. "Names. Everyone under this roof, every night, and I notice which ones I've had to stop saying." Her pale eyes came round and counted him, the way they counted every room. "We take a vow in this House, four words. To mend what climbs. Nobody who wrote it thought to add and hand it down to whoever asks. I have never once given a name to a writ. Did you think I would start with yours?"

"I thought you might have wanted the choice."

"I had it. I held the bell so that you would have one too." She got to her feet, which took a while, and which she would not be helped with. "You're in the count, Captain. You were in it before you gave him your name. Shall I tell the Mender you came here instead of the hall, or will you go now and save me the walk?"

Scene 1Good Night, Captain

He saved the Prior the walk. The hall had his name before he reached his cot: two novices at the door stopped talking when he came through it, a lay brother banking the fire stood up from it to watch him pass, and somewhere down the double row a man he could not see said "Edgemoor" to the man beside him, low, the way you pass a taper along a line.

Hollis came down last, when there was nothing left at the gate to watch but the fire, in his chair, with two lay brothers at the poles and Hollis telling them where the stair turned as if they had not carried him up it. They set him by his cot. He did not get out of the chair at once. He sat in it with his hands on its arms and gave the hall the names, because somebody had to and he had heard them read: the Ninth's, the Sixth's, slow, the way Marrant had, and at each one a man in a cot let his breath go or did not.

"Is that all of them, Ser?" Benedek had himself up on his good elbow, the fever sitting high on his cheeks. "The Sixth?"

"All of them."

"Then I wasn't." He looked from Hollis to the Captain, and his brows went up in the middle, ready to apologise for it. "I lay here waiting for it the whole time you were down there. They didn't read me, Captain."

"No," Darrow said. "They didn't."

"Then it's mercy." The lad lay back as if a string had been cut. "Like the heralds said. It's mercy after all."

Darrow sat on the end of his own cot. The Crown has no quarrel with men who can't answer. He had heard the Prior give Marrant the law to his face: no name under the bell taken down against its will. A man who could not answer had no will to be taken against. He could not give the lad that without telling him why it had followed him up from the gate, and the lad was smiling at the roof.

"Get some sleep, Orrin," Hollis said, and looked at Darrow over the boy's head, and said nothing else.

Maelis came with a lamp.

She did not ask where he had been. She set the lamp on the floor by his cot, so that the light came up the leg from below, put the pipe in her teeth, laid two fingers either side of the kneecap and shut her eyes, and the hall went on around them: a cough, the fire settling, Hollis being got out of his chair and swearing at the beech leg for being where he'd left it.

The lamp threw his hands on the blanket in gold and shadow. Then there was more gold than the lamp could account for. The script came up over his palms the way it had at the window, unasked, fine and angular, and under his name the word had changed. It sat on his skin warmer than the old one had, the way a coal sits in a banked fire, and it was still there when he read it a second time.

He said nothing. Maelis did not look up from the knee.

"Held," she said.

He let the breath go.

She opened her eyes on the knee, not on him. "Another man's weight on that door a moment longer, and I'd have had nothing left to tie it to." She took her fingers away. "It's still soft. It'll be soft at the thaw."

She took up the lamp and went on down the hall with it, to the next leg and the next, and the gold went off his hands with her light, though it had never been the lamp's.

He got up, good leg, bad leg, transfer, and went to the window at the end of the hall. Snow had come on again, fine and dry, and through it the lower terrace was a darker shelf below the gate with one fire on it. He took its measure the way he would have taken a camp across a river. One fire, banked low for a long night. Four men sitting round it, and from the hand of one of them a thread of smoke going straight up, as if the cold had no wind in it at all. A fifth on his feet at the terrace's edge, walking it end to end and turning at the same stone every time. Every so many turns the walker came in to the fire and one of the four got up and took the edge from him, and nobody lay down. They were not sleeping in watches. They were sitting up in turns, all night, where the whole House could see them do it.

Far below, where the Steps went into the dark, the winch-house had one lamp lit, small as a spark someone had forgotten to stamp out.

"Captain."

The gate-ward stood in the hall doorway with snow on his hood, turning his cap round in his hands: the man who had drawn the wicket's bar at dusk. He did not come further in.

"One of them's at the bars. He asked me, very civil, to give the Captain of the Ninth the Confessor's good night." He swallowed. "By your rank, Ser, he said. Not your name. Your rank. And I said I would." He looked at the cap. "I don't know why I said I would."

"Tell him the Captain of the Ninth," said Hollis, from his cot, "says the Confessor can—"

"Did you give it to me?" Darrow said.

The gate-ward blinked. "I— yes, Ser."

"Then tell him you gave it."

The man went. Hollis lay back with his hands on his chest. "That'll keep the bastard up," he said, to the roof, and sounded pleased about it.

At the night bell Benedek's lips began to move, and his eyes came past Hollis to the Captain's mouth and waited. Hollis turned his face to the wall. His hand lay on the blanket, shaking now that nobody was meant to be looking, and after a moment he put it under.

Darrow tried, this time. He had the breath for the first word and the shape of it in his mouth, and his throat shut on it like a door. So he gave the lad the shapes again, the whole oath, word after word with nothing in them, and Benedek watched them go by and was satisfied.

"Good night, Captain," the lad said to the roof, when it was done.

That one Darrow could answer aloud. "Good night, Ser Benedek."

BetweenSame Paper

He had known all day which three cots were his, near the banked fire on the far side of the hall, and all day he had kept his eyes off them. After the night bell he went down to them the slow way, the blanket round his shoulders.

Two were awake; he could tell by how still they lay. The third slept, or did it well enough that the other two let him. There was no getting down to a man on a cot with this knee, so he sat on the end of the nearest, and the man in it drew up his feet to make room: the oldest of the three, who had ridden with the Ninth longer than Darrow had commanded it, his forearm splinted across his chest.

"You didn't look at us," the old one said. "At the gate."

"No. And when he reads that paper again, you don't know me. You're three sore men who never once looked at their captain. I'll tell him myself you're not mine."

"There it is."

"There what is?"

"You. Deciding." He shifted the splint. "You walked up to those bars and gave him your face, and never asked one of us whether we'd sooner you hadn't. Now you've come down the hall in the dark to not-ask us again."

The middle one had his head bound in linen and had been listening with his eyes shut. "Since nobody's asking," he said, "I'd take it. His offer. The Captain goes down, the rest of us go back in the cloak. I'd take it tonight and be ashamed of it till I died." He opened his eyes. "Only he read us. Same paper: your name at the top and ours under it. There's no bargain where we go back in the cloak. There's only you going down, and us watching."

"And I'll not watch it," the old one said. "We came up those Steps on other men's backs, Captain. We'll not go down them on yours."

Darrow had carried an order down the hall, and there was nothing behind it when he let it go.

"Then what do you want?"

"Asking," the old one said. "Next time, before. Then do as you like. You're the captain."

"And you?"

"Sleep." The middle one shut his eyes again. "That's a thing you can give."

"You'll be asked," Darrow said. It was not an order, and it cost more than one.

"Aye, Captain." The old one settled the splint on his chest. "First time for that, too."

He went back up the hall slower than he had come down it. Hollis had not turned from the wall, and the blanket over his hand had not gone still.

"All three?"

"All three."

"And?"

"They wouldn't let me go down for them."

Hollis kept his face to the wall. "Ninth," he said, as if it were the worst word he knew.

BetweenHim Who Can Stand

The word was still in the dark between the cots when Darrow said, "Would you?"

Nothing from the wall.

"One of them would. He said so, to my face, and I'd sooner have that than your back. Would you have let me go down for you?"

"Go to sleep."

Darrow found the stool with one hand and the bones with the other, and tipped them onto the blanket. "Story told true, then. Same stakes as before. Unless your hands are too much of a wonder tonight."

The cot complained. Hollis came over onto his elbow, which cost him something he did not pay out loud, and held out his hand. It shook, and he held it out anyway, and the shake went out of it the moment it had work: ones, twos, threes, fours, the thick fingers as quick as by daylight, and at the stone a hair late, so that the bone went off his knuckles onto the blanket. He looked at it as if it had said something about his mother, and passed the bones across.

Darrow's hands were nobody's wonder. He got through the slow way, and swept, and the thrown bone came down on the back of his hand and rocked there.

He did not breathe until it stopped.

"That's a cheat in any taproom in the Lowmarch," Hollis said, and chanted it to the roof, flat, the way the loser does: "Four for the bell and five for the hand, and the bones go to him who can stand." He heard the last line as he said it, and said it again anyway, because the rhyme was the rhyme.

"Pay."

"At the window. When he said it: come down and kneel, Captain, and the rest goes back in my cloak. I thought, good." The word lay there between the cots. "Long as it takes to say it. Then I thought it again, slower, to be sure, and it was still good. Then you walked."

"You wanted me to go."

"For a moment."

"And now?"

Hollis was a long time answering, and when it came it went to the roof. "Now you've asked, and I've said it, and I'd have to carry you down those Steps myself before I let him have you. Look at me." He knocked the beech leg once with his knuckles. "That's your doing, Captain. All of it."

Darrow sat with that until it had stopped hurting enough to answer. "I had three heartbeats in that river. I know what a man can think in one."

"Then what's the face for?"

"That I had to win it off you with sheep's bones. Next time you think it, say it. To my face."

"Next time I'll think it quieter."

"Hollis Garrow."

"…Aye." A breath. "Out loud. Bells help me."

Darrow put the bones into the open hand, and the fist shut on them and did not shake while it had something to hold.

"Now go to sleep," Hollis said, to the roof, "before you ask me anything else."

Scene 2Feast Bread

He woke to bread.

It came down the hall ahead of the dawn bell, warm and yeasty under the smoke of the banked fire, and for a moment he did not know why an infirmary should smell like a house with a mother in it. Then the roof, the cot, the cold coming up off the flags. On the next cot Hollis lay on his back with his mouth open and his fist still shut on the knucklebones, and the fist had not shaken that Darrow had woken to see.

The feast. The kitchen had been counting loaves for it all week. Through the window at the hall's end the lay brothers were already going down the court toward the gate, two to a basket, the baskets heaped and steaming in the grey light. Below the gate the Confessors had let their fire burn up for the morning, and the grey cloaks stood along the terrace edge to watch the bread come.

At the kitchen door a novice with flour to the elbows was running a book in a whisper that carried the length of the hall: would the grey cloaks take it? Two to one they would. Evens they'd make the lay brothers set it down in the snow first.

"Saint Ysolde's bread," said a lay brother coming in with the kindling, the way a man says grace.

"Saint," said the one behind him. "She was a bone-setter with a bad temper and a bell. Call her saint to her face and you'd have got the bell."

The dawn bell rang.

Darrow turned on his elbow toward the far side of Hollis's cot, as he had at every bell since the lad first started watching his mouth. He had the first word ready. This morning he meant to try for the sound.

Benedek lay on his back with his hands folded on the blanket and his eyes open on the roof. His lips did not move. The bell went on, and his eyes did not come.

"Orrin."

Nothing. The lad was breathing: the blanket rose and settled, slow and even, the breath Darrow had lain listening to from the far end of the hall every night since the Steps. There was frost on Benedek's mouth. It lay white along the line of the lips the way it comes on a window, in a hall with a fire in it.

He was up before he had decided to be, good leg, bad leg, transfer, and his hand came down on Hollis's shoulder on the way past, hard.

"What." Hollis came awake swearing, and stopped.

Somebody down the row said it first, low: Hollowed.

Everyone in the Lowmarch knew the word. Knights came home from the field breathing and walking and doing as they were told, with nobody behind the eyes, and in a season or two their families sent them up a mountain with a chit and a lamp. The far end of this hall was where they ended.

It went from cot to cot faster than his name had the night before. As it went the heads turned, and they did not turn to the lad. They turned to the door, to the court beyond it and the gate beyond that, where grey cloaks had come through the wicket once already. A man by the fire was on his feet. At the kitchen door the novice had stopped taking wagers.

"Here," Darrow said.

He did not shout. He had a voice that had carried across a river in flood, and he used the bottom of it.

"Not the door. Here. Look at his mouth."

They looked. That was the worst of it, afterwards: a hall full of men who had been knights, staring at what frost had done to a lad's lips twelve paces from a fire, and not one of them looking at the door. He kept them there because he did not know what else to hold.

Maelis came up the row at a walk, which was worse than if she had run. She looked at the hands first, folded on the blanket, and laid the back of her own against them. Then she set two fingers under the lad's jaw and turned his face to the window, put her thumb to his chin, and opened his mouth.

Under the tongue lay a chip of black glass no bigger than a barleycorn. It took the light from the window and gave none of it back.

She looked at it a long time. Then she looked down the hall to the far cots, where the lamp that is never let go out burned on its hook over the slow, even breathing, and back at the glass.

"The far cots never had this," she said.

It was the only thing she said. She lifted the chip out onto a square of linen with the tip of a probe, folded the linen twice and put it inside her coat, and went down the hall without looking at anyone.

Hollis had got himself to the edge of his cot without the beech leg. He reached across the gap and took the lad's hand, and when that was not enough he took it in both of his and held it on his knee. His hands did not shake.

"Out loud, Captain," he said, without looking up. "Since I swore it." His thumb moved once over the lad's knuckles. "I told him to get some sleep."

He said nothing else, and he did not let go.

The last thing Darrow had given the lad was the oath's shapes with nothing in them, and a good night he could say aloud. He found his own hands closed on nothing, and opened them.

At the kitchen door there were no more wagers. Behind the novice someone had begun to count the loaves on the shelves, aloud, the way you count men after a ford, and at the end of the shelf they started again from the top, as if the number might come out different.

Darrow sat down on the end of his cot, because the knee would have it so, and looked down the hall at the fire. The oldest of his three was sitting up on his cot beside it with the splint across his chest, waiting on him.

"Were you awake?" Darrow asked him, across the hall.

"Most of it, Captain."

"Did anyone come up the row?"

The old man thought about it honestly, which took a while. "You, early, and back again. The lamp came round in the small hours the way it does, counting, and lost its place at Garrow's snoring and started over from the door." He shook his head. "Nobody else. Nobody passed, Captain."

Darrow looked at the door nobody had come through, and then at the frost on the lad's mouth, which had come anyway.

Scene 1The Right Side of the Bridge

The rider had been gone into the alders a quarter of an hour, and Hollis had started on the speech he had been saving since noon.

“Thirty years,” he said, to the whole bank. “Thirty years I’ve told young knights to stand still and let the other man do the work, and not one of them ever listened. It took a river, a mountain and a bad knee, and he’s finally learned it.” He beamed at Darrow, who sat on the cart’s tail with his sleeve cut away. “I’m proud of you, Captain. I’ll be telling it from here to the landing. You won a duel by standing on a rock.”

“A dry rock,” said Darrow. “It’s harder than it looks.”

“Hold still,” said Maelis.

“I’ve held still all day.”

“Then you’re practised.”

She sewed the way she did everything, quickly and without comment. The cut ran from his wrist almost to the elbow, shallow and neat, and she closed it in a row of small stitches and tied the thread off with her teeth. Then she turned his hand palm up on her knee and pressed the last two fingers flat with her thumb, as she had at the noon ford, and let them go. They came back up late, the same as before.

“The arm will mend,” she said. “Those won’t. Nothing about today changed that.”

“I didn’t think it had.”

“You hoped it had. I watched you look.” She began to roll the bandage onto his forearm. “There. Now you’ve two reasons to hold a sword badly.”

Wren sat at the far end of the tailboard with the ledger open, writing, and she kept her eyes on the page.

“Steps the Captain took on the weir,” she said, “one, and he didn’t mean it. Steps she took: I stopped counting at sixty.” Her pencil moved. “Money taken on the morning: none. Nobody would bet against him. I’m the worst bookmaker in the Lowmarch.”

Ash got up off Wren’s feet.

She went down the bank below the fall on three legs and the splint, slid the last of it on her haunches, and waded into the broken ice at the foot of the weir, where the water came over white and loud. She put her head under. When she came up she had the stick in her teeth, the borrowed stick from the Steps, black with wet. She brought it up the bank the slow way, dropped it at Darrow’s boots, and lay down across them.

“That dog doesn’t fetch,” said Hollis.

“Times the dog has fetched,” said Wren, and forgot for a moment to be cross with anyone. “One.”

Darrow picked it up in his left hand, since the right was Maelis’s for the moment, and leaned on it. It stood where he put it.

That was when the shouting started upstream.

The bridge crossed the river a little way upstream of the mill: one span of old timber, wide enough for one cart, with the mill’s toll-board nailed to a post at the near end. The carts that had stood on the far bank since the middle of the night had all started across at once. At the near end, where the bridge came down onto the bank, the first two had come off side by side and locked wheels against the toll-board post, and the rest were jammed up behind them on the span, the old timber creaking under all of them. Down from the mill, white to the elbows with flour, came the millers, half a dozen of them, and in front a broad man with a voice made for shouting over a millrace.

“One at a time!” he was bawling. “The board says one at a time! You’ll have my bridge in the river!”

Darrow went along the bank toward him on the stick, and the company came with him, because it did now without being asked.

The miller saw him coming and stopped shouting at the carts. He looked at the stick, and the bandage, and the coat with the cut in its cape.

“You’re the captain of the Ninth,” he said. “The one on the bell-post.”

Darrow did not turn his head. He had learned that once, young, at a rail, and he had kept it. He was looking at the far bank.

“That’s the post’s word for it,” he said.

Rae had come up beside him, and she was looking where he was looking.

“Look how they’re stood,” she said, before he could.

The carts were drawn up across the far bank behind the people, like a wall. In front of the carts, by the bridge, stood the old ones and the children, and the children were being held still, a hand on a shoulder or over a mouth. And every man standing by the carts had his back to the bridge. They were all looking the same way: back across the open field behind them, to the dark line of hedge and trees on its far side.

“That’s not a queue for a toll,” said Rae. “That’s people with something behind them.”

He had seen people stood that way once before. He had been in the water at Harrow Ford with a lance in his hand, and on the far bank the carts had been behind and the children in front and the men looking back into the trees, and he had been on the wrong side of them. Somewhere under the noise of this river a woman with red hands said, Look. Remember it.

This time he was on the near bank with a bridge in front of him, and he could bring them over instead of riding at them.

“Why first,” he said, and he felt Rae turn to look at him. He said it to all of them, the miller too. “I’ve seen a line stood like that once. I was on the wrong side of it, and I’ve been sorry every day since. I’d like to be on the right side of one, once. So: we keep this bridge open tonight. One cart at a time, as your board says, and every one of them over before morning.”

“The board says no cart after the dusk bell,” said the miller.

“Then we’ll want a lot of lamps.”

The miller looked at the far bank a long moment, at the children with hands over their mouths, and the men looking back into the field. Then he turned and shouted up at the mill for every lamp in the place.

“Aye,” said Rae.

“Nobody sleeps, then,” said Hollis. He sounded pleased about it.

“Nobody sleeps,” said Maelis, “and he sits whenever he can, and I’ll be counting.”

“I’ll count the carts,” said Wren. She had the ledger open again. “Somebody has to.”

They got the two locked carts apart with a pole and a great deal of language from Hollis, backed the second one up, and let the first come off the bridge. The old woman on its seat looked down at Darrow as she passed, and said nothing, and held her grandson’s hand very tight.

The light went off the field slowly, the way it does in frost. The mill rang for dusk, flat and cracked. Lamps came out along the mill’s gallery one by one and down onto the bridge, and the fourth cart came over, and the fifth.

At the far edge of the field across the river, where the open ground met the trees, something moved.

It was hard to see, and then it was not. Shapes were standing at the edge of the trees in a long uneven row, many of them, grey in the last of the light, upright and very still. While he watched, one of them took a step out into the field, slowly, as if it had forgotten how and was remembering. Then another did.

Nobody on the near bank said what they were.

Beside him, under her breath, Wren had begun to count them. She lost her place at forty, and started again at one.

BetweenName the Load

Rae was standing at the near end of the bridge with a lamp, where the carts came off the span one at a time, and she was looking at every one of them the way other people read a letter.

Darrow came up beside her on the stick. He had been sewn, praised and sat down for most of the afternoon, and he had a thing to ask her and no good way in.

“I was going to walk up the line,” he said. “See what’s come over.”

Rae nodded, her eyes on the next cart.

“You know carts.”

“I do.”

“So you could come. If you wanted. Tell me what I’m looking at.” He heard how it sounded as it came out, and so, plainly, did she.

Rae looked at the next cart coming off the bridge, and at the field across the river going dark, and then at him. “That’s two things you’ve asked me,” she said, “and you’ve only said one of them.” She kept the lamp, since he had the stick in one hand and Maelis’s stitches in the other. “Aye. To the end of the lamps and back. And you’ll name the loads.”

The carts that had crossed were drawn up along the mill road, one behind another, with their families sitting on them or standing by the wheels. Rae walked slowly, at his pace, and did not seem to be doing it on purpose.

“That one,” she said, at the first. It had a tarp roped over it, and it sat low at the tail with the shafts riding up. “What’s on it?”

He looked at how it sat, the way she did. Heavy at the back. Something square and tall under the tarp, and out of the back of it a long smooth beam of pale wood, worn darker where hands had held it for years.

“A loom,” he said. “In pieces.”

“It is.” Rae lifted the lamp. “How’d you know?”

Rae laughed. It came out of her loud, before she could stop it, and she put the back of her wrist to her mouth and failed to put it back. “You’d charge a woman for her loom,” she said, “and let her mother ride over free.”

“I’d have charged them both.”

“You’d have missed her.” She was still laughing. “I’m telling Hollis.”

“You’re not.”

“I’m telling Wren, then. She’ll want it for the book.”

They named the rest of the line that way, Rae fast and right, Darrow slow and right about half the time, and for every one he missed she told him why: a cart with bedding on it sits soft and rocks when you lean on the wheel, iron sits dead and doesn’t, and a family that packed in a hurry ropes everything twice. At the end of the line the lamps stopped. Past it was the road north, and nothing on it.

Rae stopped there and held the lamp a little way out into the dark, as if to see how far it reached.

“That was a walk, Captain,” she said. “In case you were wondering. I’d have said if it wasn’t.”

She turned back toward the bridge and the lamps, and waited for him to come level before she walked on.

Scene 2The Biggest Lamp in the Hills

“Carts over: nine,” said Wren, a little before midnight. “Carts to come: six.”

Behind her the mill stood over the river with a lamp in every window, four floors of it, and its great wheel still turning in the dark because nobody had thought to stop it. Every farm in the hill country brought its grain to that wheel. The miller had told Darrow so twice since dusk, the way a man tells you the name of his child.

Across the river the shapes had come out of the trees on the west side of the field, and they were walking. There were hundreds of them. Wren had stopped saying the number aloud when it passed four hundred.

They were not walking at the bridge. Darrow had watched long enough to be sure of that. They were walking at the people. When the carts on the far bank shuffled up the road, the whole front of the line bent to follow them, slowly, the way a field of barley bends after the wind.

“By day they only walk,” said a man off one of the carts, at Darrow’s elbow. He had a child asleep on his shoulder, and he kept his voice down for her. “You can stand in front of one by day and he’ll stop, like a cow. It’s after dark they come for you. They came for us two nights ago. We had a house then.”

“And when they reach you?”

“They take hold,” the man said.

They all saw it a little after that.

One of them was far out ahead of the rest, already on the road at the far end of the bridge, where the last carts were waiting their turn. A bundle fell off the tail of a cart there, and a woman ran back for it.

It turned toward her. That was the whole of it, and it was the worst thing Darrow saw that night: the walking stopped, and the head came round, and then the body, as if someone had spoken its name.

It took her by the wrist.

She screamed. Her husband had her by the other arm, and two carters had hold of the thing itself, and it did not fight any of them. It did not seem to know they were there. It only held on. It took three men to open its fingers, one finger at a time.

By then the miller’s daughter was across the bridge with a lamp off the rail. She was perhaps fourteen. She held the lamp up in its face, and its head came round to the flame, and it stood there, staring, as still as a post. For ten breaths it stood. Then the flame shrank, though there was no wind, and guttered blue, and went out; and its head began to turn again.

They had the woman over the bridge before it finished turning.

Maelis took her arm into the light. The wrist was as white as tallow, with frost standing on it in the print of four fingers and a thumb.

“Can you feel that?” Maelis pressed it.

“No.”

“Keep it inside your coat, against your own skin. Not near a fire.” She looked up, and found the whole bank looking at her. “It may come back. Ask me at dawn.”

“That was one of them,” said Hollis. Nobody looked at the field.

“They’ll not go in the water,” said the man with the child. “They never have. It’s the bridge or nothing, for them the same as us.”

Rae was standing at the toll-board, her own cart drawn up beside her with Hob and Dun in the traces. She had been watching the field the way she watched a load.

“They’ll be at the far end before the last cart’s off this one.” Her eyes stayed on the field. “I’ll want my cart across the end of the bridge when it is. Hob and Dun standing.”

“And somebody on the bridge in front of it,” said Darrow.

“I didn’t say that.”

“I know. I did.”

Maelis looked at him, and at the field, and at the stitches on his arm. Then she held out the stick, which she had taken off him an hour ago to make him sit down, and he took it.

Three carts. Two. “One,” said Wren, and her voice had gone thin.

The last cart was a two-wheeled thing with a man between the shafts instead of an ox, his wife pushing at the tail and three children on top of the load. It came over the bridge at a run with the front of the line twenty paces behind it. Darrow had walked out a few paces onto the span to meet it. The bridge was barely wider than a cart. He stood against the rail and let it go past him, so close the wheel brushed his coat, and as soon as it was off, Rae drove her own cart up behind him and turned it across the end of the bridge, rail to rail, and Hob and Dun stood still in the traces. Now he was on the span with a wall at his back, and in front of him there was nothing but old timber, lamplight, and the far end.

The first of them came onto the bridge.

He was a man, or had been. He wore mail under a surcoat whose colours had washed out past reading, and there was frost in his beard. His eyes were open, and there was nobody behind them, and they were on Darrow. Behind him came another, and another, and every face on the bridge was turned the same way.

Darrow set his feet. He took the stick in both hands and held it crosswise, like a bar across a door, and the first man walked into it.

The man did not push. His hands came up, slowly, and closed on the stick, one either side of Darrow’s own. The cold came down the wood like water.

Darrow stood. His feet were where he had put them, and the stick stayed level, and the man could not come one step nearer. But the second man reached past the first one’s shoulder and took hold of Darrow’s sleeve. A third hand found the cape of his coat.

“Lamp!” said Darrow.

Wren was already up on the cart behind him with a lamp tied to the end of a hay-fork. She swung it out over his shoulder, into their faces.

Every head on the bridge came round to it. The hands on his sleeve and his coat went slack. The line stood and stared at the flame, and for as long as it burned, nothing on the bridge moved.

“Count it,” said Darrow.

“Eleven breaths,” said Wren. The flame shrank, and went blue, and went out. The heads began to turn back.

The miller’s daughter passed her up another, lit. “Nine,” said Wren, when that one died. More of them had come onto the bridge behind the first, and the cold off them was like an open door in winter. “Seven. It’s getting shorter, Captain. Every one’s shorter than the last, and we’ve four lamps left.”

His hands had stopped feeling like his own. He could see them on the stick, and that was all he knew of them.

On the near bank, the miller had been watching all of it, with his wife beside him.

“The lamps hold them,” he said.

“The lamps go out.”

“A big enough one wouldn’t.” He was not looking at the bridge any more. He was looking at his mill. “My grandfather built that.”

“I know who built it. I know what it grinds, and who’ll go short this winter without it, and that we’ll never see the money to build another.” She watched Wren swing the next lamp out. Five breaths. “And I know what a mill’s worth with nobody left alive to bring it grain.”

She reached up and took the lamp down off the hook by the mill door, and put it in his hand.

“Then they can have the biggest lamp in the hills,” said the miller, and he went in through the door with it, and came out without it.

The fire went up through the mill floor by floor, faster than Darrow would have believed a building could burn, and the flour in the air inside went with a sound like a great breath let out. Light went out across the river, yellow and enormous.

Every face on the bridge turned to it. Every face in the field behind turned to it, hundreds of them, all at the same moment, like one head.

The hands opened. The cold on the stick was only cold. The line stood on the bridge with the fire in its eyes and nobody behind them, and did not move again.

“Back, Hob,” said Rae, quite quietly. The oxen took one step back, and the tail of the cart swung away from the rail, leaving a gap a man could just get through. Darrow went back through it a step at a time, the stick still crosswise in front of him. Then Hob stepped up again, and the gap closed.

His hands would not open off the stick. Maelis opened them for him, one finger at a time, and looked at each one in the firelight before she let it go, and said, “Yours. All of them. Put them inside your coat.”

They sat round the burning mill until morning, because it was the only fire there was, and because nobody was willing to turn his back on the bridge.

“It’ll burn till daylight,” the miller said. “There’s four floors of it.” He sat with his back to his mill and did not turn round to look. His wife sat beside him with her hand on his knee.

“And at daylight they only walk,” said the man with the child.

“So I’m told,” said the miller.

There were forty from the mill, counting the children, and five of the company, and the dog, and every one of them was black to the eyes with smoke. Somebody had carried bread out of the bakehouse before the roof went, and it went round the circle in pieces, hand to hand, and nobody asked whose it was. A miller’s boy started a song about a heron that could not swim and was shouted down, and started it again.

Wren had the ledger open on her lap.

“Carts over,” she said. “All of them. Carts lost: none.” She wrote it and drew a line under it, the way she closed a day. Then, because Rae was sitting beside her: “Loads the Captain named right last night: half. Grandmothers missed: one.”

Hollis laughed so hard he had to be thumped on the back.

An old woman of the mill’s, wrapped in two shawls, had been looking at Darrow across the fire since the bread went round. When it came to her she broke it and passed it on and said, “You’ll be going up the north road, then.”

“I don’t know where we’re going yet.”

“There’s nothing up the north road but the marsh,” she said, “and the old ring of stones in it. Older than Calden, they say. Frozen half the year, and the stones stand up out of the ice.” She drew the shawls closer. “Mill-folk don’t go near it. Never have.”

Nobody asked her why. The fire cracked.

“Where do four hundred of them come from?” said Wren. She had the pencil still. “Nobody loses four hundred knights and doesn’t notice.”

“West,” said the man with the child. “That’s all anybody knows. They come out of the west with the dark.”

The line had trodden a swathe through the field as wide as a road, and in the firelight it ran straight back into the trees. Darrow found he was looking along it. A road that wide came from somewhere, and by day there would be nothing on it to stop a man walking up it to see. He would not find where it began in a day, or in three. But a man could go two nights up it and be back by the third day, and know what lay along it.

“No,” said Maelis, across the fire, to nothing he had said yet.

He looked round the circle. Forty people who had burned their own mill to hold a bridge for strangers, black to the eyes, with nowhere to go back to, and every one of them, he found, looking at him.

Rae had seen it too. “Forty’s a long line,” she said, low, for him. “A line that long leaves tracks, and lamps at night, and somebody has to feed it. And it’ll be my cart.”

“There’s the House up the Holloway,” said Hollis. “If they’ll still open the gate.” He did not sound as if he liked it either.

On the bridge, the line of them stood pressed up against Rae’s cart with their faces to the fire, and waited for it to go out.

BetweenWhy First

By the small hours the mill had burned down to its second floor, and most of the millers had gone to sleep where they sat, in heaps, with the children in the middle. The company sat apart at the end of the fire nearest the bridge, because none of them would turn their backs on it.

Darrow waited until Hollis had finished telling Wren what the Sixth would have done with a bridge like that. It was mostly lies, and she was writing down which parts.

“Why first,” he said.

They all looked at him. Rae turned her head last.

“I’m going west on that trail at first light. Two nights out, back by the third day. Ash comes. Nobody else.” He let it stand a moment, as he had let the message stand on the weir. “Now the why.”

“Go on,” said Maelis, in the voice that meant she was already charging for it.

“Those forty need people who can keep them alive. Somebody who knows a road.” He looked at Rae. “Somebody who can keep a line moving and stop it running when it’s frightened.” Hollis. “Somebody who counts heads every morning and notices when one’s missing.” Wren. “And somebody who can tell frostbite from a burn in the dark.” Maelis. “That’s you four. It isn’t me. You saw me tonight. I’m good on a bridge for as long as the bridge holds still. On a road, at a cart’s pace, with forty people and their children, I’m a man with a stick at the back.”

Hollis opened his mouth.

“Let me finish. Then you can call me what you like.” Darrow turned his hands over in the firelight. The knuckles were still white. “They’ll come again tomorrow night, and the night after, wherever we are. A lamp buys eleven breaths. A mill buys one night, and we haven’t got a mill for every night. Somebody has to go and look at where they come from, by daylight, while they only walk, and see if there’s anything there that stops them. I can do that. I can walk a road, and Ash can smell one. It’s the most use I can be to forty people, and it’s the one thing I can do for them that you four can’t.”

There was a third reason, and he kept it behind his teeth. The rider had gone into the alders on that side of the river with his message in her mouth, and at the end of whatever road she rode was the man who had sent her. He would keep his eyes open on the trail. That was all. He told himself that was all.

“Do you know what a captain who walks off is?” said Hollis.

“Tell me.”

“A man walking.” Hollis said it flat, with none of the usual music in it.

“Then the forty will need a captain,” said Darrow. “You’ve had sixty in the dark before.”

Hollis looked at him for a long moment. “You’d put me over millers.”

“Over people who just burned their own mill for strangers. I’d put them up against the Sixth.”

“The Sixth would’ve drowned the mill and burned the river.” But something had gone out of his shoulders. “I’ll teach them to march. They’ll hate me for it.”

“They’ll be alive to.”

“Aye, well.” Hollis looked at the fire. Then he made himself look back. “Two nights, Captain. I’m not saying it twice.”

Maelis had been watching his hands the whole time.

“And if you find where they come from,” she said. “What then? You’ll stop four hundred with a stick?”

“I’ll come back and tell you what I saw, and you’ll tell me what it means. You’re the one who knows what frost does.”

She took that without a word for longer than he liked. Then she named her price. “Two nights, not three. Your hands inside your coat whenever they’re off that stick. A fire you can feed till dawn, both nights, or you find a tree and sit in it. And when you come back, you sit down and I look at you before you say a word to anyone.”

“Done.”

She got up and went to the cart without another word, and came back with her roll, and began, without asking him, to put things into his pack.

Rae had not said anything. He waited, and she let him.

“You haven’t said,” he said at last.

“You said why first.”

“I did.”

“Out loud. Before.” She looked at the bridge, and past it, at the dark where the trail ran into the trees. “Then there’s nothing for me to say aye or no to. You didn’t ask me anything.” She got up to see to the oxen. On her way past him she said, as if it were nothing, “North side of the trail for your fires. The wind’s been from the south since the weir. It’ll carry your smoke off the road.”

That left Wren. She had the ledger open and the pencil moving, and for once she did not seem to be counting anything.

“I’d come,” she said. “I’d be useful. I could count them on the trail and tell you which way more of them go.”

“Who counts the forty, then? Every morning. The same number breathing.”

She looked up. “That’s a dirty trick, Captain. That’s my own column you’re using on me.”

“It is.”

She held his eyes a moment longer, then bent to the book. He watched her write Told first. Four. under the old line, and draw a line under that. Then she turned to a clean page, wrote his name at the top of it and nothing underneath, and turned the book round so he could see.

“Three days,” said Wren. “Then I close it myself.”

Scene 3The North Side of the Trail

At first light the line on the bridge were walkers again.

Darrow watched it happen from the fire. Nobody gave an order. The sun came over the hills behind the mill, and the first man on the span turned from Rae’s cart as if he had remembered an errand, and walked away down the far bank, downstream, at the same walk as the night before. The next one went after him, and the next, and in a while the whole line was walking away along the river in the cold light, and the bridge was empty, and the field behind it was empty, except for the road they had made through it.

Maelis had the woman’s wrist out of her coat. “Warmer than it was,” she said, and that was all she would say.

The rest had been said at the fire in the night, and nobody said it again. Hollis got the forty on their feet by shouting at them, and they hated it, and they went. Wren stood at the head of the column with the ledger open and counted them past her, one by one, children included, and wrote the number down. Maelis put the pack on his back herself and pulled the straps tight enough to make a point. Rae was at Hob’s head with her eyes on the road north. She nodded to him once, the way she would to any carter going the other way.

He crossed the bridge with Ash and did not look back until he was in the field, and when he did, the column was already small.

The road the walkers had made ran straight west across the field, through the hedge, and into the trees, and it did not bend for anything.

It went through a sheep-fold and left the hurdles flat. It went through a hayrick, and there was hay scattered for a hundred yards beyond it. It went up to a cottage and through the gate, and through the garden, and the cottage door hung by one hinge, and the back door was gone altogether. Nobody was in the cottage. He did not go in to make sure.

Nothing lived on that road. There were no birds in the trees along it, and no crows in the fields, and once, when Ash put her nose into a rabbit hole at the edge of the trodden ground, she took it out again at once and came back to his heel and stayed there. In the deep prints where the ground was soft, there was frost at noon, in full sun, that had not been there at dawn.

He walked all day. The knee carried him and complained about it, and by the middle of the afternoon he had stopped listening to either of them. He had a stick and a pack and a dog, and the road went on.

An hour before dusk he stopped and started gathering wood.

He made his camp on the north side of the trail, as Rae had said, with a bank of earth at his back and the trodden road twenty paces off. He gathered wood until the light went: deadfall, broken branches, a fence rail the walkers had knocked flat. One arm was still Maelis’s, and the pile took him longer than it should have. He looked at it when it was done and told himself it would see him to dawn.

The first one came a little after full dark.

He fed it. He fed it and watched the pile go down.

Scene 4Worn Smooth

He woke with the sun on his face and his foot full of pins, and the fire still going.

Rae was where he had left her, on the far side of it, with her coat round her ears and her boots standing toe to the embers, dry. Ash lay across her feet. Most of the oak was gone. Out at the edge of the light, where the five had stood, there was trodden frost and nobody.

“They went at first light,” Rae said. “East, down the road after the rest.” She reached for her boots. “You snore.”

“I don’t.”

“Only when you’re warm.”

He put the foot down and stood on it. It held, and complained all the way up the shin, and he walked it twice round the fire until it gave up complaining.

They went on west. The trodden road ran on as it had the day before, straight through whatever stood in its way: a beck it had waded and come out of muddy, a sheepwalk, a long wall it had pushed over for twenty yards. Rae walked on the north side of it. He walked in it, because the going was flat, and Ash walked wherever he did.

A little before noon the road came up a rise to a crossroads, and at the crossroads stood a field-stone.

It was like every other he had seen: black Oathstone in an iron frame, set by the road for travellers to kneel at, the grass in front of it worn bare by years of knees. The sun was full on it. The iron of the frame was dry. And the black face of the stone was white with frost, thick as a winter morning, at noon.

The trodden road ran up to the stone from the east, one road, the one they had come by. Beyond it the road came apart. West of the crossroads there were a dozen narrower roads going off into their own country, one up the hill, one along the beck, one over the fields toward a farm with no smoke, and between them a hundred thinner tracks, crossing and recrossing, braided like a river’s mouth seen from a height. They had come here from everywhere. They had come to the stone, and from the stone they had gone on together.

Ash would not go within ten yards of it. Darrow did not ask her to. He stood at the edge of the bare ground and looked at the frost on the black, and the two slow fingers of his right hand ached, the two the frost had kept.

“Well?” said Rae.

“I don’t know.” There was nothing to do but say it. “I came two days to find out how to stop them, and what I’ve found out is that they all came past a stone. I don’t know what that’s worth. I don’t know if it’s worth anything.”

“It’s worth knowing.”

“It’s not worth a day.”

“No,” Rae agreed. She looked at the dozen roads, and then up at the sun, which had a long way still to go. “Half a day in hand, if we turn now.”

He looked at the roads too. Any one of them would tell him something by dusk. A year ago he would have been on one already, and been sorry by dark.

“We turn,” he said.

They made the second fire half a day back along the road, in a hollow on the north side of it with an oak over them, and this time he gathered with both hands and she let him. Nothing came to the edge of the light. Rae set the lamp on a root above them all the same, looking down the road.

The rope she had carried the wood in had frayed through at one end. She sat with it across her knees, unlaid the strands with her fingers and began to splice it back, and in the firelight he saw her hands.

He had seen them a hundred times on a goad, a rein, a yoke-pin, always closed on something. Open, they were another thing. Across both palms ran ridges, hard and pale and shiny, like the groove a rope wears into a post when it has run round it for years; and the left thumb was crooked at the joint, set wrong and left that way.

He was learning to ask. “What did that?”

She finished the tuck she was making. Then she put the rope down and turned both hands up to the fire, and held them there, so he could look.

“The cage,” she said. “At the foot of the Steps. My father built it for the House, rope and drum and all, and ran it. Six winters back, a splice let go with him in it.”

The fire cracked.

“I had hold of the rope when it went. Not for long.” She closed the left hand halfway, as far as the thumb would let it. “That’s the turn it took, going through.”

He waited. He had learned that from her too.

“A rope talks, under a load,” Rae said. “It creaks in the drum. It ticks at the splices. That one had talked all summer, and he’d say, she’s grumbling, she’s fine. Then a week before, it went quiet.” She was looking at her hands, not at him. “I heard it go quiet. I thought it had settled.”

She let that stand a moment.

“Folk think a rope goes where it’s frayed. It doesn’t. Fray, you can see, so you mend it. A rope goes where it’s worn smooth. It’s softest there, and it looks the best of the whole length.”

He knew that. He had heard it, nearly word for word, from a woman with two fingers on his knee in a cold room at the top of the Steps: softest just when it starts to feel good. The quiet is a liar. As far as he knew, Rae and Maelis had never said ten words to each other that were not about a load. They had not needed to.

She laid her hands in her lap, palms up still, and left them open.

“I re-laid the rope after,” she said. “Built the cage again. Ran it since.” She picked up the splice. “That’s the hands.”

He looked at his own. The script came up on the right palm, unasked, as it did when it liked, pale in the firelight. The fine line he had first seen at the House ran beside the others, and it was not as fine as it had been. It lay along them now the way a second rope lies beside the first, when it has begun to take some of the weight.

He closed the hand. When he looked up she was watching him, with the splice half made across her knees.

“Can I ask you a thing,” said Rae, “after the weir?”

“You’ve earned a few.”

“One.” She tucked a strand. “There’s a stone at every crossroads from here to Calden. I was under that cart at Coldmere when Oswin lifted it off the boy, one heave, and he’d not been cold since the summer. You’ve that hand, and that arm, and that foot, and the leg you had before the ford. Go down on one knee at a stone and you’d have it all back by morning.” She looked up. “If anyone offered it to carters, I’d be down before they’d finished asking. So why not you?”

Nobody on his bank had asked him that. The ones who wanted him on his knees had sent writs, and the ones who loved him had never asked, for fear of the answer. She asked it as she would ask what a load weighed, and she waited for the answer as she waited for everything.

InterludeSecond in Reserve

On the first night north, with the Captain a day gone west and the carter gone after him, Ser Hollis Garrow had nobody to be loud at.

He had been loud all day. He had shouted forty millers up a road they did not want, and the miller’s wife had said she would marry him, and he had told her he was spoken for by a mule, and then he had been loud at the mule. At noon the carter had put her oxen’s halters into Wren’s hands, said their names to the girl twice, and walked off west with a lamp and a load of wood on her back, and he had let her go, because somebody ought to. Now the camp was down along a stream, the children asleep in the carts, Wren asleep on her ledger, Maelis asleep sitting up as she always slept, as if somebody might call her. The fire was his.

He unbuckled the leg.

The paper was in the cup, where it had been since the House: folded small, soft at the folds, the wax turned inward against the wood. He had been going to look at it since midsummer. He had said so to himself at the House, and at the bars, and across the brazier, and at the well in Coldmere with the Captain throwing pebbles into a bucket. He had a drink’s worth of reasons for not looking, and no drink. The millers had a jar in the third cart. He knew which cart. He had smelled it at noon.

He unfolded the paper.

He knew the words. The Sixth and the Ninth to clear the ford. Second in reserve. He read them anyway, once, to the bottom of the sheet, because he had promised himself once.

Then the wax.

He had broken it with his thumb on the west bank, with the river loud behind him, and it had held on to the paper in two halves, flaking at the edges. It was blue, the size of a child’s palm. He fitted the halves together on his knee with a thumb that would not keep still, and turned it to the fire.

He had seen that seal at the bars, on a Confessor’s writ held up for the court to look at. He had seen it on the letters in the Captain’s lap, and running blue into the brazier and curling black. He had looked away from it every time, because every time some part of him had known, and the rest was for clerks.

Aldric Vane. The captain of the Second had sealed everything, all the years Hollis had known him: orders, passes, a note to a farrier. He had a good hand and good wax, and he liked them seen.

Second in reserve.

The Second had sat its horses on the west bank and stayed dry. The Sixth had gone in under Hollis, because the paper in Hollis’s fist said so, and he had carried that paper up the bank himself, broken the seal without looking, and held it out to the Captain like a man handing over a cup. A Confessor had read the Sixth’s names off a list at the House gate since. Hollis knew them without the list.

He sat very still for a while. He knew what he wanted, and it was in the third cart.

At the well in Coldmere he had told the Captain, sober, that if he took them into the river again he would come in after him. He had said it then because he did not know whether he would be sober on the day it was worth saying. Well. He was sober. And it had not been the Captain who sent them into the river. It had been the man on the bank with the better seat, who sealed everything, and liked his wax seen.

He would still come. That was the thing he had not expected to find in himself, and it was there, as plain as the seal.

He folded the paper along its old folds, wax inward, and put it back in the cup, and buckled the leg on. He did not go to the third cart. He sat until the fire was a red bed and the cold came down through the trees.

In the morning he did not shout them up the road. He said the children’s feet wanted a day, and nobody argued with him, and that was the worst of it.

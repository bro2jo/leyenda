Chapter 4Old Mercy

Scene 1The Almoner

The thin novice who fed the far cots was told at the night bell, standing at the foot of Darrow's cot with his hands in his sleeves, and he thanked the Captain for it, which nobody had expected, and went back up the hall to look at the floor under forty beds.

Mother Ione was told at the dawn bell.

Darrow went to her. He did it on his own leg, up the row with the ends of his right-hand fingers wrapped in Maelis's linen, so that the hand would not quite close, and the cold had got into him in the night and stayed, so that he walked the hall the way a man walks a frozen field, carefully, each step a thing decided. He found her in the chapel with the lamps not yet lit, as he had found her once before, and told her all of it there with his back to the door: the painted hall, the black thing in the rock under the row, the lid of new mortar, the satchel. He told her it had been Hollis's asking that the keepers came first, because it was.

She heard him out with her hands folded. When he had done she looked at him the way she looked into a full room.

"You said you would tell me when you knew what it was," she said. "I counted the nights between. Two." She unfolded her hands. "I say every name under this roof in this chapel every night, Captain, and I have not had to stop saying one since you came. I shall go on saying all of them. That is all I have to say about where I come in your order of telling."

She asked him nothing. She lit the first lamp, and he went back down the row.

Hollis came at the end of the dawn bell, carried.

Two lay brothers had him between them in a chair with poles lashed to it, the way the House moved its heaviest, and he sat in it with his one foot braced and the lad's blanket folded on his knee, and in front of the chair, walking ahead of it the way a man walks in front of a spear, came Brother Anselm.

Darrow knew the almoner as he knew the hour bells: a soft, tired man in his forties with ink on two fingers, who took the House's letters down to the carter each week with the satchel across his chest, and apologised before he asked for anything, and had a sister at Calden he mentioned too often. The satchel was across his chest now. His hands were shaking so that the flap of it trembled.

"Set me down here," said Hollis, at the foot of the cot. "Brother. Tell the Captain what you told me at the third bell. All of it. I'll know if you leave out the part you left out the first time."

Anselm sat on the end of the next cot because his legs put him there.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Captain. I'm so sorry. I should have— the stores key." He touched it, on its cord at his belt, as if to show it was real. "It opens the stores. It opens the old stair door as well, where the wine is laid down, below the kitchen end. Nobody's used it for the wine in my time; the wine's in the stores now, it's drier. But the stair goes down. It comes out in the hall below, at the kitchen end, behind a pillar. I never went near the other door. The one with the flour."

So there were two ways down. He had been counting steps on one of them for a week.

"Before the snow," Anselm said. "I take the letters down to the carter. Every week, the cage down, the bag on my chest, and I wait at the winch-house for her to come, and sometimes it's a day. There was a man there. A clerk. In grey." His eyes went to the window, to the court, to the lower terrace below it. "Not one of— not one of those. Older. He wrote. He had a desk in the winch-house, they'd put a board on two kegs for him, and he had my sister's letter."

"Her letter."

"In his hand. He'd written it for her, he said, as she'd dictated it, because she was—" He stopped, and started again from somewhere else. "She's at Calden. Mirren. She's the brave one of us. She went to the Spire to ask what had become of the brothers' letters from Coldmere, because they'd stopped, and she asked the wrong man, in front of the wrong people, and now she's—kept. In Calden. Comfortably, he said. He said it twice. He showed me her hand on the letter under his. He'd written it, but she'd signed it, and she'd signed it with the mark she puts, that nobody else knows, from when we were—" He trailed off, and did not come back.

"And the thing," said Darrow.

"He gave me two." Anselm's hands went to the satchel's flap and could not work the buckle. "Wrapped. He said they were a mercy. For the wounded. He said the fever comes off a man through the floor if there's a stone to take it, that's how the Spire does it, that's why the Spire's wards are quiet, and the Menders were too proud to use it and he'd been sent to see the House had the use of it anyway. Low in the wall, under the beds, as low as I could get. One under the hall, and when it had taken, the other under the far cots." He looked up. "I thought it was a cruel kind of mercy. I set the first one. I carried it down the wine stair with a trowel and a lamp and I set it and I came up and I was sick on the stair. I couldn't— I haven't set the other one. It's still—"

"Give it here," said Maelis.

She was behind Hollis's chair. Darrow had not heard her come. She put out her hand, flat, and Anselm found the buckle at last and brought out a thing the size of a fist wrapped in the letters' oilcloth, and put it in her palm as if it might go off, and she closed her fingers on it and looked at nothing, and put it in her coat, and said no more than that.

"I never saw him kneel," Anselm said, to the floor. "The clerk. They all kneel, the grey ones, at the dawn bell, you can see them from the wall. He wrote. He never knelt. I thought that was—"

Hollis moved.

He did not stand; he could not. He leaned forward in the chair with his hands on the poles and his whole weight behind his face, and the chair creaked, and the two lay brothers stepped back from it without deciding to, and Anselm shut his eyes the way the lad shut his when he was told.

"You set it under my boy," said Hollis, very quietly. "You set it under forty of them and you went up to your supper. Mercy. I'll show you what a Sixth sergeant does with—"

"Ser Hollis Garrow."

It came out of Darrow in the voice from the ford, the one that had put the Ninth into a line it did not want to hold. He had not used it since the water. He had not known he still had it. It went up the row like a thrown stone and the hall went quiet to the far cots, and Hollis stopped with his hands on the poles.

"Sit down," Darrow said. "Sergeant."

A moment. Then Hollis let out a breath that was half a laugh, with nothing in it, and settled back into the chair, and his hands let go of the poles.

"Captain," he said.

Darrow found he was standing. He did not remember getting up. The knee held.

"Brother Anselm." The almoner opened his eyes. "You'll stay in the hall where I can see you. You'll eat. You'll keep the stores key on you and you'll give it to nobody, not the Prior, not me. Nobody goes down that stair again but with my word and the Mender's count." He looked at the satchel. "When's the next letter?"

"They come up with the carter," Anselm whispered. "Every seventh day. There's— there's always another letter."

Behind them, at the far end of the row, the thin novice was on his knees on the flags with his ear to the floor, listening, and when he saw the Captain looking he got up, and dusted his knees, and went on with his bread.

Scene 2Her Hand

The cage went down empty the next morning, for the first time in anyone's count. The almoner's satchel hung on its hook by the stores door with nothing in it, because the man who carried it was sitting on the cot next to Darrow's with the stores key in his fist and had been told not to move, and the winch-brothers, who had been told nothing, sent the cage down bare and stood at the drum looking at one another.

It came up at dusk with nothing in it either. Rae Thorne came up the Steps instead.

Wren saw her first, through Pell's glass from the wall: a hooded shape on the Last Hundred, climbing the way she climbed, not counting. "She's got something inside her cloak," Wren reported, coming down the row at a run with her scarf flying. "She's holding her arm across it. She did that with your brother's letter."

Darrow was on his feet before she had finished. The cold had let go of him a little in the night; there was warmth in his hands again, and a dull red heat in the ends of the right one's fingers where the feeling was coming back under the linen, which Maelis said was good and felt like the opposite. He went down the hall to the gate with Wren, and Hollis had himself carried after, and Brother Anselm came because Darrow had told him to stay where he could be seen, and so the four of them were at the wicket when Rae came through it with the snow on her hood.

She did not say what she had. She looked at the almoner, and at the key in his fist, and at Darrow's wrapped fingers, and then she reached inside her cloak and brought out a letter and put it, not into the almoner's hands, but into Darrow's hand.

"For him," she said. "I'll not leave a thing like that in the cage overnight with that man's stool in the door."

It was addressed to Brother Anselm, almoner, at the House of Menders at Saint Ysolde's, in a neat upright clerk's hand with a long tail to its y, and sealed with plain wax.

He knew the hand.

He had seen it three times on the outside of folded paper, over blue wax, addressed to the Captain of the Ninth at the House of Menders: the same upright letters, the same long tail. Not Aldric's hand. Aldric wrote like a man falling downstairs. Whoever had written the direction on his brother's letters had written the direction on this one, and the man who had carried both up the Steps to the winch-house sat at a board on two kegs and never knelt.

"Rae." She had already turned for the kitchen. "This hand. Where have you seen it?"

She stopped. She did not turn round.

"I've seen that hand," she said, to the door.

"Where?"

"I've seen it." Her shoulders did something under the cloak, a settling, the way she set them to a cart in a rut. "That's what I've got for you on it. The kitchen's counted me in, I expect."

"Rae—"

"Aye," she said, and went in.

Wren looked at him. He shook his head, and gave the almoner his letter.

Anselm opened it standing, by the gate lamp, with the four of them watching and the snow coming in at the wicket, because he could not wait to sit and could not have sat if he tried. Something fell out of the fold as he opened it, and Wren caught it before it reached the flags.

A lock of hair. Dark, and tied with thread.

He read it. His lips moved along it the way Wren's moved along a row of lamps. Then he read it again, and folded it, and held out his hand to Wren for the hair, and put both inside his shirt, and did not weep. Darrow had been braced for weeping. This was worse. The man's face had simply shut, like a door with no handle.

"She says she is well kept," Anselm said. "She says they have told her I have a thing to finish, and that when it is finished I may come down to Calden and fetch her home. She says to do it." His voice had gone flat and careful, the way a man's goes when he is carrying something full to the brim. "It's her mark at the bottom. Nobody else has that. But she would never say kept. She'd never in her life say she was well kept. That's his word. He wrote it and she signed under it."

"Who did?" said Hollis.

"The clerk." Anselm looked at Darrow. "The one who never kneels. He wrote this, Captain. He writes all of them. He wrote her words for her and put her hair in with them so that I'd—" He stopped. "So that I'd finish."

Darrow stood in the gate lamp's light with his hand open and the snow on it, and read the shape of the thing the way he read a ford.

Marrant had not touched a weapon in front of the House in a month. He had read a list, and camped, and smiled, and kept the cage, and sent lamp oil up three jars short; and under all of it, through a clerk's hand and a cage rope, he had reached into the House and found the one man in it who loved somebody more than he feared anything, and had put a hand round that man's sister in Calden and squeezed, gently, with a lock of hair. He did not need a blade. He had never needed one. The lever was the whole of his method, and the House had been standing on it since before the snow.

"He's got your name as well," Rae said.

She was in the kitchen door with a cup. He had not heard her come back.

"The dealer. On the Holloway." She said it to the cup. "He took it. He said a captain's word was worth the mare's keep till the thaw, and nothing past it, and he'd hold her that long on it. After the thaw it's coin or she's sold on." She drank. "I said you'd hear. Now you've heard."

Till the thaw. A grey mare in a dealer's byre with his name over her like a roof that would last until the snow went.

"Captain." Hollis, from the chair. He had the lad's blanket on his knee as he always had now, and he was looking at the almoner, who was looking at the floor. "I'll ask it, since nobody else will, and since I'm the one who nearly broke his neck for it. What does a captain do with a man who did a terrible thing for love?"

Nobody answered him. The snow came in at the wicket and lay on the flags, and the almoner stood with his sister's hair inside his shirt and the stores key in his fist, and waited to be told.

BetweenOne Question

He was at the wicket before her, in the grey before the dawn bell, with the snow stopped and the lower terrace's fires gone to red eyes in the dark below.

She came along the wall with the ox-bell at her belt not ringing, because she walked so that it did not, and stopped when she saw him, and went on pinning the cloak at her throat. The hood had given up on her hair in the night; it came out past the wool and the wind took it.

"You're going down," he said.

"Hob won't eat till I'm there."

"Rae." He had the good hand on the gate-bar and the other inside his shirt where the fingers could not be looked at. "I don't know where you're from. I don't know what you carry when it isn't the House's, or why you keep coming up a mountain you don't live on. I know your oxen's names. That's what I've got."

"That's a fair bit."

"It isn't."

She finished the clasp. Then she looked at him properly, the grey-green eyes going over him the way they went over a load, and he had the feeling of being weighed and the weight written down somewhere he would not see.

"One question a night," she said. "You know the rule. It's not night, so I'll allow it's a new one."

He thought about the hand on the letter, and let it go. It was the thing she would not give, and asking for it again would only cost him the one he had.

"What do you want?" he said. "Not the House. You."

For a moment he thought she would say Aye and go. The bell on the terrace rang for the grey cloaks' dawn, far down, thin, and the House's own answered it above them, and she waited for both to finish.

"Coldmere," she said. "The inn on the tarn, the one with the bell-house across the road, where they read your list. My uncle keeps it. The cart's mine, and Hob and Dun, as far as the road knows. He paid for them, once, and I've been paying him back by the load since. One day I'll pay the last of it, and there'll be nobody's hand on the rope but mine, and I'll drive what I like where I like." She said it flat, as a manifest. "That's one."

"You said one."

"I did." She looked down the Steps into the cloud, where the road was. "I've brought a deal of people to the foot of those stairs. Some I've brought up. I don't count them; Wren counts. I keep the faces." Her hands had found each other inside the cloak. "I'd like, once, to carry a thing up this mountain and see it come down whole. That's the other. You'll not get a third."

He had nothing to put against that, and did not try.

"My turn," Rae said. "You sent your name down for a lame mare. Not the Prior's, which would have bought her for a word. Yours, which the Lowmarch won't take for a copper and you knew it." The ox-bell stirred as she shifted, and she put her hand on it to still it. "Why?"

He looked at the cloud. There was a song about a grey mare of Edgemoor that every road in the Lowmarch sang, and his men had sung it worse than most. She never once asked me the why or the where. It had not been written about his mare; it was older than both of them. It had been true of her anyway.

"Her name's Thistle," he said.

He had not said it since the water. It came out of him rough, like a thing kept in a pocket too long.

"She carried me into that river and she didn't ask the why or the where, and I've not said her name since. I'm not having her stand in a dealer's byre under somebody else's word. If it has to be a bad word, it'll be mine."

Rae said nothing for the length of the dawn bell's last stroke. Then she nodded, once, to the cloud and not to him, and drew up her hood.

"Road's the road," she said, and opened the wicket.

At the turn of the Last Hundred she stopped, the way she had stopped twice before, and spoke without looking back.

"I'll tell her."

BetweenWhat Climbed

The undercroft door stood at the dark end as it had stood all week: shut, the Prior's key gone from it, the cold still coming under it along the flags. Nobody sat by it now. Darrow stood by it instead, one hand on the wall, and waited, because between every bell of those nights an old woman had come down from her tower to sit at this door, and he had been asleep for all of it.

She came at the end of the grey, when the lamps were out and the windows had not yet taken over: two shawls, the dark kerchief, the walnut knuckles, moving down the row at the pace of someone who had been up and down a tower all night and meant to go up it again. She saw him before she reached him and did not slow. Sister Pell did not alter her pace for knights.

"Sister."

She looked at his mouth, not his eyes, and then past him at the door, and said nothing. He had seen her do it at the council, watch the Prior's lips as if the words were on them and not in the air, and had taken it for a trick of the old. It was the bells. Fifty years under them, and whatever she heard now she heard with her eyes.

"What does the bell say at dusk?" he said.

"It says dusk." She had the rasp of a hinge nobody oiled. "Be under it and you'll hear it."

"Not the bell. You. The Prior says names in the chapel. Hollis says a thing to the lad at every bell that doesn't carry. The lad said his oath. I've been in this hall a season and I don't know what the woman on the rope says."

She had not caught all of it. He saw her lose it somewhere around Hollis, in the bad light, and stop reaching for it, the way you stop reaching for a rope that has gone past.

"Slower," she said. "And to me, not the floor. I can't read a floor."

He turned so the window was on his face and said it again, plain, and left off the joke he had been going to put on the end of it, because she would only have made him say that again too.

"Hm." She looked at the door. "There's a rope wants me."

"After."

"There's no after a bell, Captain. There's the next one." But she did not go. She put one hand flat on the oak of the undercroft door, and as it turned he saw the palm: two lines rubbed red across it, straight as a rule, a week old and not healed. He opened his mouth.

"Ask the bell," she said, without looking up from her hand, and that was the end of that.

"Three bells," she said. "You've heard two. The hour bell you hear till you don't. Old Mercy you heard at the gate, late, which I'll thank you not to say to me, and every dawn and dusk since. The third's the Small. Once, flat, when one of them goes." Her chin went down the row, toward the breathing dark. "You've not heard it. The Prior's not had a name to stop saying since you came up. Keep it that way, if you've any say in it, which you haven't."

"And Old Mercy. Why does the valley know her, and not the hour bell?"

"Because there's not another like her and never will be. She cracked, the Quenching's winter, before any count I keep. They broke her up and poured her again with the cracked piece in the pour, and she came out with that voice. You can't make it twice. A bell with her own crack in her."

Darrow's right hand was inside his shirt, where the linen was. He left it there.

"The words," he said.

"They're said to the rope. One line to a stroke, four strokes, and nobody below hears them; she's over them. I've said them every dusk she's rung, and there's been fewer of those than you'd think, and nobody's asked." She considered him, mouth first and then the rest. "You'll have them wrong. You'll say them like a Lowmarch soldier and she'll know."

"Then give them to me right."

She knocked once on the oak with the knuckle of her first finger, the sound of a cup on stone, and said the first line with it.

"Day's done and the door's shut." Knock. "The blade stays below." Knock. "What climbed, we keep." Knock. "And the bell says so."

The hall took them and gave nothing back. Down the row a far cot breathed in, and out.

"Again," she said. "You. To me."

He said them to her, slow, with the window on his mouth, and got as far as the third line and stopped on it.

She read the stop. She had read harder things.

"Aye," said Sister Pell. "That one's not yours to say." She took her hand off the door and put it back under the shawls. "Dusk. Under the tower, in the court, where I can see your mouth from the window. You'll say them with the strokes, and if you're late on a line I'll know it, and so will she."

"You'll be on the rope."

"I've been on the rope fifty years, Captain. I can look out of a window." She was already going, back up the row toward her tower at the pace she had come, and said the last of it to the hall and not to him, since she could not see his answer and did not want it.

"Four strokes. Don't be clever at it."

Scene 3The Ramp

He had the four lines by heart before the hour bell was done, and the third one still stopped him, so he was saying the first two under his breath at the window when Wren came up the row at a pace that meant something was about to be killed.

"There's a dog," she said, before she had stopped. "In the kennels, under the cloister. A grey bitch. She's bitten two lay brothers and the farrier, who says he's never been bitten by anything in his life, so that's three bites and one liar. Four days she's not eaten. She sleeps against the door that faces this way." She jerked her chin at the far wall, the hall's footings, the cloister below them. "The kennel-master's going to knock her on the head before the grey cloaks look up from the terrace and see what we feed. He says today." She held out the stick, the borrowed one he would not use. "You've got something in common."

"Have we."

"She's got a splint on her back leg."

He looked at the stick, and took it, and put it under his arm like a captain's baton, which it had never been.

The way down to the kennels was a ramp, not a stair: a long ramp of worn boards with slats nailed across it for purchase, built, Wren told him, so that a dog with a bad leg could be got up it. The thaw had come in under the cloister arch and left the slats running with grey slush. Halfway down he put the stick to the boards once, because the alternative was going down the rest of it on his backside, and Wren had the grace to be looking at the sky.

"You caught it," he said. "The hair. At the gate. He'd have had to pick his sister out of the slush in front of all of us, and you didn't let him."

Wren did not look round. Her lips moved, the way they did along a row of lamps.

"Technically this is a ramp," she said. "It doesn't count as a stair." A pause, three steps of it. "It can go in the room under the one."

The kennel-master met them at the run with a mallet in his belt and a face like a shut gate: a lay brother who fed the House's dogs on what the kitchen would not admit to, and had views. He looked at the Captain of the Ninth, at the stick under the arm, at the wrapped fingers.

"Menders spend good meat on bad knights," he said, "and I've said so to the Prior. They'll not spend it on a bad dog as well. She's not the House's. She climbed, in the first snow; they do, and the kitchen feeds them till I notice." He nodded down over the wall, to the lower terrace and its grey fires. "And that lot keep the cage now, and count what comes up it. The oil was three jars short last week and nobody said why. I'll not give them the meat to count next." He looked at the run. "Mind her. She's quick."

She was against the far door of the run, the one in the wall that faced up the hill toward the hall: a grey bitch, long-boned, all rib and shoulder, with a splint of two laths and twine bound on the hind leg and the hair worn off around it. Her lips came up off her teeth as they came in. There was no sound with it, which was worse.

Darrow gave Wren the stick. He put his good hand on the post of the run, bent the good leg, took the bad one out straight in front of him as Maelis had taught him to take it anywhere near a floor, and let himself down the post into the straw, slow, the way a man lowers a full bucket. It was not kneeling. He made sure of that. He sat with his back to the boards and the bad leg out in the straw, and held out his left hand, flat, low, and did nothing else at all.

She came off the door like a thrown thing. He saw it coming the way he saw a line move, the shoulders first, and every year of the Lances in him said take the hand back, and he left it where it was. She had it in her teeth before he had finished deciding: the meat of the thumb, a hard clamp and a tooth through the skin, and her eyes on his from a hand's width off, yellow and waiting to see what he would do.

He did nothing. It hurt. He sat in the straw with his hand in a dog's mouth and his blood going warm down his wrist, and breathed, in and out, the way he had breathed with the bell.

"Captain," said Wren, very quietly, from the gate.

"Leave it."

The jaw let go. Not all at once: a loosening, as if she had been told something. She stood over the hand with her head low and did not smell it. She was shaking, a fine shake along the ribs, the kind a horse has after a hard road and not before one, and her eyes stayed on the hand for whatever it would do next. It did nothing. The other hand, the wrapped one, had come an inch off his knee toward her without being asked, and he put it back. Then she smelled the hand, and smelled up his arm, and went down the length of the bad leg in the straw with her nose an inch off it, all the way to the boot. There she stopped. She stood for a long breath on three legs, and lay down, the way a tired thing lies down, in pieces; and put the splinted leg across his boot, and laid her head on her own paws, and shut her eyes.

The kennel-master had his hand on the mallet. He took it off.

"Four," said Wren, under her breath. "One of them a knight."

"She's not slept since she came in," the kennel-master said. He looked at the dog for a long while, and then at the blood on the Captain's hand, and then down over the wall at the fires on the terrace. "Till they're gone. The grey cloaks. If she's eating from somebody's hand by then, I'll find her the meat. If she isn't, I'll not be told twice."

Darrow looked at the leg across his boot, the laths and the twine, the weight of her, which was more than he had thought.

He did not move his foot.

BetweenHer Mark

Getting out from under the dog took longer than getting bitten had. He drew the boot back a finger's width at a time while she slept, and at the last of it she opened one eye and watched the foot go and did not lift her head, and the kennel-master at the gate let him go without a word about the hand. Wren gave him the stick at the foot of the ramp and took it back at the hall door, and wrote something.

Brother Anselm was where he had been told to be, on the cot next to Darrow's, with the stores key in his fist and his sister inside his shirt. He saw the hand before Darrow had sat down.

"Oh, Captain. I'm sorry, let me—" He was up and gone to the linen press and back before he had finished being sorry, with a strip of clean linen and the basin, and sat on the edge of his own cot and drew the bitten hand across the gap into both of his as if it had been given him to keep. His own hands were steady. Darrow had not expected that. "The grey one. I heard. Hold still, I'm sorry, this will—"

"Brother. Your sister's mark. The one nobody else has." Anselm's hands stopped on the linen, and went on. "What is it?"

"It's an M." He said it to the hand. "She was five. Our mother was teaching her her letters on the hearthstone with a burnt stick, and she'd not have the M the way it was shown her, two hills and a valley. She put a loop in the valley, like a hook, and said that was so it would hold. She's put it on everything since. On her clothes-chest. On the lintel of our mother's house the day we buried her, with a nail. Under her name on every letter to me, so I'd know it was her hand even when—" He wound the linen once round the thumb. "Even when it wasn't."

"And the clerk wrote the words over it."

"She signed under them. That's what I can't—" He trailed off and did not come back, and tied the linen instead, a flat knot, a storesman's knot.

"Why is she the brave one?"

Anselm sat back. For a moment he looked almost as if he might smile.

"When she was twelve the herald cried our father's name at the bell. Debt. The mill's debt, not his, but he'd put his hand to the paper for the miller, and the miller was gone. Cried from the steps after the noon bell, in front of the whole village, and we were in the crowd, the two of us, and I was going to take her home." He turned the key over in his free hand. "A man named at the bell may answer at the bell. Everyone knows that. Nobody does it. She went up the steps in her pinafore and answered. She told the herald her father had put his hand to a paper, and the man who had asked him to was gone down the Holloway with the mill's money, and the herald could cry that too if he liked crying things." He looked at the hand he had bound. "He did. The next day. I apologised to him. I was fourteen." He turned the key again. "That's the two of us, Captain. I say sorry first and ask second. She asks, and she has never said sorry in her life, and she went to the Spire to ask what had become of the brothers' letters because somebody should, and I—" He stopped. "I write the letters."

Darrow looked at the knot on his thumb. It would hold.

"Where's the man with the mill's money now?"

"Nobody knows. She's not forgiven him that either." Anselm got up with a hand on the cot's edge and stood with the basin, and then did the thing Darrow had seen him do with the lay brothers and the cook and once with Hollis, and had taken for a tic.

"Thank you, Captain," he said.

"For what?"

"I don't know yet." He took the basin away up the row. "You will."

BetweenNever Bitten

The forge was at the far end of the kennel yard, under the cloister wall, and Darrow found it by the sound, which was not the sound of iron. It was a knife on leather, slow, the stroke of a man who had all day and meant to use it.

The farrier did not look up. He was a lay brother built short and wide, with a leather apron black at the belly and the House's hood pushed back off a square head cropped close and going grey, and he sat on an upturned keg in the one square of light the door let in, a leather cup in his lap and a round knife in his fist, taking the inside of the cup down a shaving at a time. Behind him the fire was banked to a red eye. Nothing in the place had the shape of a horseshoe. There were laths stacked by length, a drum's tooth half-filed on the anvil, a row of bell clappers on pegs from the size of a thumb to the size of a forearm, and on a board by the door, in chalk, a dozen small marks that Darrow's eye went to before the rest of him had decided to.

"You're in my light," the farrier said.

Darrow moved. The farrier went on with the knife.

"I'm told you've never been bitten by anything in your life."

"Cobb," he said. "And that's right."

"Wren Ashdown says that makes three bites and a liar."

"Wren Ashdown counts her own feet going downstairs." He turned the cup a quarter and took another shaving. "Burn," he said, before Darrow had asked, and lifted the back of his right hand into the light without stopping the knife in his left: a half-ring of small white marks across it, the size of a hound's jaw, old and shiny. "Took a bar out of the fire with the wrong hand. Forty years back."

"It's got teeth in it."

"It's got a burn in it." The hand went back to the leather.

Darrow looked at the cup instead, because the man wanted him to look at the hand and argue, and he had spent a season learning what it cost to argue with people who worked with their hands. It was a good cup. Oiled leather, dark, the shape of a man's thigh an inch above where the thigh stopped, and three lines scored round the inside of it with the knife's point where the leather had been taken down and would be taken down further.

"Whose?"

"Whose do you think." The farrier held it up to the light and looked down it like a man sighting a bow. "Beech leg, leather cup, nine steps, and Ser Hollis swore at every one, and the whole hall heard him, and I heard him from here. A cup's not made once. It's made to the stump, and the stump changes, and he'll not come down to me." The knife went in again. "I made that leg in a week off a tracing on a bit of sacking, because the Mender said he'd not wait. He didn't wait. Now he's got a boot that fits somebody else and he's blaming the beech."

"He blames everything. He got to the carter by the second milestone."

"Aye, well. The carter's not the one who has to look him in the face when it bites." He blew the shavings off the leather. "You rode a grey."

It was not a question, and Darrow looked at the board by the door. The chalk marks were brands, a dozen of them, each the size of a palm, drawn the way they sit on a horse's quarter. The Second's was a tower; the Second liked you to know it. The Sixth's was a mule's shoe, which the Sixth had not chosen and could not be rid of. The third from the end was two posts and a bar across, and he had stood in the smoke at the fair while it went onto sixty horses, the greys fighting the men who held them.

"Ninth," said the farrier, not looking. "The gate. Edgemoor breeds them and the fair burns them, and I held the iron twenty years before the mountain. Every lance has its mark and every grey carries it on the near quarter, and a man who's held the iron knows them in his sleep." A shaving fell. "There's not a horse within a thousand steps of us. I chalk them up anyway. A hand forgets."

Darrow had nothing to say to that, and said nothing, and the farrier seemed to approve, because he said nothing back for the length of three strokes.

"Right." He held the cup up again, looked down it, and tossed it; and Darrow caught it against his chest with both bad hands, and swore, and the farrier watched him swear with the first interest he had shown in anything. "That's close. Not there. Take it up to him. He puts it on, he stands, he takes ten steps, not nine, and he tells you where it bites, and you tell me, not him. If he says it's the beech, you tell him the beech is fine. The beech is."

"I'm your runner now."

"You're a man going up to the hall with his hands full." He had already picked up the next piece of leather. "Better than coming down with them empty. Go on. You're in my light."

Scene 4The Price

Hollis took his ten steps at the noon bell, with the farrier's new cup strapped on and the whole row pretending not to count, and swore at seven of them.

"It bites at the back," he said, at the tenth, standing, with his hand on the end of the lad's cot and his face the colour of the window. "Tell Cobb that. Tell him the beech is fine, since he'll want to hear it, and that if he says ten again I'll walk down to him on it and he can measure the stump from underneath." He sat. "Captain. Your hands are a disgrace."

They were, and they were warm, which was the strange part; Darrow turned them over by the brazier once to be sure of it, the wrapped one and the bound one, and found he was pleased with them, and kept that to himself.

The rain came in at dusk, the thin kind that does not fall so much as arrive, and the court was running with it when Old Mercy began.

He stood where he had said he would, under the tower, in the court, with his face turned up to the window where he could not see her and she could see him. The Prior stood beside him. She had come out of the chapel without being asked and had not said why. On the first stroke he said the first line, aloud, to the rain.

Day's done and the door's shut. The bell, slow, with the crack in its voice. The blade stays below. Across the court, at the bars, a grey shape had come up to the gate with the smell of hot iron ahead of it, and stood, and listened. What climbed, we keep. He said the third line and did not stop on it this time, and the bell went over it. And the bell says so.

The last stroke went down the mountain and the valley had it. Nobody in the court moved until the bronze had finished arguing with itself.

"Captain," said Confessor Ivo Marrant, pleasantly, through the bars. "I've never heard it said. Only rung. Thank you."

Darrow walked across the court to him, slow, with the Prior keeping his pace, and the wet flags trying to take his feet, and the knee holding every one of them.

"Mother Ione." Marrant's hood was back; the rain stood on his bald head and he did not seem to feel it. The censer hung from his fist and smoked at the rain. "A small thing, and I'd not raise it at the bell if it would wait. The House's letters haven't come down this week. The cage has gone down empty twice." He said it as if he were sorry for the cage. "I ask after your almoner. Brother Anselm. There's a woman in Calden waiting on her brother's reply, and she's asked me, as a kindness, to ask."

Nobody at the bars said anything. Behind them in the court the House had come out as it always came out, and stood in the rain, and listened.

"I'll be plain, Captain, since you are. Send the almoner down to me, and two names come off my paper. I'll not say whose. You'll know whose." He smiled at the whole court. "A good man, I'm told, who did a foolish thing for his sister. The Spire is kinder to those than to captains."

Darrow looked at him the way he had looked at him the first night, for the thing under the words, and the script came up pale behind his palms and let him look.

A man in the rain with a smile and a censer and borrowed warmth in his chest, which he had seen before; and under the words, nothing. No lie. That was the worst of it. He meant the two names. He could afford to mean them: they would come off his paper, and his paper was not the only paper on the road. There were banners a day off on the Holloway that were not grey, and whoever rode under them carried their own. Marrant would keep his word and lose nothing by it, and have the almoner, and a man who had found that a lever worked did not stop at one door.

"The almoner is a brother of this House," said Mother Ione. She had not raised her voice. She had lowered it, and the court leaned. "He came up the Steps unarmed, years ago, and was warmed and fed, which is the law of guests, Confessor, and older than yours. Whoever sent him. A guest the House has kept that long is the House's. You'll not have his name from the Captain, because it isn't the Captain's to give."

"Thank you, Mother." Marrant bowed his head to her, a little, in the rain. "For your candour. I'll remember who said it."

"You'll have an answer at the dawn bell," Darrow said.

"I'll wait." The smile did not change. "It's the thing I'm best at. Your Prior has just told me whose roof the answer comes from. I'll ask under it, at dawn." He drew his hood up at last. "Good night, Captain. Good night, Mother. A fine verse. I'd not known the third line was yours to say."

He went down the terrace path into the dark and the rain took him.

Wren was on the wall-walk with Pell's glass, where nobody had told her to be. She came down the gatehouse stair at a run and stopped at Darrow's elbow and did not, for once, say a number straight off.

"Five fires," she said. "There's always been six. One of them went down the Steps before the bell, with a horse. He's not come back up."

They were at his cot after the night lamps: Anselm on the next one with the key in his fist, as he had been told; Hollis in his chair with the lad's blanket and the new cup still strapped on, because he would not give Cobb the satisfaction; Wren on the floor with her back to the brazier and the ledger shut on her knee; and Maelis at the cot's foot with her hands in her sleeves, who had not been asked and had come.

Darrow gave them the price the way Marrant had given it, without the smile, and what he had read under it: that the man meant every word, and that meaning it cost him nothing.

"Send me down," said Anselm. He said it to the floor, at once, as if he had been holding it ready since the dawn bell. "Two names, Captain. Yours is one. I'll go down in the morning, and I'll apologise to him, and—"

"No."

"Captain, I'm sorry, but she's—"

"Brother Anselm." The full name, and the almoner's mouth shut on it. "You'll not go down. That isn't on the table, and I'll not have you put it there again."

A silence, in which the brazier settled.

"Then I'll ask it a second time," said Hollis, "since the first one went begging." He had both hands on the chair's arms and the beech leg out in front of him in its new cup. "What does a captain do with a man who did a terrible thing for love? Only now there's a price on him, and a paper, and a woman in Calden, so it's not a riddle any more. It's a night with a bell at the end of it."

Darrow looked at Maelis, because she had come and had not said why.

"Wrong question," she said, before he had asked one.

Wren opened the ledger, and shut it, and did not write.

In the doorway from the chapel stood the Prior, with her hands in her sleeves and the rain still on her coif, and she asked it the way she asked everything, so that it was not a question.

"What is the House for, Captain?"

BetweenThree Answers

The Prior went back into the chapel without waiting for an answer, which was her way of giving him the night, and the four of them sat on in the brazier's light with the question where she had left it.

"I'll ask it round," Darrow said. "Since Hollis asked twice and got nothing. What would you do with the price. Each of you. Then I'll tell you what I'm thinking, and you can tell me what's wrong with it."

"Nothing," said Hollis at once. "That's what I'd do with it. You don't buy from a man who's told you he'll wait. You let him wait. He said the names come off his paper. He never said whose paper they go on next." He shifted the beech leg in its cup and winced, and did not say the cup's name. "Shield the brother. Say nothing of the satchel to the hall or the gate. Keep him where you can see him, which you're doing. Let the grey bastard camp till the thaw."

"Wren."

She had the ledger open on her knee and had not written in it. "Technically he's already using the letters," she said. "The clerk writes hers. Brother Anselm writes the House's. Every seventh day one goes down the cage and one comes up, and the grey cloaks read both at the winch-house, because they read everything now. That's what inspected means." She looked at the almoner, and then at Darrow, and he saw her see it. "So a letter down that says what you want him to think. It's not even a lie if it's in his hand and his words and he'd have written it anyway. It only has to be a letter he'd write. Frightened. Sorry. Asking what to do next, so they tell him, and then you know what they want him to do." She stopped counting, which was the frightening part. "And if they catch it?"

"Then the next letter up comes without the hook in her M," Darrow said.

Nobody said anything. The brazier ticked.

"That's what I'm thinking," he said. "The letters."

"I know you are," said Hollis. "You've had the face on since the gate." He leaned forward on the chair arms. "Then hear this from the one man here who's lied to a lad every bell for a week. I'd not do it. But if you do: he's sat there and heard what it costs. He's not heard what it says. Or you put your own name on it and go down the Steps yourself. There's no third way that isn't the Confessor's."

"Brother."

Anselm had the key in his fist and his sister against his chest, and he had been looking at the floor since the word mark.

"I'll write it," he said. "I'm sorry. I'll write whatever you give me, in my hand, the way I'd write it, and I'll put it in the satchel and take it down on the cage with my own—" He stopped. "No. They'll not let me down. Somebody else takes it down."

"And if the next one up has no hook in the M, then I'll know what I did, and you'll know, and I'd sooner know than not. She would. She'd say so." He looked up. "She'd say ask first and be sorry after. Ask them what they want me to do. I've been sorry a month, Captain. I'd like to ask."

Darrow looked at Maelis, because she had sat through all of it with her hands in her sleeves and her eyes on his hands, not his face.

"Who signs it?" she said.

"He does."

"Then it's his to say yes to, and he's said it." She stood. "Decide by the bell. Whatever you decide, I'll want those hands at the dawn forms, both of them, so don't sit up writing letters you've not decided to send."

"Not whatever you give him," he said. "Every word, and what it's for. That's my price. It's cheaper than his."

Wren wrote something in the ledger, small, and shut it on her finger.

BetweenThe Loop

Wren had gone, and Maelis, and the lamps were down to the far one and the brazier, and Darrow had the almoner's ink and a sheet of the House's paper on the end of his cot between them, and had not touched either.

He did not sit close. He sat on his own cot with the width of the gap between them, the way he had sat since the night the lid came off, and Anselm, who noticed such things, kept to his.

"Before a word goes on that," Darrow said, "you'll have all of it. What it says, what it's for, and what it could cost. Hollis made it his price, and I'd have done it without him, so hear it as mine. I'll not have one more man in this House sent somewhere on a paper he hasn't read."

Anselm had the key in his lap and his hands folded over it, the way he folded them before he apologised, and did not.

"And hear this first, so there's no mistaking where I am. You set that stone under forty beds and went up to your supper. I've not stopped seeing the trowel in your hand, and I'll not pretend I have." He let it sit. "And a man whose trade is lying put it there for you, with your sister's hair, and I know the shape of that. I was sent into a river on a paper once. So you get every word, Brother. Not because you've earned it. Because the other way is his."

"Yes, Captain."

"The letter's to your sister, the way every one of them has been. He'll read it at the winch-house before it goes anywhere, the way he reads them all, and he'll not know that you know it. So you write to her, and you let him read. Your hand, your words, the way you'd write if nobody had told you anything. You tell her the thing they said you had to finish is finished: the second one set, under the far cots, two nights ago, and the hall gone quiet since. You let her see you're frightened, which is true, and you ask her to ask them what you're to do next, since they're the ones with her ear. The words are yours. You know how your letters go. I don't." He laid it out the way he laid a line before a charge, one piece after the other, so a man could see where he stood in it. "And then you ask after her. Not how she is. Where. You ask where she's kept, so you can send her warm things for the winter, because that's what a brother asks. And you ask her one thing only she knows the answer to. Not for her hand. A hand can be made to order, and he's made one already. An answer can't, not by him. That's the letter."

"And what it's for."

"For the House, three things. If he believes it, he waits for it to work, and a man who's waiting isn't climbing. If it's worth sending down the road, somebody rides to Calden with it, and that's one fewer on the terrace. And whatever he tells you to do next tells us what he's afraid of." Darrow looked at him across the gap. "For her, one thing, and it's the one I care about. We don't know where she is. Kept, he says. Comfortably, he says twice. A kept woman is kept somewhere, and a clerk who answers a brother's question about her shawl will tell us the place, or lie about it, and either one is more than we have. That's the only road I can see that goes toward her. The other one, the trade, sends you down the Steps into their hands and gets her nothing. I'll not take it. I read him for a lie and found none, and I'm still not taking it."

The brazier ticked. Across it Hollis had the lad's hand in his and his eyes on the roof, and had not moved.

"And if it's caught," said Anselm.

"Then she pays for it. The next letter up comes without her mark, and I'll have done that, and you for writing it. I'll not tell you otherwise." He held the almoner's eyes. "It's a risk on her. It's a better risk than leaving her where she is and doing nothing, which is what I've done for a month. That's my judgment. You can refuse it."

Anselm looked at the paper for a long time.

"She'd say ask," he said at last. "She'd rather be asked after than traded for. She'd be angry at the trade. She'd be angry at me for the stone first, and she'd be right." He took the key off his lap and set it on the cot, and picked up the pen, and dipped it, and wrote.

He wrote the way he spoke, in starts, with the apologies in, and Darrow read it over his shoulder as it went down and changed not a word. It said everything he had asked it to say and none of it in the words he had given, and it was better for that: a man writing to his sister about snow and a shawl, with the thing in the middle where a clerk would look for it and a sister would read past it.

Mirren. I'm sorry this is late. The cage has gone down twice with nothing in it and I didn't trust the next one. We've had the first snow and a thaw on top of it and the Steps are all slush, Sister Pell has rung herself raw, and the Prior says every name every night the way she does. I'm well. I'm eating, before you ask.

You said in your last that they'd told you I had a thing to finish, and that when it was finished I could come down and fetch you home. It's finished. Two nights ago, the way I was shown, and the hall has been quieter since than I've known it. So tell them, and tell me when I'm to come, and I'll come.

You didn't say where they have you. Is it in the town, or up at the Spire? Mother's shawl is in the stores chest with the camphor and I'll send it down the moment I have a door to send it to. You'll want it before the cold.

And tell me what mother called the brindle cat. I've been trying to remember it for a week and it's gone, and it's been bothering me more than it should, and you'll know.

At the bottom, under his name, the almoner wrote one more line that Darrow had not given him, and sat back from it.

The loop still holds.

"That's mine," he said. "He'll read it and think nothing. She'll read it and know it's me writing, and not what they've had me write. If you want it out, say so. I'd sooner it stayed."

"It stays."

"When it's done," he said, "caught or not caught, I'm going down. Not to him. To the bell at Coldmere, where they cried my father. I'm going to stand on the steps and ask what they did with my sister, out loud, with the village there, the way she would. I'll want you to let me."

"Yes."

"You keep saying that." He put the letter inside his shirt, against the lock of hair, and the key back on its cord, and lay down on his cot with his face to the Captain, and shut his eyes, and did not sleep.

Across the brazier, Hollis's thumb went on over the lad's knuckles.

"That's paid," he said, to the lad, not to Darrow. "Go to sleep, Captain. You've a bell to answer."

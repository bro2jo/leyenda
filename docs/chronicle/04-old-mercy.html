Chapter 4Old Mercy

Scene 1The Almoner

The thin novice who fed the far cots was told at the night bell, standing at the foot of Darrow's cot with his hands in his sleeves, and he thanked the Captain for it, which nobody had expected, and went back up the hall to look at the floor under forty beds.

Mother Ione was told at the dawn bell.

Darrow went to her. He did it on his own leg, up the row with the ends of his right-hand fingers wrapped in Maelis's linen, so that the hand would not quite close, and the cold had got into him in the night and stayed, so that he walked the hall the way a man walks a frozen field, carefully, each step a thing decided. He found her in the chapel with the lamps not yet lit, as he had found her once before, and told her all of it there with his back to the door: the painted hall, the black thing in the rock under the row, the lid of new mortar, the satchel. He told her it had been Hollis's asking that the keepers came first, because it was.

She heard him out with her hands folded. When he had done she looked at him the way she looked into a full room.

"You said you would tell me when you knew what it was," she said. "I counted the nights between. Two." She unfolded her hands. "I say every name under this roof in this chapel every night, Captain, and I have not had to stop saying one since you came. I shall go on saying all of them. That is all I have to say about where I come in your order of telling."

She asked him nothing. She lit the first lamp, and he went back down the row.

Hollis came at the end of the dawn bell, carried.

Two lay brothers had him between them in a chair with poles lashed to it, the way the House moved its heaviest, and he sat in it with his one foot braced and the lad's blanket folded on his knee, and in front of the chair, walking ahead of it the way a man walks in front of a spear, came Brother Anselm.

Darrow knew the almoner as he knew the hour bells: a soft, tired man in his forties with ink on two fingers, who took the House's letters down to the carter each week with the satchel across his chest, and apologised before he asked for anything, and had a sister at Calden he mentioned too often. The satchel was across his chest now. His hands were shaking so that the flap of it trembled.

"Set me down here," said Hollis, at the foot of the cot. "Brother. Tell the Captain what you told me at the third bell. All of it. I'll know if you leave out the part you left out the first time."

Anselm sat on the end of the next cot because his legs put him there.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Captain. I'm so sorry. I should have— the stores key." He touched it, on its cord at his belt, as if to show it was real. "It opens the stores. It opens the old stair door as well, where the wine is laid down, below the kitchen end. Nobody's used it for the wine in my time; the wine's in the stores now, it's drier. But the stair goes down. It comes out in the hall below, at the kitchen end, behind a pillar. I never went near the other door. The one with the flour."

So there were two ways down. He had been counting steps on one of them for a week.

"Before the snow," Anselm said. "I take the letters down to the carter. Every week, the cage down, the bag on my chest, and I wait at the winch-house for her to come, and sometimes it's a day. There was a man there. A clerk. In grey." His eyes went to the window, to the court, to the lower terrace below it. "Not one of— not one of those. Older. He wrote. He had a desk in the winch-house, they'd put a board on two kegs for him, and he had my sister's letter."

"Her letter."

"In his hand. He'd written it for her, he said, as she'd dictated it, because she was—" He stopped, and started again from somewhere else. "She's at Calden. Mirren. She's the brave one of us. She went to the Spire to ask what had become of the brothers' letters from Coldmere, because they'd stopped, and she asked the wrong man, in front of the wrong people, and now she's—kept. In Calden. Comfortably, he said. He said it twice. He showed me her hand on the letter under his. He'd written it, but she'd signed it, and she'd signed it with the mark she puts, that nobody else knows, from when we were—" He trailed off, and did not come back.

"And the thing," said Darrow.

"He gave me two." Anselm's hands went to the satchel's flap and could not work the buckle. "Wrapped. He said they were a mercy. For the wounded. He said the fever comes off a man through the floor if there's a stone to take it, that's how the Spire does it, that's why the Spire's wards are quiet, and the Menders were too proud to use it and he'd been sent to see the House had the use of it anyway. Low in the wall, under the beds, as low as I could get. One under the hall, and when it had taken, the other under the far cots." He looked up. "I thought it was a cruel kind of mercy. I set the first one. I carried it down the wine stair with a trowel and a lamp and I set it and I came up and I was sick on the stair. I couldn't— I haven't set the other one. It's still—"

"Give it here," said Maelis.

She was behind Hollis's chair. Darrow had not heard her come. She put out her hand, flat, and Anselm found the buckle at last and brought out a thing the size of a fist wrapped in the letters' oilcloth, and put it in her palm as if it might go off, and she closed her fingers on it and looked at nothing, and put it in her coat, and said no more than that.

"I never saw him kneel," Anselm said, to the floor. "The clerk. They all kneel, the grey ones, at the dawn bell, you can see them from the wall. He wrote. He never knelt. I thought that was—"

Hollis moved.

He did not stand; he could not. He leaned forward in the chair with his hands on the poles and his whole weight behind his face, and the chair creaked, and the two lay brothers stepped back from it without deciding to, and Anselm shut his eyes the way the lad shut his when he was told.

"You set it under my boy," said Hollis, very quietly. "You set it under forty of them and you went up to your supper. Mercy. I'll show you what a Sixth sergeant does with—"

"Ser Hollis Garrow."

It came out of Darrow in the voice from the ford, the one that had put the Ninth into a line it did not want to hold. He had not used it since the water. He had not known he still had it. It went up the row like a thrown stone and the hall went quiet to the far cots, and Hollis stopped with his hands on the poles.

"Sit down," Darrow said. "Sergeant."

A moment. Then Hollis let out a breath that was half a laugh, with nothing in it, and settled back into the chair, and his hands let go of the poles.

"Captain," he said.

Darrow found he was standing. He did not remember getting up. The knee held.

"Brother Anselm." The almoner opened his eyes. "You'll stay in the hall where I can see you. You'll eat. You'll keep the stores key on you and you'll give it to nobody, not the Prior, not me. Nobody goes down that stair again but with my word and the Mender's count." He looked at the satchel. "When's the next letter?"

"They come up with the carter," Anselm whispered. "Every seventh day. There's— there's always another letter."

Behind them, at the far end of the row, the thin novice was on his knees on the flags with his ear to the floor, listening, and when he saw the Captain looking he got up, and dusted his knees, and went on with his bread.

Scene 2Her Hand

The cage went down empty the next morning, for the first time in anyone's count. The almoner's satchel hung on its hook by the stores door with nothing in it, because the man who carried it was sitting on the cot next to Darrow's with the stores key in his fist and had been told not to move, and the winch-brothers, who had been told nothing, sent the cage down bare and stood at the drum looking at one another.

It came up at dusk with nothing in it either. Rae Thorne came up the Steps instead.

Wren saw her first, through Pell's glass from the wall: a hooded shape on the Last Hundred, climbing the way she climbed, not counting. "She's got something inside her cloak," Wren reported, coming down the row at a run with her scarf flying. "She's holding her arm across it. She did that with your brother's letter."

Darrow was on his feet before she had finished. The cold had let go of him a little in the night; there was warmth in his hands again, and a dull red heat in the ends of the right one's fingers where the feeling was coming back under the linen, which Maelis said was good and felt like the opposite. He went down the hall to the gate with Wren, and Hollis had himself carried after, and Brother Anselm came because Darrow had told him to stay where he could be seen, and so the four of them were at the wicket when Rae came through it with the snow on her hood.

She did not say what she had. She looked at the almoner, and at the key in his fist, and at Darrow's wrapped fingers, and then she reached inside her cloak and brought out a letter and put it, not into the almoner's hands, but into Darrow's hand.

"For him," she said. "I'll not leave a thing like that in the cage overnight with that man's stool in the door."

It was addressed to Brother Anselm, almoner, at the House of Menders at Saint Ysolde's, in a neat upright clerk's hand with a long tail to its y, and sealed with plain wax.

He knew the hand.

He had seen it three times on the outside of folded paper, over blue wax, addressed to the Captain of the Ninth at the House of Menders: the same upright letters, the same long tail. Not Aldric's hand. Aldric wrote like a man falling downstairs. Whoever had written the direction on his brother's letters had written the direction on this one, and the man who had carried both up the Steps to the winch-house sat at a board on two kegs and never knelt.

"Rae." She had already turned for the kitchen. "This hand. Where have you seen it?"

She stopped. She did not turn round.

"I've seen that hand," she said, to the door.

"Where?"

"I've seen it." Her shoulders did something under the cloak, a settling, the way she set them to a cart in a rut. "That's what I've got for you on it. The kitchen's counted me in, I expect."

"Rae—"

"Aye," she said, and went in.

Wren looked at him. He shook his head, and gave the almoner his letter.

Anselm opened it standing, by the gate lamp, with the four of them watching and the snow coming in at the wicket, because he could not wait to sit and could not have sat if he tried. Something fell out of the fold as he opened it, and Wren caught it before it reached the flags.

A lock of hair. Dark, and tied with thread.

He read it. His lips moved along it the way Wren's moved along a row of lamps. Then he read it again, and folded it, and held out his hand to Wren for the hair, and put both inside his shirt, and did not weep. Darrow had been braced for weeping. This was worse. The man's face had simply shut, like a door with no handle.

"She says she is well kept," Anselm said. "She says they have told her I have a thing to finish, and that when it is finished I may come down to Calden and fetch her home. She says to do it." His voice had gone flat and careful, the way a man's goes when he is carrying something full to the brim. "It's her mark at the bottom. Nobody else has that. But she would never say kept. She'd never in her life say she was well kept. That's his word. He wrote it and she signed under it."

"Who did?" said Hollis.

"The clerk." Anselm looked at Darrow. "The one who never kneels. He wrote this, Captain. He writes all of them. He wrote her words for her and put her hair in with them so that I'd—" He stopped. "So that I'd finish."

Darrow stood in the gate lamp's light with his hand open and the snow on it, and read the shape of the thing the way he read a ford.

Marrant had not touched a weapon in front of the House in a month. He had read a list, and camped, and smiled, and kept the cage, and sent lamp oil up three jars short; and under all of it, through a clerk's hand and a cage rope, he had reached into the House and found the one man in it who loved somebody more than he feared anything, and had put a hand round that man's sister in Calden and squeezed, gently, with a lock of hair. He did not need a blade. He had never needed one. The lever was the whole of his method, and the House had been standing on it since before the snow.

"He's got your name as well," Rae said.

She was in the kitchen door with a cup. He had not heard her come back.

"The dealer. On the Holloway." She said it to the cup. "He took it. He said a captain's word was worth the mare's keep till the thaw, and nothing past it, and he'd hold her that long on it. After the thaw it's coin or she's sold on." She drank. "I said you'd hear. Now you've heard."

Till the thaw. A grey mare in a dealer's byre with his name over her like a roof that would last until the snow went.

"Captain." Hollis, from the chair. He had the lad's blanket on his knee as he always had now, and he was looking at the almoner, who was looking at the floor. "I'll ask it, since nobody else will, and since I'm the one who nearly broke his neck for it. What does a captain do with a man who did a terrible thing for love?"

Nobody answered him. The snow came in at the wicket and lay on the flags, and the almoner stood with his sister's hair inside his shirt and the stores key in his fist, and waited to be told.

BetweenOne Question

He was at the wicket before her, in the grey before the dawn bell, with the snow stopped and the lower terrace's fires gone to red eyes in the dark below.

She came along the wall with the ox-bell at her belt not ringing, because she walked so that it did not, and stopped when she saw him, and went on pinning the cloak at her throat. The hood had given up on her hair in the night; it came out past the wool and the wind took it.

"You're going down," he said.

"Hob won't eat till I'm there."

"Rae." He had the good hand on the gate-bar and the other inside his shirt where the fingers could not be looked at. "I don't know where you're from. I don't know what you carry when it isn't the House's, or why you keep coming up a mountain you don't live on. I know your oxen's names. That's what I've got."

"That's a fair bit."

"It isn't."

She finished the clasp. Then she looked at him properly, the grey-green eyes going over him the way they went over a load, and he had the feeling of being weighed and the weight written down somewhere he would not see.

"One question a night," she said. "You know the rule. It's not night, so I'll allow it's a new one."

He thought about the hand on the letter, and let it go. It was the thing she would not give, and asking for it again would only cost him the one he had.

"What do you want?" he said. "Not the House. You."

For a moment he thought she would say Aye and go. The bell on the terrace rang for the grey cloaks' dawn, far down, thin, and the House's own answered it above them, and she waited for both to finish.

"Coldmere," she said. "The inn on the tarn, the one with the bell-house across the road, where they read your list. My uncle keeps it. The cart's mine, and Hob and Dun, as far as the road knows. He paid for them, once, and I've been paying him back by the load since. One day I'll pay the last of it, and there'll be nobody's hand on the rope but mine, and I'll drive what I like where I like." She said it flat, as a manifest. "That's one."

"You said one."

"I did." She looked down the Steps into the cloud, where the road was. "I've brought a deal of people to the foot of those stairs. Some I've brought up. I don't count them; Wren counts. I keep the faces." Her hands had found each other inside the cloak. "I'd like, once, to carry a thing up this mountain and see it come down whole. That's the other. You'll not get a third."

He had nothing to put against that, and did not try.

"My turn," Rae said. "You sent your name down for a lame mare. Not the Prior's, which would have bought her for a word. Yours, which the Lowmarch won't take for a copper and you knew it." The ox-bell stirred as she shifted, and she put her hand on it to still it. "Why?"

He looked at the cloud. There was a song about a grey mare of Edgemoor that every road in the Lowmarch sang, and his men had sung it worse than most. She never once asked me the why or the where. It had not been written about his mare; it was older than both of them. It had been true of her anyway.

"Her name's Thistle," he said.

He had not said it since the water. It came out of him rough, like a thing kept in a pocket too long.

"She carried me into that river and she didn't ask the why or the where, and I've not said her name since. I'm not having her stand in a dealer's byre under somebody else's word. If it has to be a bad word, it'll be mine."

Rae said nothing for the length of the dawn bell's last stroke. Then she nodded, once, to the cloud and not to him, and drew up her hood.

"Road's the road," she said, and opened the wicket.

At the turn of the Last Hundred she stopped, the way she had stopped twice before, and spoke without looking back.

"I'll tell her."

BetweenWhat Climbed

He went back up the hall from the wicket with the thaw-wind at his back, and somewhere past the brazier he was no longer counting the cots, and could not have said where he had stopped.

The undercroft door stood at the dark end as it had stood all week: shut, the Prior's key gone from it, the cold still coming under it along the flags. Nobody sat by it now. Darrow stood by it instead, one hand on the wall, and waited, because between every bell of those nights an old woman had come down from her tower to sit at this door, and he had been asleep for all of it.

She came at the end of the grey, when the lamps were out and the windows had not yet taken over: two shawls, the dark kerchief, the walnut knuckles, moving down the row at the pace of someone who had been up and down a tower all night and meant to go up it again. She saw him before she reached him and did not slow. Sister Pell did not alter her pace for knights.

"Sister."

She looked at his mouth, not his eyes, and then past him at the door, and said nothing. He had seen her do it at the council, watch the Prior's lips as if the words were on them and not in the air, and had taken it for a trick of the old. It was the bells. Fifty years under them, and whatever she heard now she heard with her eyes.

"What does the bell say at dusk?" he said.

"It says dusk." She had the rasp of a hinge nobody oiled. "Be under it and you'll hear it."

"Not the bell. You. The Prior says names in the chapel. Hollis says a thing to the lad at every bell that doesn't carry. The lad said his oath. I've been in this hall a season and I don't know what the woman on the rope says."

She had not caught all of it. He saw her lose it somewhere around Hollis, in the bad light, and stop reaching for it, the way you stop reaching for a rope that has gone past.

"Slower," she said. "And to me, not the floor. I can't read a floor."

He turned so the window was on his face and said it again, plain, and left off the joke he had been going to put on the end of it, because she would only have made him say that again too.

"Hm." She looked at the door. "There's a rope wants me."

"After."

"There's no after a bell, Captain. There's the next one." But she did not go. She put one hand flat on the oak of the undercroft door, and as it turned he saw the palm: two lines rubbed red across it, straight as a rule, a week old and not healed. He opened his mouth.

"Ask the bell," she said, without looking up from her hand, and that was the end of that.

"Three bells," she said. "You've heard two. The hour bell you hear till you don't. Old Mercy you heard at the gate, late, which I'll thank you not to say to me, and every dawn and dusk since. The third's the Small. Once, flat, when one of them goes." Her chin went down the row, toward the breathing dark. "You've not heard it. The Prior's not had a name to stop saying since you came up. Keep it that way, if you've any say in it, which you haven't."

"And Old Mercy. Why does the valley know her, and not the hour bell?"

"Because there's not another like her and never will be. She cracked, the Quenching's winter, before any count I keep. They broke her up and poured her again with the cracked piece in the pour, and she came out with that voice. You can't make it twice. A bell with her own crack in her."

Darrow's right hand was inside his shirt, where the linen was. He left it there.

"The words," he said.

"They're said to the rope. One line to a stroke, four strokes, and nobody below hears them; she's over them. I've said them every dusk she's rung, and there's been fewer of those than you'd think, and nobody's asked." She considered him, mouth first and then the rest. "You'll have them wrong. You'll say them like a Lowmarch soldier and she'll know."

"Then give them to me right."

She knocked once on the oak with the knuckle of her first finger, the sound of a cup on stone, and said the first line with it.

"Day's done and the door's shut." Knock. "The blade stays below." Knock. "What climbed, we keep." Knock. "And the bell says so."

The hall took them and gave nothing back. Down the row a far cot breathed in, and out.

"Again," she said. "You. To me."

He said them to her, slow, with the window on his mouth, and got as far as the third line and stopped on it.

She read the stop. She had read harder things.

"Aye," said Sister Pell. "That one's not yours to say. You're what it's about." She took her hand off the door and put it back under the shawls. "Dusk. Under the tower, in the court, where I can see your mouth from the window. You'll say them with the strokes, and if you're late on a line I'll know it, and so will she."

"You'll be on the rope."

"I've been on the rope fifty years, Captain. I can look out of a window." She was already going, back up the row toward her tower at the pace she had come, and said the last of it to the hall and not to him, since she could not see his answer and did not want it.

"Four strokes. Don't be clever at it."

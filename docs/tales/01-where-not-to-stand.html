Tale 1Where Not to Stand

Edgemoor and the Lowmarch, in the years before the Ninth was his

told in full.

Piece 1The Breeder’s Son

The bay had put two men over the rails that spring and a third into the water trough, and after the third, Darrow’s father stopped using the name the horse had been sold under and called him only the bay, which in that house was a sentence.

Darrow was eleven. He had walked at a horse’s head since before he could argue, which was how a horse-breeder’s son was raised in the Edgemoor hills: a lead in the hand and a horse on the end of it, and the horse teaching the boy where his feet went. He knew the greys by their legs before he knew the neighbours by their faces. He knew that an Edgemoor grey went up a slope the way water goes down one; that the men who came up from the Lowmarch in autumn with a lance’s mark on their coats bought greys and nothing else; and that the bay was not a grey, and nobody had come for him, and nobody would. In that house a horse called by its colour went down the hill on the knacker’s cart before the month was out.

What he knew that the big boys from the lower holding did not know was where not to stand.

They came up on the first dry day to try the bay for a wager. The older one lasted a stride and a half. The younger one never got his leg over, because the bay turned his head and bit him in the thigh while he was still on the rail, and the boy went into the mud with the print of the bay’s teeth coming up blue through his breeches. Darrow’s father, leaning on the gate with his arms folded, said nothing about it, as he said nothing about most things.

“Let me,” Darrow said.

“No.”

“I’ll not get on him. I’ll only go in.”

His father looked at him the way he looked at a foal he had not decided about. Then he looked at the bay. Then he moved his chin a quarter of an inch, which was yes.

The bay came for him before he had the gate shut behind him. Darrow had watched the bay come for the others, head low and flat, and had watched them do what anyone does, which is go backward; and he had seen that the bay liked that, because backward is a straight line, and a horse owns straight lines. So Darrow did not go backward. He went sideways and a little forward, into the place the bay had just left, and the bay went past him into the fence with a bang that shook the rails. When the bay turned, the boy was not where he had been. He was at the bay’s shoulder, close in, where a horse has to swing half round to bite and can hardly kick; and by the time the bay had swung half round, he had moved again.

The bay thought about this. Darrow walked away from him, not fast, toward the middle of the paddock, and did not look back, and the bay came again, and Darrow was not there again. The third time the bay stopped a length off and blew, and the boys on the rail had gone quiet.

He had meant it, about not getting on. He only went up the rail to be higher than the bay’s eye, because a horse will listen to a thing that is higher than its eye. The bay came to the rail to see what he was, and stood there blowing, flank to the fence, close enough to touch.

Darrow had a leg over before he had thought about having a leg over.

The bay did nothing. He stood with his ears flat and his skin shivering and did nothing at all, the way a horse does when the thing has happened and there is no more sense in objecting to it. Darrow sat him with both hands in his mane and his heart going like a drum, and looked past the boys and past his father to the far field, where the long grey mare nobody had got round to yet was standing with her head up, watching all this the way the women of the house watched a fight they had no money on.

His father unfolded his arms. He did not say well done, and he did not say get down, and he did not say what the two boys on the rail were going to say in the lower holding by nightfall. He took the gate by the top bar, swung it open onto the hill, and stood aside from it.

Darrow rode the bay out through it.

Piece 2His Own

The grey filly was in the thistles when he left, at the top of the far field where the ground went to stone and the mowing stopped, with her head down and one hind foot rested. She had stood there since she could stand. He had named her Thistle for it the spring before, on the grounds that a creature who picks a place to be let alone in should be called by it, and his father had let the name go by, which was how names were made in that house.

She kept her head down. Her dam, the long grey mare under him, kept hers to the hill road, and he took the hint from both of them and kept his eyes on the road.

Darrow was sixteen. His father had come as far as the gate and stopped at it with his arms folded, as he had five years before when there was a bay on the other side of it.

“She’s yours,” his father said. “The brand’s mine. Anybody asks you, that’s the order of it.”

It was the longest thing his father had said about a horse all year. The boy carried it down the hill like a coin he meant to spend.

He heard the muster before he saw it. The hill road came out of its last loop above the market town at the bottom, where the Calden road went through, and up from the green came forty voices on the two lines they knew.

Billhook, billhook, who d’you serve?

The bell that rang and the man with the nerve.

The green was full of them: levy boys from the mills and farms with billhooks held every way a pole can be held, the bell-house clerk walking the line with a tally stick and losing count. Along the far wall a string of hill greys stood tied head to tail with breeders’ marks on their shoulders, and men in dark coats went down the string looking at teeth and feet. The men had a mark on their coats that the boy knew. He had watched it burned onto his father’s greys at the autumn fair, the day they were sold: a gate, two posts and a bar, on the near quarter, where the lances put their own.

One of the men straightened from a hock and looked across the green at the grey coming onto it, and the look went down her from ear to heel and stayed.

“How much for the mare?”

“She’s not for sale.”

“Then what’s she doing at a muster?”

“Carrying me.”

The man had a sergeant’s voice and a sergeant’s patience, and he used neither. He jerked his chin at the wall behind him: come and say that again, then, where I can see your feet.

Between the boy and the wall stood the levy. They had been told to make a line across the green and had made one, after their fashion, forty boys and forty hooks at forty heights, and every one of them turned to look as the grey came up, because she was worth looking at. When a boy turns, his hook turns with him.

The boy from Edgemoor got down. He put her head at his shoulder and his hand under her jaw and walked into the line.

He watched their shoulders, not the steel. A shoulder tells you where a hook will be a breath before the hook knows, and he stepped into each place a hook was about to leave, a half-step early, sideways and a little forward, with the mare’s long head following his hand as if the two of them had agreed it on the hill. The hooks went by behind her tail and in front of her nose. One boy came round late and hard, with the blade at the height of a horse’s eye, and found the place empty, and nearly took his neighbour’s ear.

“Mind that,” said the boy from Edgemoor, going past him.

He came out on the far side with the mare’s breath warm on his neck and every hair of her where it had been. The man in the dark coat was looking at him, and had been for some time.

“Whose son are you?”

A boy with a grey like that and the hill in his mouth was some house’s son, and a lance took its squires from houses.

“My own,” the boy said.

The sergeant looked at the mark on the mare’s shoulder, which was a horse-breeder’s and no lance’s. “Then your own man rides with another man’s brand on his horse.”

“That’s the brand. The horse is mine. Anybody asks, that’s the order of it.”

The sergeant put his hands behind his back.

“Edgemoor,” he said. “Man and horse go better uphill, they tell me, and neither of them will say why they came down.”

“No.”

“No, they won’t, or no, you won’t?”

The boy looked past him at the lancers’ own horses, picketed beyond the bell-house with a gate burned on every near quarter, and at the road going away toward Calden.

“I’ve come down,” he said.

The sergeant looked at him a while longer. Then he looked at the sky, which was going.

“The string goes down the road at dusk,” he said. “Walk her at the back of it. If she’s sound at the first stone and you’re still on your feet at the fifth, we’ll find out whose you are.”

The Ninth went out of the green at dusk, mounted, with the bought greys on a string behind them and the boy behind that, and before the last of them was off the grass they had begun to sing, all sixty, loud and badly, a thing with a horse in it whose words he had yet to learn. The levy on the green sang back at them and lost. The light went off the hill first, as it does off hills, and the road went down into a dark the colour of the mare.

He walked at her head with his hand under her jaw, past the first stone, and the second, and on toward the fifth in the dark.

Piece 3Once

The autumn he was first a knight of the Ninth, the lance went down to the horse fair to buy its greys, and the fair had a ring in it.

It was a roped square of trodden earth behind the horse lines, with a rail for leaning on and a boy to pick up the wooden swords, and by the second afternoon every lance at the fair had put a man into it against every other. The Ninth leaned on the rail and watched and lost money. Darrow leaned with them. He had been one of them for a season, long enough to know every man’s horse and most of their songs, and he was still Ser Darrow to every one of them, which is what a lance calls a man it has decided nothing about.

He wanted them to look at him the way they were looking at the ring. It was a boy’s want, and he had it anyway.

The man who came in after the noon bell was a knight of one of the Calden lances, a head taller than anyone at the rail, and he had put three men over the rope since the morning. He looked along the rail for a fourth. The look stopped on the Ninth, and the Ninth, which had money to get back, looked at Darrow.

“Go on, Edgemoor,” said the sergeant. He had bought a horse that morning and was in a good temper. “Let’s see what we fed.”

Darrow took the wooden sword from the boy.

The big man came as big men come, straight, with his weight already in the blow, because a man that size has never needed to be anywhere but in front of you. Darrow had watched a bay horse come like that when he was eleven, and had learned where not to stand. He went sideways and a little forward, into the place the big man had just left, and the blow went through the air where he had been, and the big man went through it after his blow; and when he turned, Darrow was at his shoulder, close in, where a tall man has to swing half round to reach you. He tapped him behind the knee with the wood, lightly, as you knock at a door.

The big man swung half round. Darrow had moved. He tapped him behind the other knee.

It went on like that for as long as it took the rail to understand what it was watching and begin to laugh. The big man was quick for his size and strong past anything in the ring, and all of it arrived late, because every time he got where Darrow was, Darrow had gone, and had gone toward him and not away, so that he was forever turning to find a man already behind his turn. The third time he swung at nothing. Then he stood still, blowing, and gave it up.

Darrow stepped round in front of him, where a man ought to be, and lowered the sword. The big man looked down at him for a moment. Then he laughed, hugely, and put out a hand, and the rail roared.

From the Ninth’s end of it, in the lull after the roar, the youngest of them, who had been a knight a year longer than Darrow and was a year younger, said, “Quickstep.”

He said it as a jeer, to the men beside him, half a dancer’s name and half a horse’s, and it carried farther than he meant.

Darrow heard it. He was walking back to the rail with the sword held out for the boy to take, and he heard the word arrive at his back and go past him along the rail, and he did with it what a man does with a thing he wants very badly and has been handed as a joke: he did not turn his head.

Nobody took it up. He learned why at the rail, where the sergeant stood with his arms folded and his good temper intact.

“One bout,” said the sergeant, without looking round. “He did it once. Call him what he does twice.”

They rode out of the fair at dusk with the bought greys on a string behind them and the lance singing the thing with the horse in it, and Darrow knew the words now, and sang them.

He rode Thistle. She was long in the back, with his father’s mark on her shoulder and the Ninth’s gate raw on her near quarter since noon, and she had come down the hill that week in his father’s string and stood in the horse lines with her head down, to be let alone, as she had stood in the thistles. His father sold the Ninth every grey in the string but her, and gave her to him, and said about that what he said about most things.

The word stayed in the yard. The only thing that followed him out of the fair was the singing.

Tale 2Road's the Road

The Holloway, when Rae Thorne was small enough to ride the box

1 of 3 pieces told; the rest is still to come.

Piece 1The Elbow

Her grandmother let her have the lines on the straight below the seventh milestone, where the Holloway ran so deep between its banks that the hedges met overhead and the oxen could have walked it asleep. Rae held them the way she had been shown, loose, a finger's weight on each, and felt the off ox lean into the yoke a breath before the near one, as if he had to be sure first.

She was small enough still to ride the box with her feet on the shaft, and old enough to have opinions about it. Her grandmother sat beside her with her eyes half shut and said a word to the oxen in her own tongue, not because they needed it but because, Rae had decided, oxen liked being spoken to by someone who wanted nothing from them. At every milestone there was a story. At the sixth it had been a carter who sold his bell for a drink and was run down in the fog by his own brother. At the seventh it had been a pig.

Past the eighth the road began to tip. Rae felt it in the load settling back against the tailboard, and she held the lines a little tighter so they would be harder to take.

Her grandmother took them anyway. She put out one hand, palm up, the way you hold your hand out for an egg, and Rae put the lines in it.

"I had them," Rae said.

"You had them on the straight."

"I can do the Elbow."

"Aye," said her grandmother, which in her mouth meant nothing at all, and the cart went down into the bend the carters called the Elbow, where the Holloway turned back on itself between banks higher than a man on a horse, and there was room for one cart and the shadow of one cart and nothing else.

The rider came up behind them halfway round. Rae heard him before she saw him: a horse being asked for more than the road had, and a voice doing the asking. When she twisted round he was close enough that she could see the lance's colours on his coat, the mud on his good boots, his horse's nose nearly in the tailboard.

"Mother," he called. "Draw over. I'm wanted at the landing."

There was nowhere to draw over to. Rae could see that, and could see that he could see it, and still he said it again, louder, as though the banks might step back.

"I'll go by on the bank side, then."

"You'll not," said her grandmother, without turning her head.

He tried. He put the horse at the bank where it was lowest, and the horse looked at it and decided, and came back down into the road with its ears flat. He swore at the horse, which was not the horse's fault. Then he swore at the cart.

Rae looked at her grandmother. Her grandmother was looking at the oxen's feet.

"Why won't you let him?" Rae said, low.

Her grandmother took both lines in one hand. With the other she found the ox-bell at her belt, the plain dark one that knocked at her hip all day, that Rae had stopped hearing years ago, and she lifted it and let it fall. It rang once, and the sound went back along the hollow road and up the banks and out of it.

"That says there's a cart," her grandmother said. "Anybody coming up hears it and stands. Anybody coming behind hears it and waits. That's the law down here. There's no other."

"He's a knight."

"He's behind a cart." She let the bell hang. "Road's the road."

Rae turned that over. It did not seem like enough. Then the bend went on and on, and the horse blew behind them, and it began to seem like more than enough. She reached across and put two fingers on the bell, the way she had held the lines, to feel that it was there.

The rider stayed behind them all the way down the Elbow, his horse's breath at the tailboard, and Rae did not look back at him once.

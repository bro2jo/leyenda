## 1. Prep situations, not plots: The Alexandrian (Don't Prep Plots, Three Clue Rule, node-based design, revelation lists, scenario structures)

### Takeaway
Justin Alexander's method replaces a planned sequence of events with a *situation* (places, people, agendas) plus a redundancy rule for information: every conclusion the players must reach gets at least three independent clues, and every node gets at least three leads out and three leads in. The arc then lives in a **revelation list** (what must become known) and a **node graph** (where things are), not in a scene order.

### Cited Findings
- "Don't Prep Plots" (The Alexandrian, 2009) argues GMs should prep situations rather than fixed sequences of events; the sequencing of problems is left to the players' actions. Alexander presents node-based design as the way to make that workable ("Don't Prep Plots, Prep Situations"). — [The Alexandrian, Don't Prep Plots](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots)
- **Three Clue Rule:** for any conclusion you want the players to reach, include at least three clues; three distinct options give enough redundancy that a missed or misread clue still leaves a path forward. — [The Alexandrian, Three Clue Rule (series)](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=1105)
- **Corollary, permissive clue-finding:** lean toward letting a player find a clue the GM never prepared when the investigative action logically justifies it; these unprepared finds are "dynamic clues". Understanding the underlying situation comes first, then the GM welcomes unexpected approaches. — [The Alexandrian, Three Clue Rule Part 5: More Corollaries](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1109/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule-part-5-more-corollaries)
- **Corollary, proactive clues:** clues that come to the PCs instead of waiting to be found (Raymond Chandler's "a guy with a gun walks through the door"); used when players hit a dead end by misreading or ignoring clues; the event should hand them a new clue, preferably several. — [The Alexandrian, Three Clue Rule Part 5](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1109/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule-part-5-more-corollaries)
- **Corollary, red herrings are overrated:** not banned, but to be used with extreme caution; getting players to the intended deduction is already hard and players generate their own false suspicions. — [The Alexandrian, Three Clue Rule (series)](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=1105)
- Related cautions: the "fallacy of the perfect crime" (GMs wrongly assume the culprit destroyed every clue), and clues that are too similar give only superficial redundancy. — [The Alexandrian, The Fallacy of the Perfect Crime](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=46340); [The Alexandrian, Random GM Tips: Making Clues](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46338/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tips-making-clues)
- **Inverted Three Clue Rule** (the basis of node-based design): if the PCs have access to *any* three clues, they will reach at least one conclusion. This is about access, not guaranteed discovery; he assumes players miss some. — [The Alexandrian, node-based design ("The Three Clue Rule states:")](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=7985)
- **Node structure:** each scenario element is a node; clues link nodes. If every node contains at least three clues and has at least three clues pointing to it, both the rule and its inverse hold and flow through that layer is robust; a lead that dead-ends still leaves other clues to follow. — [The Alexandrian, Node-Based Scenario Design](https://thealexandrian.net/creations/misc/node-design/node-design.html)
- **Node types:** five — locations, characters, organizations, events, activities (listed in Part 9 of the series). — [The Alexandrian, The Secret Life of Nodes Part 2](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/45268/roleplaying-games/the-secret-life-of-nodes-part-2-node-based-campaigns)
- **5-Node Mystery template:** an initial node, three locations/people, and a finale; "You've got five nodes. Connect 'em with clues." Alexander's default wiring (given in a comment): A points to B, C, D; B points to C, D, E; C points to B, D, E; D points to B, C, E. — [The Alexandrian, 5 Node Mystery (short link returned by search)](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=37903)
- **From scenario to campaign:** design campaigns by first brainstorming the major scenarios (many themselves node-based); build five 5-Node Mysteries and wire *them* in the same clue pattern (Mystery A points to B, C, D ...); "seasoning your scenario with clues pointing to other scenarios" is the way to grow interwoven campaigns. Plan at a macro level, then develop each macro-node as its own node structure. — [The Alexandrian, The Secret Life of Nodes Part 2: Node-Based Campaigns](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/45268/roleplaying-games/the-secret-life-of-nodes-part-2-node-based-campaigns)
- **Fractal nodes:** a node can be zoomed into and broken into sub-nodes; large networks are grouped (e.g. by city) and each group given a simple template. Some nodes carry only "move on" clues and none toward the deeper mystery. — [The Alexandrian, The Secret Life of Nodes Part 3: Fractal Nodes](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/45272/roleplaying-games/the-secret-life-of-nodes-part-3-fractal-nodes); [Advanced Node-Based Design Part 5: The Two Prongs of Mystery Design](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/8202/roleplaying-games/advanced-node-based-design-part-5-the-two-prongs-of-mystery-design)
- **Revelation list:** the list of conclusions the scenario's clues point toward; each conclusion has its clues listed beneath it (the per-clue breakdown is a "clue list"); it can also be built by working backwards from the scenario. — [The Alexandrian, Three Clue Rule tag archive](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/tag/three-clue-rule/page/2)
- ***So You Want To Be a Game Master* (2023)** organises GM craft around **scenario structures** ("what you prep and how you use that prep to run the adventure"): dungeons, mysteries, raids, heists, urbancrawls, faction-based adventures, wilderness exploration, node-based campaigns. — [Thaumavore interview/review](https://thaumavore.substack.com/p/rpg-superbrain-justin-alexander-finally); [Wikipedia: Justin Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Alexander)
- Community objection (forum opinion, not Alexander's position): if every node funnels to the same ending, three-clue design can itself be a soft plot; the counter-view is that all three ideas only aim to remove brick walls. — [EN World, "The Alexandrian's Insights In a Nutshell"](https://www.enworld.org/threads/the-alexandrian%E2%80%99s-insights-in-a-nutshell.703060/post-9285913)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) Data shape.** The technique is already a graph plus a checklist, so it encodes directly:
  ```yaml
  revelations:            # "what must become known for the arc to move"
    - id: R2
      conclusion: "one sentence the player should be able to reach"
      needed_for: [bottleneck B3]          # why it matters to the arc
      clues:                               # lint: >= 3, different kinds
        - {id: R2a, at: N1, kind: physical,  text: "...", seen_in: null}
        - {id: R2b, at: N3, kind: testimony, text: "...", seen_in: ch04-d2}
        - {id: R2c, at: null, kind: proactive, fire_when: "player stalls 2 blocks", seen_in: null}
      status: unreached | suspected | concluded      # set from what the page shows, never assumed
  nodes:
    - id: N3
      kind: location | character | organization | event | activity
      situation: "who is here, what they want, what is true here"   # not a scene
      leads_out: [N1, N4, N5]              # lint: >= 3
      leads_in:  [N1, N2, N4]              # lint: >= 3 (computed)
      state: unvisited | visited | changed
  ```
- **(Inference) Instruction to the narrator.** "Before a block, read the open revelations and their unseen clues. Place a clue only where the fiction makes it findable; if the player does something sensible you did not plan for, invent a dynamic clue for an open revelation and record it. If a needed revelation has one or zero unseen clues left, or the player has stalled, fire its proactive clue. Never state the conclusion for the player; record `concluded` only when he says or acts on it." The record step after the block (which clue ids were shown) is what keeps the three-clue redundancy auditable by code instead of memory.
- **(Inference) Lint is cheap.** "every revelation has >= 3 clues of different kinds", "every node has >= 3 in and out", "no revelation needed for the next bottleneck has < 2 unseen clues" are mechanical checks an engine can run at chapter-planning time; this is the single most code-friendly technique in this document.
- **(Inference)** The 5 x 5 pattern gives a campaign skeleton for a serial: five arcs of five nodes, each arc seeded with leads into two or three others, so the order of arcs is the player's.

### Gaps
- The full text of "Don't Prep Plots" was not opened; its internal checklist (e.g. the tools he lists for running a situation) is not reproduced here.
- The "5+5 dungeon" recipe from the 2023 book could not be verified; only a third-party gist names it. — [gist mentioning it](https://gist.github.com/StevenACoffman/13bcf675c9815676a1ed18adfab844a4)

- No report was found of an LLM GM explicitly using a revelation list or node graph with seen-flags; the LLM projects found (section 8) track quests, NPCs and clocks, not clue redundancy.

## 2. Threat and pressure mechanics: fronts, grim portents, threat maps, countdown and progress clocks, the faction game

### Takeaway
Fronts and clocks give a campaign an arc that advances *by default*: each threat has a stated end state and a short ordered list (or a segmented clock) of visible steps toward it, and the GM advances one whenever the players are elsewhere or time passes. The player's agency is the power to interrupt a sequence that is otherwise already written; nothing about the player's own future scenes is written.

### Cited Findings
**Dungeon World fronts (Sage LaTorra and Adam Koebel, 2012)**
- Fronts are "secret tomes of GM knowledge", built outside active play to organise what opposes the players. Checklist: choose an impending doom for each danger; add grim portents (**1–3 for an adventure front, 3–5 for the campaign front**); write **1–3 stakes questions**; list the front's general cast. A campaign front spans sessions on a slower burn with bigger consequences. — [Roll20 Dungeon World compendium: Fronts](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts)
- Grim portents are the visible steps a danger takes toward its doom: "a single interesting event or a chain of steps"; many have a logical order, some are unconnected early signs; a portent can simply be "a change in direction for a danger". When unsure what happens next, "push your danger towards resolving a grim portent". — [A Dungeon World wiki: Fronts](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Fronts)
- Impending doom is where the danger ends if nobody stops it; named types include Tyranny, Pestilence, Destruction (partial list confirmed). Dooms should be "sinister enough to get your players' attention but not so dire that they have no other choice but tackle it." Danger categories include Ambitious Organizations, Planar Forces, Arcane Enemies (partial list confirmed). — [A Dungeon World wiki: Fronts](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Fronts); [Dungeon World Tavern archive: "Let's Talk About Dangers"](https://dungeonworld.gplusarchive.online/2017/01/15/lets-talk-about-dangers)
- Stakes questions are "1–3 questions about people, places, or groups that you're interested in"; a good one is such that "when it's resolved, means that things will never be the same again." — [Roll20 Dungeon World compendium: Fronts](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts); [Dungeon World Tavern: "Talk to me about stakes"](https://dungeonworld.gplusarchive.online/2017/11/10/talk-to-me-about-stakes-those-questions-you-write-as-part-of-your-fronts)
- Practitioner advice (forum): "Write the Impending Doom as if there were no PCs in the universe"; each portent should be a specific change in the world (the deal struck), not a vague one (villagers go missing). Whether portents may fire offscreen unannounced is debated and unsettled in the sources. — [Dungeon World Tavern archive: fronts](https://dungeonworld.gplusarchive.online/tag/fronts)
- Sly Flourish has two articles adapting and later reassessing fronts for D&D. — [Sly Flourish: Fronts in D&D](https://slyflourish.com/fronts_in_dnd.html); [Sly Flourish: Looking Back on Fronts](https://slyflourish.com/looking_back_on_fronts.html)

**Apocalypse World (Vincent and Meguey Baker; 1st ed. 2010, 2nd ed. 2016)**
- The **threat map** is the one map the game requires: PCs (and their resources) in the inner circle, threats plotted around them, with an "outside" position for threats from the psychic maelstrom or elsewhere. It is the spatial tool; the **countdown clock** is the temporal tool for the same threats. — [The Daily Apocalypse: Threat maps](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/83-threat-maps); [Troy Press: Threat maps as campaign prep](https://troypress.com/threat-maps-as-campaign-prep)
- Each threat is a half-sheet: type, impulse (drive), description, cast, what is at stake, and optionally a custom move or a countdown clock. Second edition has 7 threat categories (Warlord, Grotesque, Affliction named in the snippet). Between sessions the MC is told to take out the threat map and ask whether any threat has moved closer. — [The Daily Apocalypse: Threat categories and lists](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/74-threat-categories-and-lists); [The Daily Apocalypse: Threat maps](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/83-threat-maps)
- The countdown clock is "a reminder to you as MC that your threats have impulse, direction, plans, intentions"; it lets the MC "put down a bid on future events without scripting or mandating those future events." Clocks are "both descriptive and prescriptive": descriptively, "when something you've listed happens, advance the clock to that point." The face is three quarter-hour segments followed by three five-minute segments (six segments, accelerating). — [The Daily Apocalypse: Threat countdown clocks](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/85-threat-countdown-clocks); [The Gauntlet archive: "Locked, Clocked and Ready to Rock"](https://gauntlet.gplusarchive.online/?p=27616)
- A countdown shows what happens if the thing proceeds unchecked; past the 9 o'clock mark (given as "21:00") the outcome "cannot be reversed, it can only be mitigated." — [The Gauntlet archive: "Locked, Clocked and Ready to Rock"](https://gauntlet.gplusarchive.online/?p=27616)

**Blades in the Dark (John Harper, 2017)**
- Clock types confirmed: **danger clocks** (a growing threat such as alert level; when full, the danger comes to fruition), **racing clocks** (two opposed clocks), **tug-of-war clocks** (one clock filled and emptied by events, e.g. a turf war), **faction clocks**. Clocks come in 4, 6 or 8 segments. — [Roll20 Blades in the Dark compendium: Progress clocks](https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Rules:Progress%20clocks); [CBR: Blades in the Dark's clock system](https://www.cbr.com/blades-in-the-dark-clocks-system-ttrpg/)
- **Faction clocks:** each faction has a long-term goal; "when the PCs have downtime, the GM ticks forward the faction clocks that they're interested in"; the PCs "may also directly affect NPC faction clocks, based on the missions and scores they pull off." — [Roll20 Blades in the Dark compendium: Progress clocks](https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Rules:Progress%20clocks)
- **Faction game:** status with each faction runs −3 to +3, 0 the neutral default; an operation gives −1 or −2 status with factions it hurts; at −3 the crew is at war and temporarily loses 1 hold. — [Roll20 Blades in the Dark compendium: The Faction Game](https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/The%20Faction%20Game)
- Practitioner variants: two racing clocks instead of one tug-of-war clock to avoid stalemate; status tracked as clocks that get bigger the more extreme the status ("quick to judge and slow to change their minds"). Both are house rules. — [Blades in the Dark G+ archive](https://bitd.gplusarchive.online/?p=189278); [Blades community forum: Faction Tier Changes](https://community.bladesinthedark.com/t/faction-tier-changes/855)

**Kevin Crawford's faction turn (Stars Without Number 2010/2017; Worlds Without Number 2021)**
- Worlds Without Number factions carry six statistics (Cunning, Force, Wealth, Magic, Treasure, hit points), own assets tied to Cunning, Force or Wealth, and each has a goal; pitting assets against each other in contested attribute checks during the faction turn decides whether goals advance. — [Glyph & Grok: Creating factions in an open world](https://glyphngrok.substack.com/p/creating-factions-in-an-open-world)
- Fan-written procedure summary: each faction acts once per faction phase, the first to act chosen by die roll; a faction can hold no more assets of a type than its rating in that stat. (Secondhand; not the rulebook text.) — [GM Binder fan guide: Faction Phase](https://www.gmbinder.com/share/-LHLV_1F1lmVHW4UQBKc)
- The faction turn is used in *solo* play to make the world move between sessions (a Dolmenwood solo campaign that runs faction turns as interludes). — [Solo TTRPG play report: Faction Turns](https://solottrpg.substack.com/p/faction-turns-dolmenwood-play-reports)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) Why it produces an arc without a plot.** A front is a pre-written *default future*: doom + ordered portents. The story that actually happens is the difference between that default and what the player did. Because only the antagonists' side is scripted, the technique satisfies "don't prep plots" while still guaranteeing escalation, a midpoint and a deadline. Apocalypse World's "bid on future events without scripting" is the cleanest statement of this.
- **(Inference) Data shape.**
  ```yaml
  fronts:
    - id: F1
      scope: campaign | book | chapter           # campaign: 3-5 portents; chapter: 1-3
      stakes: ["Will <person> hold the pass?", "Does <place> stay free?"]   # 1-3; the GM does NOT pre-answer
      cast: [C4, C9]
      dangers:
        - id: D1
          impulse: "what it does when nobody stops it"
          doom: "what is true of the world if every portent lands"
          portents:                                # ordered
            - {n: 1, text: "a specific visible change", status: landed, shown_in: ch03-d5}
            - {n: 2, text: "...", status: pending}
            - {n: 3, text: "...", status: pending}
          clock: {segments: 6, filled: 2, irreversible_from: 4}   # AW: late segments can only be mitigated
          last_opposed_in: ch04
  ```
- **(Inference) Tick rules belong to the engine, wording to the narrator.** A rule such as "at chapter close, every danger not opposed on the page this chapter advances one portent; a danger opposed successfully does not; a missed day/failed roll may tick one segment" is arithmetic and should be computed, with the brief then telling the narrator "portent D1.2 landed this week: show it or its symptom in this block". This mirrors Blades (GM ticks faction clocks in downtime) and matches the one concrete LLM practice found (threat clocks advanced by a script; section 8).
- **(Inference) Fairness constraint worth encoding.** From the Dungeon World advice that dooms must be visible and portents specific: "a doom may not land unless at least N of its portents were *shown on the page*" (the record carries `shown_in`). That converts "the world moves offscreen" into something the reader could have seen coming, and it is checkable.
- **(Inference) Stakes questions are the anti-railroad field.** They are explicitly things the GM is curious about and has not decided; storing them as open questions (with `answered_in`) gives the planner a list of outcomes it is forbidden to pre-write.
- **(Inference) Threat map for a serial.** A two-axis position per threat (near/far from the protagonist; which direction) is a compact way to tell the narrator which threats may appear in *this* block (near ones) and which may only be heard of (far ones).

### Gaps

Dooms: Tyranny, Pestilence, Destruction, Usurpation, Impoverishment, Rampant Chaos. Fronts are made after the first session. ## 3. Low-prep frameworks: the Lazy DM's eight steps (secrets and clues), the Five Room Dungeon, Crawford's sandbox tools

### Takeaway
Mike Shea's eight-step checklist is a per-session prep loop whose key device is **ten one-sentence secrets written with no location or delivery attached**, improvised into play when the moment fits; about half get used and the rest are discarded or carried. The Five Room Dungeon is a five-beat shape for any small arc. Both are small enough to be regenerated every chapter.

### Cited Findings
**Sly Flourish (Mike Shea), *Return of the Lazy Dungeon Master* (2018; steps restated 2023)**
- The eight steps: Review the Characters; Create a Strong Start; Outline Potential Scenes; Define Secrets and Clues; Develop Fantastic Locations; Outline Important NPCs; Choose Relevant Monsters; Select Magic Item Rewards (renamed "Select Rewards" in the expanded book; "the eight steps of Lazy GM Prep haven't changed"). — [Sly Flourish: The Eight Steps (2023)](https://slyflourish.com/eight_steps_2023.html)
- Step 1 exists to remember "who they are, what they want, and what connections they have to the world", and to ask what hook could draw each character into the next session. — [Sly Flourish: The Eight Steps (2023)](https://slyflourish.com/eight_steps_2023.html); [Sly Flourish: Step Zero, Reviewing PCs](https://slyflourish.com/step_zero_reviewing_pcs.html)
- "Outline Potential Scenes" is the catch-all ("the junk drawer of the eight steps (in a good way)"). — [Sly Flourish: Scenes, the Catch-all Step](https://slyflourish.com/scenes_catch_all_step.html)
- The steps are modular: GMs "often cut steps out depending on the type of game"; "None of the eight steps are mandatory"; if only one is kept, secrets and clues "may be the main one you want to include." — [Sly Flourish: Choosing the Right Steps](https://slyflourish.com/choosing_the_right_steps.html); [Sly Flourish: The Eight Steps (2023)](https://slyflourish.com/eight_steps_2023.html)
- **Secrets and clues, the definition:** ten things the characters may discover in the next game, "independent of where they might find them"; the prep question is "What secrets could the PCs uncover next session?", answered with ten tweet-sized secrets. Each is a single short sentence describing a clue, a piece of the story or a piece of the world. — [Sly Flourish: Sharing Secrets](https://slyflourish.com/sharing_secrets.html); [Sly Flourish: Lists of Ten](https://slyflourish.com/lists_of_ten.html); [Goodreads reader notes on the book](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/41729343-return-of-the-lazy-dungeon-master/55197536-matcha-mellow/c36b482a-ae78-4efc-9cdf-aa5e11766879)
- **Delivery is improvised:** "How we reveal these secrets is the part of actual improvisation. We wait for the right moment to reveal the right secret." — [Sly Flourish: Revealing Secrets](https://slyflourish.com/revealing_secrets.html)
- **Lifecycle:** Shea finds about half of the ten are revealed in a session; unrevealed secrets are thrown away and a fresh list written each time, though still-relevant unused ones may be reviewed and carried forward. — [Sly Flourish: Throwing Away Secrets](https://slyflourish.com/throwing_away_secrets.html); [Sly Flourish: Reusing Secrets and Clues](https://slyflourish.com/reusing_secrets_and_clues.html)
- The method suits mysteries because whichever path the characters take they can still uncover what they need; the eight steps contain no "adventure hook" step, and the secrets list is where hooks go; players can also supply secrets. — [Sly Flourish: Managing Mysteries and Plot Twists](https://slyflourish.com/managing_mysteries_and_plot_twists.html); [Sly Flourish: Types of Secrets](https://slyflourish.com/types_of_secrets.html); [Sly Flourish: Player-Generated Secrets](https://slyflourish.com/player-generated_secrets.html)
- The Lazy GM's Resource Document (the openly licensed reference text of the method) exists at the URL below; not opened this session. — [Sly Flourish: The Lazy GM's Resource Document](https://slyflourish.com/lazy_gm_resource_document.html)

**Five Room Dungeon (Johnn Four, Roleplaying Tips, 2006)**
- Five beats: (1) Entrance and Guardian; (2) Puzzle or Roleplaying Challenge; (3) Trick or Setback; (4) Climax / Big Battle; (5) Reward, Revelation or Plot Twist. The order may change. — [Scabard: 5 Room Dungeon](https://www.scabard.com/features/5-room-dungeon); [Strolen's Citadel: 5 Room Dungeon](https://strolen.com/viewing/4276)
- Design notes per room: the guardian explains why the place has not been looted already and starts with action; room two should spotlight different characters than room one and not be solved by combat; room five is often what makes it memorable and need not be a place (it can be a twist revealed in room four); the shape works for towns, ships and other non-dungeons. — [Sean McP: Five-room dungeons](https://seanmcp-com.pages.dev/adventure-games/five-room-dungeons); [Scabard: 5 Room Dungeon](https://www.scabard.com/features/5-room-dungeon)

**Crawford's sandbox tools:** see the faction turn in section 2.

### Inferences
- **(Inference) Secrets are the right granularity for an LLM brief.** A secret is one sentence, self-contained, and location-free, which is exactly what survives being injected into a prompt without dictating a scene. Suggested record:
  ```yaml
  secrets:
    - id: S14
      text: "one sentence that would be true whether or not anyone learns it"
      serves: [R2, F1.D1, promise P7]      # which revelation / front / promise it advances
      weight: must | should | colour       # must = needed before the next bottleneck
      status: unrevealed | revealed | retired
      revealed_in: null
      carry: true                          # false = drop at chapter close if unused
  ```
  Instruction: "This block's brief lists up to ten unrevealed secrets. Reveal one only if the scene naturally offers a way (a person who would know, a thing that would show it). Do not bend the scene to deliver it. After the block, record which ids the page actually revealed." At chapter close the engine retires `colour` secrets, carries `must` ones, and the planner writes a fresh ten.
- **(Inference)** "Untethered from location" is what makes the list railroad-proof: the planner commits to *what can be learned*, never *where the player must go*. It pairs with the revelation list of section 1 (a `must` secret is a clue for a revelation).
- **(Inference)** The eight steps map onto a chapter-planning template: review the protagonist's current drives and open choices (step 1); a strong start for the week (step 2); potential scenes as *options*, never an order (step 3); secrets (step 4); two or three places with one striking feature each (step 5); who is present and what each wants (step 6); the opposition (step 7); what can be gained (step 8).
- **(Inference)** The Five Room shape is a ready-made weekly chapter: seven daily slots can carry five beats plus two breathers; "Reward or Revelation" being a twist is the natural home for a chapter-end choice.

### Gaps
- The exact rule text for "Define Secrets and Clues" in the book/resource document was not opened; the "ten" count and "independent of where they might find them" come from Shea's own articles via search snippets.
- Shea's campaign-level tools (campaign pitch, "six truths", three campaign fronts, spiral campaign development) could not be retrieved; the one search for them returned unrelated results. **[recall, unverified]:** he recommends a one-page campaign pitch with six truths about the world, three fronts each with a goal and three grim portents, and building the world outward from the characters' starting location ("spiral campaign development").
- Johnn Four's original 2006 article was not opened; room names vary slightly between the secondary sources.

## 4. Long-campaign shape: sandbox versus arc, branching that reconverges, storylets and quality-based narrative, drama managers

### Takeaway
The interactive-fiction literature gives precise names for the shapes a long branching story can take. The one that fits "an overall arc that still responds to choices" is **branch-and-bottleneck with heavy state tracking**: paths diverge, rejoin at fixed bottlenecks, and differ afterwards because of recorded state. **Storylets** (content + prerequisites + effects, selected by state or by salience) are the modular alternative, and a **drama manager** is the component that picks the next unit against an authored tension curve.

### Cited Findings
**West Marches (Ben Robbins, played early 2000s, written up 2007)**
- Three founding rules: no regular time (players schedule sessions), no regular party (a pool of roughly 10–14 players), no regular plot ("No overarching plot, just an overarching environment"). — [RPG Museum: West Marches](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/West_Marches)
- Sources disagree on whether it is a hexcrawl: some describe it as episodic hexcrawl exploration, others stress the original used hexless, vector-based wilderness travel. Hybrids exist (a West-Marches-style game with an overarching plot that gradually revealed itself). — [ASR: Dungeon Crawling, To The West](https://asr.bearblog.dev/dungeon-crawling-to-the-west/); [Cannibal Halfling: Intro to West Marches](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/?p=7401)

**Sam Kabo Ashwell, "Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games" (26 January 2015)**
- The patterns overlap rather than forming discrete classes. — [IFWiki copy of the article](https://www.ifwiki.org/Standard_Patterns_in_Choice-Based_Games); [original post](https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/)
- **Time Cave:** heavily branching, no state tracking, all choices of equal weight. **Gauntlet:** a nearly linear central thread with rare state tracking. — [Narrative design course summary of Ashwell](https://samsheffield.github.io/Narrative_Design/week4.html)
- **Branch and Bottleneck:** branches repeatedly rejoin; such games are "strongly shaped by the passage of time" while keeping a reasonable sense of player control; *Long Live the Queen* is the example and Choice of Games uses it as its guiding principle. — [IFWiki copy](https://www.ifwiki.org/Standard_Patterns_in_Choice-Based_Games)
- **Quest:** distinct branches that tend to rejoin toward a small number of winning endings (often one). **Sorting Hat:** the early game branches heavily and bottlenecks, and that early phase decides which of several fairly linear major branches the player gets; it leans on early state tracking and is a compromise between breadth and depth. **Loop and Grow:** a central thread loops to the same point over and over, with state tracking unlocking new options each pass. **Floating Modules:** no trunk; encounters become available based on state or at random; only feasible on a computer; tied to Alexis Kennedy's "quality-based narrative". — [IFWiki copy](https://www.ifwiki.org/Standard_Patterns_in_Choice-Based_Games); [Emily Short: Small-Scale Structures in CYOA](https://emshort.blog/2016/11/05/small-scale-structures-in-cyoa/)

**Foldback and string of pearls**
- Chris Crawford ("Flawed Methods for Interactive Storytelling") describes **foldback** as a patch on the branching tree, calls it a trick amounting to fraudulent interactivity (though "not without value"), and notes that a binary tree without foldback needs 2^N − 1 nodes for N choices; elsewhere he says every foldback "renders some decision meaningless." — [Crawford: Flawed Methods for Interactive Storytelling](https://erasmatazz.com/library/the-journal-of-computer/jcgd-volume-7/flawed-methods-for-interact.html); [Crawford: A Solution to Interactive Storytelling](https://erasmatazz.com/library/a-solution-to-interactive/a-solution-to-interactive.html)
- Linear game narrative is called "string of pearls" (a phrase attributed to Jane Jensen): nodes of local freedom joined by fixed segments. — [SF Encyclopedia: Interactive Narrative](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/interactive_narrative)
- Practitioners answer Crawford's objection with tracked state: paths diverge and rejoin, but variables (score, reputation, skill, items, elapsed time) make the rejoined story differ; every added variable raises content cost and must be budgeted. — [Blooloop: Multi-branching narrative](https://blooloop.com/theme-park/opinion/multi-branching-narrative/)

**Storylets and quality-based narrative (Failbetter; Emily Short)**
- A storylet is a unit of content with prerequisites that decide when it is available and effects on world state after it plays (Short, "Storylets: You Want Them", 29 November 2019). — [Emily Short: Storylets: You Want Them](https://emshort.blog/2019/11/29/storylets-you-want-them/)
- **Quality-based narrative (QBN)** is Failbetter's term for narratives built from storylets unlocked by qualities; Fallen London is the archetype; Failbetter later dropped the name, and Alexis Kennedy prefers "resource narrative". Prerequisites are usually a list of quality ranges the player's qualities must fall into; results change qualities. — [Emily Short: Beyond Branching (12 April 2016)](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/); [Emily Short: Mailbag, High-Agency Narrative Systems](https://emshort.blog/2017/05/25/mailbag-high-agency-narrative-systems/)
- **Salience-based narrative** is Short's own term: the system picks, from a large pool, the piece of content judged most applicable right now. The difference: in QBN the *player* chooses among currently legal storylets; in a salience system the *system* chooses. — [Emily Short: Beyond Branching](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)
- Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin's 2018 survey of the storylet design space analyses systems along four dimensions, including how preconditions are defined and whether storylets can repeat. — [Simple QBN notes citing the survey](https://videlais.github.io/simple-qbn/qbn)

**Drama managers and experience management**
- In *Façade* (Mateas and Stern, 2005) the drama manager is the top-level sequencer of **beats**; a beat is 10–100 joint dialogue behaviours, only one beat is active at a time, and each beat carries the author's estimate of how much tension it adds. The manager chooses beats so that the tension the player experiences follows an author-defined curve over time. — [Mateas and Stern, Façade paper (TIDSE 2003)](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~michaelm/tenurereview/publications/mateas-tidse2003.pdf); [Riedl and Bulitko 2013](https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/riedl2013interactive.pdf)
- Riedl and Bulitko, "Interactive Narrative: An Intelligent Systems Approach" (AI Magazine, 2013): the central problem is balancing coherent story progression with user agency; a drama manager is "an intelligent, omniscient, and disembodied agent that monitors the virtual world and intervenes to drive the narrative forward according to some model of quality of experience." "Experience management" (Riedl et al., 2008) is the broader term. — [Riedl and Bulitko 2013](https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/riedl2013interactive.pdf); [Riedl et al. 2008](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/riedl2008dynamic.pdf)

**LLM-era versions of these structures**
- **Drama Llama** (arXiv 2501.09099, January 2025; Sun, Wang, Chung, Roemmele, Kim, Kreminski): storylets whose triggers are written in natural language instead of coded preconditions; a minimal LLM drama manager checks after every message which storylets should fire; preliminary study with six experienced interactive-fiction authors. — [arXiv 2501.09099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09099)
- **Dramamancer** (UIST 2025 demo; design paper arXiv 2601.18785, Wordplay workshop at EMNLP 2025): the LLM turns an author-written story schema into a player-driven playthrough; storylets are precondition, content, effect; an event has a condition the model must detect (e.g. "the player asks about the hidden key"); effects can bring characters on or off, or change the scene; a scene moves to the next if a transition is specified, otherwise the playthrough ends; two LLM modules orchestrate a playthrough. — [arXiv 2601.18785](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18785); [ACM DL: Dramamancer](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3746058.3758995)
- **Narrative Chain** ("From Role-Play to Drama-Interaction", arXiv 2405.14231, 2024): the plot is divided into smaller consecutive segments so the model can balance player agency against scripted progression. — [arXiv 2405.14231](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14231)
- An open-source LLM GM framework tracks a three-act structure in its `state.md`. — [Bobby-Gray/open-tabletop-gm](https://github.com/Bobby-Gray/open-tabletop-gm)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) "Roads" are branch-and-bottleneck with marks.** Plan the arc as a short list of bottlenecks (things that will be true whichever way he went) and, between each pair, two or three roads. Crawford's objection (foldback makes the choice meaningless) is met only if each road *leaves marks*, i.e. sets state that later content must read:
  ```yaml
  bottlenecks:
    - id: B3
      must_be_true: ["the company reaches the far side of the river"]     # outcome, not a scene
      window: {earliest: ch09, latest: ch12}
      varies_by: [who_is_with_him, what_it_cost, who_knows]                # state the scene must read
  roads:
    - id: road.ford
      from: B2
      to: B3
      opened_by: choice.ch08.k2
      owns: [N7, N8, storylet.s31]                 # content only this road has
      leaves: {debt_to: C5, lost: item.x, front_tick: F1.D2}   # marks carried past the bottleneck
      status: open | taken | closed
  ```
  Instruction: "A bottleneck fixes *what* becomes true, never *how*; write it from the marks the road left. If no mark from the road taken appears in the bottleneck block, the choice was cosmetic; add one."
- **(Inference) Storylets suit everything that is not on the spine.** Companion scenes, callbacks, consequences and side matter can live as storylets with prerequisites over the engine's state; the engine filters to the legal set and ranks by salience; the narrator is given the top few as *offers*, not orders. This is precisely Short's salience model with an LLM as the content realiser, and it is what Drama Llama and Dramamancer implement.
  ```yaml
  storylets:
    - id: st.oath_recalled
      requires: {promise.P7: open, approval.C3: ">=20", chapter: ">=6", not_played: true}
      salience: 0.8                # or a rule: rises each chapter it stays eligible
      brief: "two lines on what this scene is for"
      effects: {promise.P7: progressing, approval.C3: "+engine"}
      repeatable: false
  ```
- **(Inference) A drama manager is a planner prompt plus a curve.** Store a target tension value per slot of the week/arc (Façade's authored curve) and tag each candidate block with an estimated tension contribution; the planner picks to track the curve. For an LLM this can be as light as "today's target: low / rising / peak / release".
- **(Inference) Natural-language triggers need a deterministic backstop.** Drama Llama's reported authoring burden around trigger consistency suggests that when a trigger matters to the arc, the condition should be recorded as an explicit flag in the block's record (set by the narrator, validated by the engine) rather than re-judged from prose each time.
- **(Inference)** Sorting Hat is the honest name for a campaign whose first act decides which of a few later tracks is played; if that is the design, the sorting choices deserve explicit marking in the plan.

### Gaps
- Ashwell's **Open Map** was summarised inconsistently by the search; **[recall, unverified]:** it is the Quest pattern with reversible travel between major nodes, giving a static geography the player can wander indefinitely. His exact wording that Branch and Bottleneck "relies heavily on state-tracking" was not confirmed verbatim.
- Short's "Beyond Branching" also covers **waypoint narrative**; its definition was not retrieved. Her list of storylet design patterns (gating by progress qualities, menaces, etc.) was not retrieved.
- Façade's beat precondition scheme (how beat selection weighs preconditions, priority and tension fit) was not confirmed in detail.
- Drama Llama's and Dramamancer's actual study findings were not available beyond one third-party summary (authors reported cognitive burden managing trigger consistency and flow). — [third-party review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/drama-llama-an-llm-powered-storylets-framework-for-authorable-responsiveness-in-interactive-narrative)
- No sourced material was gathered on tiered or act structures for tabletop campaigns (e.g. tiers of play, the 5x5 campaign method) or on hexcrawl procedure; I found no reliable sources on these in the searches run.
- Ben Robbins' own West Marches posts were not opened.

## 5. Scene- and beat-level pacing that feeds the arc: Hamlet's Hit Points, DramaSystem, scene and sequel, try-fail, story circle, TV structure, ICI

### Takeaway
These tools classify what a single scene *does* (which kind of beat, whether it moves the audience toward hope or fear, who wants what from whom) and prescribe alternation. They give a narrator a vocabulary for tagging each block and a planner a way to detect monotony, which is the main long-run pacing failure.

### Cited Findings
**Robin D. Laws, *Hamlet's Hit Points* (Gameplaywright, 2010)**
- The book sets out a beat-analysis system with **nine beat types** and maps a story's movement between **hope and fear** with up and down arrows; it works through *Hamlet*, *Dr. No* and *Casablanca*. The icons and arrows are Creative Commons licensed. — [Gameplaywright: Hamlet's Hit Points](https://gameplaywright.net/books/hamlets-hit-points/); [TV Tropes: Hamlet's Hit Points](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Literature/HamletsHitPoints)
- The two foundational types: the **procedural** beat (the protagonist pursues a practical, external goal against an obstacle) and the **dramatic** beat (a character seeks an emotional concession from another; inner goals). **Commentary** pauses the action to bring out theme. Further types named in the sources: **anticipation**, **gratification**, **reveal**, and **bringdown** (a free-floating moment of sadness). — [Gnome Stew review](https://gnomestew.com/review-hamlets-hit-points/); [Emily Short on Hamlet's Hit Points (7 January 2020)](https://emshort.blog/2020/01/07/hamlets-hit-points-robin-d-laws/); [Narrative design course: Beat types](https://samsheffield.github.io/Narrative_Design/assets/documents/beat-types.html)
- Reception: praised for showing how classics pace their rises and falls; one review calls the system over-complicated. — [Graph Paper Games review](https://graphpapergames.blogspot.com/2016/01/hamlets-hit-points-rpg-book-review.html)

**DramaSystem / *Hillfolk* (Robin D. Laws, Pelgrane Press, 2013)**
- A **dramatic scene** is a verbal contest over an emotional reward: the **petitioner** seeks it, the **granter** gives or withholds. **Drama tokens** balance the two: if the granter concedes, the granter earns a token (from the petitioner, or from the kitty if the petitioner has none); if the granter refuses, the petitioner gains the token. — [Roll20 DramaSystem compendium: Dramatic Scenes](https://roll20.net/compendium/dramasystem/Dramatic%20Scenes)
- A petitioner may spend **two** tokens to force a significant concession; the granter may block by spending **three**, which go to the petitioner. Players call scenes in turn, cast them, and the caller is usually the petitioner; the GM may cast any scene with at least one PC. Scenes that are not a contest are no-contest or expository; practical matters are **procedural scenes**. — [Roll20 DramaSystem compendium: Dramatic Scenes](https://roll20.net/compendium/dramasystem/Dramatic%20Scenes); [Pelgrane Press: Introducing DramaSystem](https://pelgranepress.com/site/?p=6317)
- A fan observation relevant to pacing: in a dramatic game, failure in procedural matters is more interesting than success. — [The Gauntlet archive: DramaSystem discussion](https://gauntlet-archive.github.io/t/drama-system-what-works-well-with-it-and-what-doesnt-and-other-considerations/2634.html)

**Scene and sequel (Dwight V. Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer*, 1965)**
- A **scene** is a unit of conflict with three parts: **goal, conflict, disaster**. A **sequel** is the response: **reaction, dilemma, decision**; the decision becomes the next scene's goal. A sequel functions "to translate disaster into a goal, telescope reality, and control tempo." — [Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel); [Advanced Fiction Writing: Scenes, Sequels and Chapters](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/blog/2009/05/25/scenes-sequels-and-chapters/)

**Try-fail cycles and "yes, but / no, and" (Writing Excuses)**
- A try-fail cycle: the character tries something to solve the problem posed at the start, fails, and must try again; it is the basic unit of a story's middle. Each attempt resolves as "**Yes, but**" (success plus a new complication) or "**No, and**" (failure plus things get worse). Mary Robinette Kowal says this replaced her earlier flat yes/no outcomes. — [Writing Excuses 16.41: Middles and Conflicts](https://writingexcuses.com/16-41-middles-and-conflicts/)
- Caution from the same podcast: do not treat "three try-fail cycles" as a checklist; conflicts should belong to the story's central thread, or the plot bloats. The MICE quotient (Milieu, Inquiry, Character, Event) is used to pick which kind of obstacle fits. — [Writing Excuses 10.29: Why Should My Characters Fail Spectacularly?](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/); [Writing Excuses 16.42](https://writingexcuses.com/16-42-m-i-c-e-quotient-after-the-fact/)

**Dan Harmon's story circle**
- Eight steps: You (a character in a zone of comfort), Need (wants something), Go (enters an unfamiliar situation), Search (adapts), Find (gets what they wanted), Take, Return, Change; a compression of Campbell's monomyth used per episode. — [The Book Designer: The 8-Step Dan Harmon Story Circle](https://www.thebookdesigner.com/the-dan-harmon-story-circle/); [Boords: The Dan Harmon Story Circle](https://boords.com/blog/storytelling-101-the-dan-harmon-story-circle)

**Serial television structure**
- A, B and C plots descend in importance and screen time; B stories tend to be the emotional, character-driven ones; the C story is often a "runner" with long-term effect on the season arc. Each thread is its own small story with beginning, middle and end, interleaved. — [Industrial Scripts: A, B and C plots](https://industrialscripts.com/?p=238092); [TV Calling: A, B and C stories](https://www.tv-calling.com/what-are-a-b-and-c-stories-in-screenwriting-tv-writing-101/)
- The cold open exists to hook immediately with a question, a shock or a comic situation; acts end on hooks; hybrids give each episode a self-contained plot over a season arc that advances underneath, so writers distinguish an episode arc from a season arc. — [No Film School: TV writing terms](https://nofilmschool.com/tv-writing-terms); [Fiveable: television series formats](https://fiveable.me/writing-the-episodic-drama/unit-1/television-series-formats/study-guide/LshRGsdHlGHBHa3m)

**The ICI doctrine (Chris McDowall, Bastionland, 17 September 2018)**
- A three-word planning checklist: **Information** (players cannot choose meaningfully without it; he questions what is gained by gating it behind perception or knowledge rolls and rewards good questions with great answers), **Choice** (several options, none easy), **Impact** ("Everything you do matters"; renamed from "Consequences", which sounded too negative). — [Bastionland: The ICI Doctrine](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09/the-ici-doctrine-information-choice.html)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) Tag every block with its beat and arrow.** A record field such as `beat: procedural|dramatic|commentary|anticipation|gratification|bringdown|pipe|question|reveal` and `arrow: up|down|flat` costs one line per block and lets code detect the failure Laws' book is about: long runs in one direction or of one type. A lint such as "no more than three consecutive down arrows; no chapter without at least one dramatic beat; every `question` beat has an id that some later `reveal` closes" turns pacing from taste into a check.
- **(Inference) Petitioner/granter is a compact scene spec for companions.** For a relationship scene the brief can state: who petitions, what emotional concession is sought, and whether the granter's current disposition makes granting cheap or costly. The token economy translates to "a companion who was refused last time is owed a concession" (a tally the engine can keep), which prevents an LLM's drift toward everyone always agreeing.
- **(Inference) Scene and sequel maps to log-then-scene serials.** A day's scene (goal, conflict, disaster or success-with-cost) followed by a short sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision) is a natural home for a player choice: the *decision* slot is his.
- **(Inference) "Yes, but / no, and" is the outcome grammar.** With dice or tiers resolved by an engine, the four outcomes (yes-and, yes-but, no-but, no-and) can be mapped to tiers and handed to the narrator as the required shape of the result; this directly counters the "AI GM is too nice" report in section 8.
- **(Inference) A/B/C threads for a weekly chapter.** A = the chapter's quest; B = one relationship thread; C = a runner that pays off in a later chapter (store it as a promise, section 9). Cold open = the strong start; chapter end = hook or choice.
- **(Inference) ICI is a pre-choice checklist for the narrator:** before offering a choice, has the page given the information needed to choose, are there at least two options with real cost, and is there a recorded impact for each?

### Gaps
- The full list of Laws' nine beats was not confirmed by any single source this session. **[recall, unverified]:** procedural, dramatic, commentary, anticipation, gratification, bringdown, pipe (plants something for later payoff), question (opens a curiosity), reveal (supplies wanted information); arrows are up (hope), down (fear) and lateral.
- DramaSystem's **dramatic poles** (each character defined by two opposed inner pulls) were only loosely described in the sources found.
- **Kishotenketsu** could not be searched (budget exhausted). **[recall, unverified]:** a four-part East Asian structure, ki (introduction), sho (development), ten (twist or turn to a seemingly unrelated element), ketsu (reconciliation of the two), often cited as structure driven by contrast rather than conflict.
- Harmon's steps 6–8 were named but not described in the retrieved snippets; his original Channel 101 tutorials were not opened.
- No sourced account of how a writers' room actually tracks arcs across a season was found; the TV sources are study guides.

## 6. Character arcs and relationships: beliefs, instincts, keys, bonds, flags; companion approval; delayed consequences

### Takeaway
Tabletop "flag" mechanics put each character's wants on the sheet as short statements and pay out when play touches them, which tells the GM what to aim scenes at. CRPG approval systems do the same for companions with a number and thresholds, and the designers who spoke on record describe them as a deliberate *limit* on how far player action can bend a character.

### Cited Findings
- **Burning Wheel (Luke Crane):** characters carry Beliefs, Instincts and Traits; play that engages them earns artha in three kinds (Fate, Persona, Deeds), which fuels success at hard tests and advancement. Instincts are "programmatic reactions your characters can always do." The design is "carrots, not sticks": nobody is forced to play to a Belief; they simply do not get paid. — [Cannibal Halfling: Burning Wheel In-Depth](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/?p=5595); [Burning Wheel: Beliefs and Instincts](https://www.burningwheel.com/pages/beliefs-and-instincts); [Burning Wheel forum: BW Best Practices](https://forums.burningwheel.com/t/bw-best-practices/12626)
- **Dragon Age (BioWare):** David Gaider on the design stance: "In Dragon Age, we don't have a good and evil meter" (approval is per companion, not a global morality score). A less established site adds that Dragon Age II replaced approval/disapproval with friendship/rivalry so disagreement could be a sustained relationship; treat as unverified. — [Cloudspress: Why Dragon Age: Origins still shapes companion-driven RPGs](https://www.cloudspress.com/why-dragon-age-origins-still-shapes-companion-driven-rpgs/)
- **Baldur's Gate 3 (Larian):** a writer describes its "Dragon-Agey" reputation system as existing to stop players totally breaking the NPCs, because otherwise "We would be working on the game forever", and says it "becomes a dice roll" at points to keep romance natural. Reactions surface as approval pop-ups in the Dragon Age / KOTOR 2 manner. — [PC Gamer interview report](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/baldurs-gate/baldurs-gate-3-writer-says-its-dragon-agey-rep-system-is-there-to-stop-you-totally-breaking-the-npcs-but-it-becomes-a-dice-roll-at-points-to-keep-romance-natural/); [GamesRadar report](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/baldur-s-gate/baldurs-gate-3-writer-says-the-rpgs-reputation-system-exists-because-larian-cant-just-let-players-break-party-members-we-would-be-working-on-the-game-forever/); [bg3.wiki: Approval](https://bg3.wiki/wiki/Approval)
- **Delayed consequences in LLM tooling:** one self-hosted AI-GM engine auto-extracts world-state facts after each turn, explicitly including promises, debts and deaths along with who is where. — [Sagesheep/NarrativeEngine-P](https://github.com/Sagesheep/NarrativeEngine-P)
- A SillyTavern practitioner pattern: when a quest finishes, the AI writes an entry attached to the people and places involved, so it is pulled back into context whenever they reappear (a callback mechanism keyed on entities). — [r/SillyTavernAI snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1sfrj3w/snapshots/2026-04-09T19%3A14%3A28.706661Z)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) Approval as a bounded variable is a guard against LLM agreeableness.** The Larian remark (approval exists so characters cannot be "broken") states the function precisely: the number is an authored limit on persuasion. For an LLM narrator that wants to please, an engine-owned approval score with thresholds, moved only by recorded events, is the equivalent guard.
- **(Inference) Data shape for a companion.**
  ```yaml
  companion:
    id: C3
    beliefs:   ["I will ... because ..."]          # 2-3, each a statement play can test
    instincts: ["always ...", "never ..."]
    key: {hit: "when she ...", buyoff: "if she ever ..."}     # buyoff = the arc's turning point
    approval: 14                                     # engine-owned
    thresholds: [{at: 20, opens: st.confidence}, {at: -10, opens: st.rupture}]
    knows: [ev.ch03-d4, ev.ch05-b1]                  # what she witnessed or heard; approval moves only on these
    owed: [{what: "an answer", since: ch04}]         # refused petitions (DramaSystem)
  ```
  Instruction: "A companion reacts only to what is in `knows`. A scene with her should test one belief or trip one instinct; say which in the record. The buyoff is never forced: it is an option the story may offer once."
- **(Inference) Consequence ledger entry.** Delayed consequences need a *due condition*, not a date alone:
  ```yaml
  consequence:
    id: K12
    cause: choice.ch04.k1
    owed: "what the world now owes him, or he the world"
    due_when: "next time he enters <place>" | "when F1.D1 reaches 4" | "by ch10"
    seen_by: [C3]
    status: pending | landed | lapsed
    landed_in: null
  ```
  The brief should surface entries whose `due_when` is satisfiable in the coming block ("due now") separately from those merely pending.

### Gaps

## 7. Solo-RPG structure engines: Mythic GME and Ironsworn/Starforged

### Takeaway
Solo engines replace the GM's judgement with three things an LLM lacks by default: an external randomiser that can contradict expectation, persistent **lists** (threads, characters) that random events are forced to draw from, and **progress tracks** whose completion is rolled rather than declared. They are the best existing answer to "how does the story stay surprising and keep old threads alive when nobody is steering."

### Cited Findings
**Mythic Game Master Emulator (Tana Pigeon, Word Mill Games; 1st ed. 2006, 2nd ed. 2023)**
- **Chaos Factor:** a number from 1 to 9 that shifts through play; after each scene the player judges how much control the protagonists had and adjusts it. — [Rolling Alone: When Mythic Takes the Wheel](https://rollingalone.substack.com/p/rolling-without-a-gm-when-mythic); [Glyph & Grok: Review, Mythic GME](https://glyphngrok.substack.com/p/review-mythic-game-master-emulator)
- **Scene test:** state the expected scene, then "Roll 1D10 against Chaos to see if the Setup is modified as an Altered Scene (odd) or an Interrupt (even)." An Altered Scene begins "in the next most expected way"; an Interrupt discards the expectation and is built from a random event. — [Word Mill adventure sheet (official)](https://www.wordmillgames.com/assets/mythicadventuresheet.pdf); [Fantasy Grounds blog on Mythic scenes](https://server.fantasygrounds.com/forums/entry.php?bt=1818)
- **Conflict between sources:** an unofficial MCP implementation states the parity the other way round (even = altered, odd = interrupted). The official adventure sheet is the better authority. — [dcm31/mcp-mythic README](https://www.val.town/x/dcm31/mcp-mythic/branch/main/version/42/code/README.md)
- **Lists:** a Characters list and a Threads list (open goals) are kept on the adventure sheet and "randomly selected when called for"; the 2e layout chunks each list into 25 slots in five groups of five. — [Word Mill adventure sheet](https://www.wordmillgames.com/assets/mythicadventuresheet.pdf); [dcm31/mcp-mythic README](https://www.val.town/x/dcm31/mcp-mythic/branch/main/version/42/code/README.md)
- **Random events** fire on a doubles result on a d100 Fate roll when the repeated digit is at or under the Chaos Factor, and on any Interrupt scene. The **Event Focus** table decides what the event is about; its entries include Remote Event, NPC Action, New NPC, Move Toward a Thread, Move Away from a Thread, Close a Thread, PC Negative, PC Positive, Ambiguous, NPC Negative, NPC Positive. — [Solo engine reference (community)](https://chicago-by-night.pages.dev/reference/solo-engine/scene-mechanics); [Obsidian Mythic v2 script library](https://github.imc.re/jon-heard/obsidian-inline-scripts-library/blob/main/mythicv2.sfile.md)
- Practitioner fallback: when you have no idea what happens next, skip the roll and declare an automatic Interrupt generated from the random event tables. — [Solo engine reference (community)](https://chicago-by-night.pages.dev/reference/solo-engine/scene-mechanics)

**Ironsworn (Shawn Tomkin, 2018) and Starforged (2022)**
- **Swear an Iron Vow:** write the vow, give the quest a rank, roll +heart. Ranks: troublesome, dangerous, formidable, extreme, epic. Each vow has a ten-box progress track; how much one milestone fills depends on rank, from three full boxes (troublesome) down to a quarter of a box (epic). The track need not be full before attempting to finish. — [Celestial Isle: Swear an Iron Vow](https://celestialisle.pages.dev/systems/Ironsworn/Swear-an-Iron-Vow); [Gnome Stew: Ironsworn review](https://gnomestew.com/ironsworn-review/); [itch.io community comment on progress per rank](https://itch.io/post/8190030)
- **Fulfill Your Vow** (Starforged wording): at the quest's end, roll the challenge dice and compare to your progress. Strong hit: mark a reward on the quests legacy track by rank (troublesome 1 tick; dangerous 2 ticks; formidable 1 box; extreme 2 boxes; epic 3 boxes). Weak hit: there is more to be done or an unforeseen complication; reward one rank lower unless you swear a new vow to set things right. Miss: among the options, roll both challenge dice, take the lower, clear that many progress boxes and raise the vow's rank by one. — [Starforged SRD: Quest moves](https://rsek.github.io/starforged-srd/moves/quest); [dataforged moves text](https://unpkg.com/dataforged@1.5.3/markdown/moves.md)
- **Pay the Price** is a fate move invoked by other moves ("if you refuse or fail, Pay the Price") and on a miss ("you fail or your assumptions betray you"); **Ask the Oracle** is the companion fate move. Starforged's clock worksheet tracks "dangers, deadlines, and faction projects." — [Starforged playkit](https://dokk.org/library/ironsworn_starforged_playkit_2023); [dataforged moves text](https://unpkg.com/dataforged@1.5.3/markdown/moves.md)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) What the solo engines add that fronts do not.** Fronts advance the *antagonists'* agenda; Mythic's Event Focus can also *close, advance or set back the protagonist's own threads* and inject an NPC from the list. That is the mechanism that revives dormant threads: a thread stays alive because the dice keep pointing at the list it sits on. For an LLM, which otherwise attends to whatever is recent, a forced draw from a persistent list is the countermeasure to recency bias.
- **(Inference) Implementation split.** The engine rolls and selects (scene test against a chaos value; event focus; which thread or character); the brief hands the narrator the result as a constraint ("interrupt: an NPC from the list acts; it is C5"; "this block moves away from thread T3"); the narrator interprets. This is how the Mythic MCP server exposes it to models (tools for chaos, fate rolls, scene checks, lists).
- **(Inference) Chaos factor as a pacing dial.** One integer, raised when a block went against the protagonist and lowered when he was in control, modulating how often plans are disrupted, is a self-correcting rubber band; it can be derived from recorded outcomes rather than judged.
- **(Inference) Vows give arcs honest endings.** A thread with a rank and a progress track, finished by a roll against progress, means the planner cannot simply declare an arc complete; a weak hit ("more remains") and a miss ("the vow grows harder") are ready-made arc extensions. In a game where real-world effort drives progress, the track is where that effort lands.
  ```yaml
  threads:
    - id: T3
      vow: "one sentence"
      rank: formidable            # sets progress per milestone
      progress: 5                 # of 10, engine-owned
      milestones: [{text: "...", in: ch05-d3}]
      last_touched: ch06
      status: open | fulfilled | forsaken
  ```

### Gaps
- **[recall, unverified]** Mythic's full loop: expected scene, test against Chaos Factor, play the scene asking Fate Questions (odds from impossible to certain; yes / no / exceptional yes / exceptional no), then end-of-scene bookkeeping (update both lists, move Chaos Factor by one). Random event = Event Focus plus two meaning-table rolls. 2e's optional Thread Progress Track and Keyed Scenes could not be confirmed (the search found no source for them).
- Event Focus percentage ranges differed between the two community sources and were not checked against the 2e rulebook, so none are given here.
- **[recall, unverified]** Ironsworn's Pay the Price options: make the most obvious negative outcome happen; or envision two outcomes, rate one likely and Ask the Oracle; or roll on the Pay the Price table. Per-rank progress per milestone: troublesome 3 boxes, dangerous 2 boxes, formidable 1 box, extreme 2 ticks, epic 1 tick (the end points were confirmed, the middle values not).
- Starforged's named clock types (campaign versus tension clocks) and Ironsworn bonds/Starforged connections were not confirmed.

## 8. Evidence of these structures being used with an LLM game master, and which representations worked

### Takeaway
By 2025–2026 there is a visible family of open-source "LLM as GM" projects, several built on Claude Code, and they converge on the same architecture: **state in files on disk, mechanics in scripts, the model for narration and judgement**. Clocks, thread lists, quest/arc state and NPC files are attested; one controlled study found a narrator-plus-archivist split beat a single big prompt. Revelation lists, fronts with portents, and beat tagging are *not* attested in what I found.

### Cited Findings
**Projects (2025–2026)**
- **Sstobo/Claude-Code-Game-Master:** a "total conversion" for Claude Code using a CLAUDE.md persona, specialist agents and bash tools (e.g. `dm-note.sh` to record world facts by category, `dm-time.sh` to advance in-game time, `/world-check` to validate campaign consistency). ## 9. Foreshadowing, setup and payoff over a long serial: promise–progress–payoff and tracking planted elements

### Takeaway
Sanderson's promise–progress–payoff gives the vocabulary (every opening makes promises of tone, plot and character; the middle must show visible motion on them; the ending must pay them), and 2026 research shows LLMs specifically fail at the payoff end even when the setup is still in context. The fix in both craft and research is the same: write each planted element down as an explicit obligation with a trigger and track it to closure.

### Cited Findings
- **Brandon Sanderson's framework:** plot is taught as **promise, progress, payoff**: make promises to the reader, maintain a sense of progress, deliver a satisfying payoff. It is the subject of his 2020 lecture series, a 2020 Odyssey Workshop lecture, and "Promise, Progress, Payoff — Plot Theory", lecture 2 of the 2025 series. — [Sanderson 2025 lecture 2 (podcast listing)](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2447316/episodes/16528169-promise-progress-payoff-plot-theory-brandon-sanderson-s-writing-lecture-2-2025); [Odyssey Workshop: Sanderson on Story Promise, Progress, and Payoff](https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=19170)
- Promises are of several kinds: **tone** promises and **plot** promises, "both big and small" (the 2025 Q&A lecture discusses types beyond tone). — [Recap of 2025 lecture 4 (Plot Q&A)](https://recapio.com/digest/plot-q-a-brandon-sanderson-s-writing-lecture-4-2025-by-brandon-sanderson); [Shanahan: Storytelling Lessons, Sanderson's "Promises"](https://shanahan.bearblog.dev/storytelling-lessons-brandon-sandersons-promises/)
- **Progress** is "the motion you apply to your promises"; without clear signposting, readers take real progress for a side plot. — [Shanahan: Storytelling Lessons, Sanderson's "Progress"](https://shanahan.bearblog.dev/storytelling-lessons-brandon-sandersons-progress/)
- **LLMs and Chekhov's gun:** "Codified Foreshadowing-Payoff Text Generation" (CFPG; arXiv 2601.07033, January 2026, Yun et al.) finds that LLMs "frequently fail to bridge these long-range narrative dependencies, often leaving 'Chekhov's guns' unfired even when the necessary context is present", and that existing evaluations overlook this by measuring surface coherence. The method represents each setup as a structured **Foreshadow–Trigger–Payoff** predicate that tracks what must later be fulfilled (pairs mined from the BookSum corpus) and reports substantially better payoff realisation and narrative alignment than standard prompting. — [arXiv 2601.07033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07033)
- A related 2026 paper catalogues consistency bugs in long LLM-generated stories (findings not retrieved). — [arXiv 2603.05890, "Lost in Stories"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05890)
- **Existing bookkeeping devices that double as setup trackers:** Mythic's Threads list (section 7); Smart Memory's arcs that stay listed as open until resolved and can be reopened (section 8); the Lazy DM's carried-forward secrets (section 3); Dungeon World's stakes questions, which are open questions awaiting an answer (section 2). — [Word Mill adventure sheet](https://www.wordmillgames.com/assets/mythicadventuresheet.pdf); [senjinthedragon/Smart-Memory](https://github.com/senjinthedragon/Smart-Memory); [Sly Flourish: Reusing Secrets and Clues](https://slyflourish.com/reusing_secrets_and_clues.html); [Roll20 Dungeon World compendium: Fronts](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) A promise ledger with the CFPG triple.** The research result is that having the setup in context is not enough; the obligation must be stated. So record, at the moment something is planted, what it obliges and when:
  ```yaml
  promises:
    - id: P7
      kind: tone | plot | character | object     # object = a planted thing (Laws' "pipe")
      planted_in: ch03-d2
      shown: "what the page actually showed, in one line"
      owed: "what a reader now expects to see answered"
      trigger: "the condition under which the payoff becomes possible or due"
      progress: [ch05-d1, ch07-b2]                # blocks that visibly moved it
      due_by: "before bottleneck B4" | ch14
      status: open | progressing | paid | released
      paid_in: null
  ```
  `released` matters: a promise the story decides not to keep must be let go *on the page* (someone gives the thing up, the question is declared closed), not silently dropped.
- **(Inference) Three engine checks cover most of the craft.** (1) Staleness: any open promise with no entry in `progress` for N chapters is surfaced in the planning brief ("owed, quiet since ch05"). (2) Trigger satisfied: when recorded state meets a promise's `trigger`, the block's brief lists it as "can be paid now". (3) Closing audit: before an arc or book ends, list every promise still open and require each to be paid, progressed with a stated carry-over, or released.
- **(Inference) Plant on purpose, with a budget.** Because every promise is a debt, the planner should be told how many new ones a chapter may open (for example, at most two) and to prefer paying an old one. Without a cap an LLM's fluency produces more setups than any serial can pay, which is the "unfired guns" failure seen from the other side.
- **(Inference) Tie promises to the other structures.** A promise's payoff should be reachable from more than one road (section 4) and should be listed as a `must` secret or revelation clue (sections 1 and 3) when the next bottleneck depends on it; a grim portent that has been shown is itself a promise (the doom) and can share the ledger.
- **(Inference)** The epithet/earned-title style of payoff, a thing the story withholds until a specific kind of scene, is a promise with a strict `trigger`; the ledger form handles it without special cases.

### Gaps
- Sanderson's own lecture notes or transcript were not opened; the three-way split into tone, plot and **character-arc** promises is how the lecture is commonly summarised but only tone and plot were confirmed in the retrieved snippets.
- The CFPG paper was seen as abstract and snippets only; its metrics and whether it was tested in interactive (not just generative) settings are unknown.
- No practitioner source on how television or serial-fiction writers keep a setup/payoff bible was retrieved; I found no reliable sources on that in the searches run.
- No LLM-GM project was found that implements a promise or foreshadowing ledger; NarrativeEngine-P's extracted "promises" are in-fiction promises between characters, which is a related but different thing.

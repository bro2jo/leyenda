## 1. What Anthropic's current prompting guidance says that bears on creative writing and role-play (Claude 4.x and 5.x families)

### Takeaway
Anthropic's current single reference page ("Prompting best practices", covering Claude Fable 5.2 back to Haiku 4.5) says: be explicit, give the reason behind each rule, say what to do rather than what not to do, use 3-5 diverse examples (because examples are copied closely), match the prompt's style to the output you want, put long material first and the ask last, and prefer high-level thinking instructions over scripted steps. Three things have changed since older advice was written: prefill on the last assistant turn is gone (400 error from Claude 4.6 onward), emphatic "CRITICAL/MUST" wording now causes over-application, and the newest models (Fable 5.x) follow style rules so literally that hard numeric style limits make prose choppy and asking for visible reasoning in the output can be refused.

### Cited Findings

**Scope of the guidance**
- [Vendor] The page states it is "the reference for prompt engineering with current Claude models, including Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Mythos 5.2, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Mythos 5.1, Claude Fable 5, Claude Mythos 5, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Opus 5, Claude Opus 4.8, Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Haiku 5.5, and Claude Haiku 4.5", with separate per-model pages, and warns: "Where a technique names a specific model, treat it as measured on that model and re-check it against your own evals before applying it to another." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] The page contains no section on fiction, creative writing or role-play. The nearest creative guidance is the frontend "AI slop" section and the "Keep Claude in character" section of the consistency page (both below). — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Be explicit**
- [Vendor] "Claude responds well to clear, explicit instructions. Being specific about the output you want can help enhance results. If you want Claude to do more than the task strictly requires, explicitly request it rather than relying on the model to infer this from vague prompts." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows. The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result." and "Key test: Show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they'd be confused, Claude will be too." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] Migration advice: "Frame your instructions with modifiers: Adding modifiers that encourage Claude to increase the quality and detail of its output can help better shape Claude's performance." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] Literalness (Opus 4.8 page): "Claude Opus 4.8 interprets prompts literally and explicitly, particularly at lower effort levels. It does not silently generalize an instruction from one item to another, and it does not infer requests you didn't make... If you need Claude to apply an instruction broadly, state the scope explicitly". — [Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)

**Give the reason behind an instruction**
- [Vendor] "Providing context or motivation behind your instructions, such as explaining to Claude why such behavior is important, can give Claude more information about your goals and help it deliver more targeted responses." The worked example contrasts "NEVER use ellipses" (less effective) with "Your response will be read aloud by a text-to-speech engine, so never use ellipses since the text-to-speech engine will not know how to pronounce them." and concludes: "Claude can generalize from the explanation." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] For Fable 5.2, on accumulated rules: "delete the cautions whose reason no longer holds, and rewrite absolute rules so that they state their scope and reason." — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)

**Positive instructions versus prohibitions**
- [Vendor] "Tell Claude what to do instead of what not to do. Instead of: 'Do not use markdown in your response'. Try: 'Your response should be composed of smoothly flowing prose paragraphs.'" — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Positive examples showing how Claude can communicate with the appropriate level of concision tend to be more effective than negative examples or instructions that tell the model what not to do." — [Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)
- [Vendor] On breaking a persistent default (written about visual design, but it is Anthropic's only documented statement on how a "don't" behaves against a strong default): "This default is persistent. Generic instructions ('don't use cream,' 'make it clean and minimal') tend to shift the model to a different fixed palette rather than producing variety. Two approaches work reliably: 1. Specify a concrete alternative. The model follows explicit specs precisely... 2. Have the model propose options before building. This breaks the default and gives users control. If you previously relied on `temperature` for design variety, use this approach; it produces meaningfully different directions across runs." — [Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)

**Examples are followed closely (and the over-indexing risk)**
- [Vendor] "Examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure." Examples should be "Relevant: Mirror your actual use case closely. Diverse: Cover edge cases and vary enough that Claude doesn't pick up unintended patterns. Structured: Wrap examples in `<example>` tags (multiple examples in `<examples>` tags) so Claude can distinguish them from instructions." Tip: "Include 3-5 examples for best results. You can also ask Claude to evaluate your examples for relevance and diversity, or to generate additional ones based on your initial set." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor, older page, via search snippet only] The earlier "Claude 4 best practices" page carried a section headed "Be vigilant with examples & details", stating that Claude 4 models pay attention to details and examples as part of instruction following, so examples should show wanted behaviour and avoid unwanted behaviour. The current page no longer uses that heading; the "Diverse" bullet above is its successor. — [Claude 4 best practices (older URL)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-4-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Multishot examples work with thinking. Worked examples in your prompt shape how Claude approaches similar problems in its own thinking blocks." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Match prompt style to output style**
- [Vendor] "The formatting style used in your prompt might influence Claude's response style. If you are still experiencing steerability issues with output formatting, try matching your prompt style to the output style you want as closely as possible. For example, removing markdown from your prompt can reduce the volume of markdown in the output." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**XML structure**
- [Vendor] "XML tags help Claude parse complex prompts unambiguously, especially when your prompt mixes instructions, context, examples, and variable inputs. Wrapping each type of content in its own tag (for example, `<instructions>`, `<context>`, `<input>`) reduces misinterpretation." Best practices: "Use consistent, descriptive tag names across your prompts" and nest where content is hierarchical. Also: "Use XML format indicators. Try: 'Write the prose sections of your response in <smoothly_flowing_prose_paragraphs> tags.'" — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Role prompting and keeping Claude in character**
- [Vendor] "Setting a role in the system prompt focuses Claude's behavior and tone for your use case. Even a single sentence makes a difference". — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Keep Claude in character" now lives as a section of the "Increase output consistency" page (the old stand-alone URL resolves there). It gives two recommendations: "Use system prompts to set the role: Use system prompts to define Claude's role and personality. This sets a strong foundation for consistent responses." with the tip "When setting up the character, provide detailed information about the personality, background, and any specific traits or quirks. This helps the model better emulate and generalize the character's traits."; and "Prepare Claude for possible scenarios: Provide a list of common scenarios and expected responses in your prompts. This 'trains' Claude to handle diverse situations without breaking character." Its only worked example is an enterprise chatbot, not fiction. — [Increase output consistency](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/increase-consistency)
- [Vendor] For long conversations where prefill used to refresh a role: "Context hydration and role consistency... For very long conversations, inject what were previously prefilled-assistant reminders into the user turn. If context hydration is part of a more complex agentic system, consider hydrating through tools... or during context compaction." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Long-context ordering**
- [Vendor] For 20k+ token inputs: "Put long-form data at the top: Place your long documents and inputs near the top of your prompt, above your query, instructions, and examples. This improves performance across all models." Note: "Queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests, especially with complex, multidocument inputs." Also wrap each document in `<document>` tags with `<document_content>` and `<source>` subtags, and "Ground responses in quotes: For long document tasks, ask Claude to quote relevant parts of the documents first before carrying out its task. This helps Claude focus on the relevant content and ignore the rest of the document." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Thinking (extended / adaptive) for planning before prose**
- [Vendor] "Claude 4.6 and later models and Claude Mythos Preview use adaptive thinking (`thinking: {type: "adaptive"}`), where Claude dynamically determines when and how much to think. On Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Mythos 5.2, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Mythos 5.1, Claude Fable 5, Claude Mythos 5, and Claude Opus 5.5, thinking is always on and adaptive thinking is the only mode." Manual `budget_tokens` "returns a 400 error" on Claude 4.7 and later. — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Prefer general instructions over prescriptive steps. A prompt such as 'think thoroughly' often produces better reasoning than a hand-written step-by-step plan. Claude's reasoning frequently exceeds what a human would prescribe." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Ask Claude to self-check. Append something like 'Before you finish, verify your answer against [test criteria].' This catches errors reliably, especially for coding and math." (Opus 5 is named as an exception that over-verifies.) — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] Do not ask newer models to write their reasoning into the reply: on "Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Opus 5, and Claude Sonnet 5.5, rely on thinking instead... a prompt that asks the model to write out its reasoning, for example in `<thinking>` tags, might be declined." Fable 5.2 has a `reasoning_extraction` refusal category ("the request asks the model to reproduce its internal reasoning in the response text") and the advice "If your prompts, skills, or tool descriptions ask the model to write its reasoning in the response, remove those instructions." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)
- [Vendor] Effort on Fable 5.2: "Start at `high`, the default on the Claude API." "At `high` and above, the model thinks before almost every reply, including a reply to a greeting." "Effort barely changes how long the model's replies are. To change the length and tone of a reply, use your prompt." "`medium` suits interactive coding, chat, and everyday knowledge work." — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)

**Prefill and its status**
- [Vendor] "Starting with Claude 4.6 models and Claude Mythos Preview, prefilled responses (providing a partial assistant message for Claude to continue from) on the last assistant turn are no longer supported. Requests with prefilled assistant messages to these models return a 400 error. Model intelligence and instruction following have advanced such that most use cases of prefill no longer require it. Earlier models continue to support prefills, and adding assistant messages elsewhere in the conversation is not affected." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] Documented replacements: for preambles, "Respond directly without preamble. Do not start with phrases like 'Here is...', 'Based on...', etc."; for continuations, "Your previous response was interrupted and ended with `[previous_response]`. Continue from where you left off."; for role consistency, reminders in the user turn (quoted above). — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] The consistency page repeats: "Prefilling is not supported on Claude 4.6 and later models and Claude Mythos Preview. Use structured outputs on models that support it, or system prompt instructions, instead." — [Increase output consistency](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/increase-consistency)

**Avoid over-emphatic language**
- [Vendor] "Claude Opus 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.6 are also more responsive to the system prompt than previous models. If your prompts were designed to reduce undertriggering on tools or skills, these models might now overtrigger. The fix is to soften any aggressive language. Where you might have said 'CRITICAL: You MUST use this tool when...,' you can use more normal prompting such as 'Use this tool when...' instead." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "Tune anti-laziness prompting: If your prompts previously encouraged the model to be more thorough or use tools more aggressively, reduce that guidance." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Style rules are now followed very literally (Fable 5.x)**
- [Vendor] "Style rules for writing: Claude Fable 5.2 follows style rules closely, so a hard limit such as 'one instruction per sentence, in 20 words or fewer' can make its prose choppy. Remove the rules you no longer need". — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)
- [Vendor] "Cautious instructions build up wherever context carries over: memory, notes, instruction files, and messages between agents. Claude Fable 5.2 follows these instructions closely". One listed fix: "Check what reviewer agents attach." — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)
- [Vendor] Fable 5.1 writing: "Claude Fable 5.1's writing has few stock phrases and little unexplained jargon. In some cases, though, its prose is denser than Claude Fable 5's: sentences run longer and there are fewer paragraph breaks. An instruction that defines the anti-pattern, mannered prose, helps. Add it to a user message (preferred) or the system prompt". The supplied block: "Mannered prose substitutes metaphor and flourish for direct statement. Instead of 'a parameter worth varying,' the mannered writer produces 'a dial worth turning.' Instead of 'this point still matters,' they write 'this point earns its keep.' The phrases exist to display the writer, not to convey the idea, and readers can tell. That is why mannered prose irritates: it makes the reader work harder so the writer can perform. It is also imprecise. Metaphors drag in connotations the writer did not choose and cannot control. The fix is to say what you mean. When a literal phrase is available, use it." and "The short version also tends to work: 'Please remove all mannered prose.'" — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.1](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)
- [Vendor] "As with any new model, prose style on long-form writing might shift... If your product relies on a specific voice, re-evaluate style prompts against the new baseline." — [Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)

**Anthropic's own statement about convergence ("AI slop")**
- [Vendor] In the frontend section, Anthropic's sample prompt tells the model: "You tend to converge toward generic, 'on distribution' outputs. In frontend design, this creates what users call the 'AI slop' aesthetic." and "You still tend to converge on common choices (Space Grotesk, for example) across generations. Avoid this". The prompt then names the specific defaults to avoid and the dimensions to vary. — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Draft, review, refine as a chain**
- [Vendor] "The most common chaining pattern is self-correction: generate a draft → have Claude review it against criteria → have Claude refine based on the review. Each step is a separate API call so you can log, evaluate, or branch at any point." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

### Inferences
- The vendor guidance is written for assistants and agents, not fiction. Applying it to scene writing is a transfer, though a direct one: a style guide is "instructions with reasons", a scene brief is "explicit context", exemplar passages are "multishot examples", and a rotating scene mode is "specify a concrete alternative" to a persistent default.
- The frontend "AI slop" block is the closest thing to an official anti-slop template. Its shape (name the convergence tendency, list the specific defaults, list the dimensions to vary, ask for unexpected choices) can be mirrored for prose.
- Because Fable 5.x follows style rules literally and literal examples closely, a fiction style guide should state ranges and purposes rather than hard counts ("one image per paragraph" as a firm rule risks the same choppiness as the documented "20 words or fewer" rule), and exemplar passages should be several and varied, or the model will reproduce their sentence shapes, openings and imagery.
- "Plan beats in thinking before writing" should be requested as a thinking behaviour ("think through the scene's turn and beats before you write") or as a separate planning call that returns a plan as its deliverable. A prompt asking the model to print its reasoning in tags inside the reply is the pattern the docs say may be declined on Fable 5.x.
- Any technique that relied on prefill (starting the assistant turn mid-sentence in the character's voice, a bracketed character tag, "continue from here") does not work on current models and must be moved into the user turn or the system prompt.
- The Fable 5.1 "mannered prose" block shows Anthropic's preferred way to ban a style: define the anti-pattern, give paired examples, and explain why it harms the reader. Its last line ("When a literal phrase is available, use it") was written for expository prose; in fiction it would need a scoped version that still permits deliberate figurative language.

### Gaps
- No Anthropic page fetched today gives guidance specific to fiction, role-play pacing, second-person narration or NPC voice. The "Keep Claude in character" section is two bullets and a chatbot example.
- I did not retrieve the older stand-alone "Keep Claude in character" page or the older "Claude 4 best practices" page verbatim. Older versions reportedly recommended prefilling a character tag to hold a role in long conversations; I could not confirm that wording and it would in any case be obsolete on Claude 4.6+.
- The per-model pages for Fable 5, Opus 5, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5 and Sonnet 5.5 were not read. The Fable 5.1 page was read only for its "Writing density" and "Formatting in chat" sections.
- Anthropic publishes no measured figures on fiction quality for any of these techniques. The "up to 30 percent" figure is for query placement with long documents, not for creative work.

## 2. Documented stylistic tics of LLM fiction and of Claude, and the measured evidence

### Takeaway
The best-measured facts are lexical and structural: a tiny set of names, settings and professions recur across models (2026 study: 11 words appear in 88.3% of 20,000 stories; "Elias" in 26.5%, "Elara" in 13.1%), some phrases occur over 1,000 times more often than in human text, "not X, but Y" is common enough to be a quarter of a public slop metric, LLM stories are "homogeneously positive and lack tension", and professional writers editing LLM prose (including Claude 3.5 Sonnet) most often fix awkward phrasing, poor sentence structure, redundant exposition and cliché. Specific tics such as em-dash overuse, "barely above a whisper", therapist-style dialogue and reflective endings are widely reported by practitioners but I found no study that counts them.

### Cited Findings

**Stock names, settings, professions**
- [Research, 2026; details via search summary and Cornell coverage, full text not read] Hamilton and Mimno, "Elias in the Lighthouse, Again? Diagnosing Low Diversity in LLM Stories" (arXiv May 2026, EMNLP 2026): 20,000 stories sampled from four current models using five prompts; 11 words occur in 88.3% of generated stories, "with little difference between models". Those words include names (Elias, Mara, Elara), settings (lighthouses) and professions (clockmakers, librarians). "Elias" appears in 26.5% of all stories, "Mara" in 16.7%, "Elara" in 13.1%; a location, a character name and a profession each appear in nearly all stories. The authors trace the tokens to preference (post-training) data rather than literature: they are rare in published fiction and pre-training data. Cornell's coverage names WildChat as the lead hypothesised origin. — [arXiv 2605.26492](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492); [EMNLP 2026 PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.2013.pdf); [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/333804)
- [Research] The Antislop paper's introduction uses "Elara" as its example of a stock character name that LLMs repeat in creative writing. — [Antislop, arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)

**Over-represented words and phrases ("slop")**
- [Research, Oct 2025, ICLR 2026; via abstract] Paech et al., "Antislop: A Comprehensive Framework for Identifying and Eliminating Repetitive Patterns in Language Models": "Some slop patterns appear over 1,000 times more frequently in LLM output than human text." The slop fingerprint was built by generating about 2,000 outputs from Reddit creative-writing prompts and comparing word and n-gram frequencies against human text. — [arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10008156)
- [Research/Tool] The EQ-Bench "slop score" is "a weighted composite metric designed to detect AI-generated text patterns": 60% slop words ("Frequency of individual words that appear unnaturally often in LLM outputs"), 25% not-x-but-y patterns ("Frequency of contrast patterns like 'not just X, but Y' which are overused by AI"), 15% slop trigrams. The contrast pattern is detected in two stages: 10 regexes on normalised text, then 35 regexes on a part-of-speech-tagged stream. The leaderboard script also computes n-gram repetition, MATTR-500 lexical diversity and Flesch-Kincaid grade. — [sam-paech/slop-score README](https://github.com/sam-paech/slop-score)
- [Research/Tool] The EQ-Bench creative-writing benchmark lists "slop bias" ("favoring overused tropes") among the uncontrolled biases of its LLM judge, alongside judge self-bias, positivity/negativity bias and stylistic preferences. Judge: Claude Sonnet 4.6; 32 prompts × 3 iterations; outputs truncated to 4,000 characters. — [EQ-bench/creative-writing-bench README](https://github.com/EQ-bench/creative-writing-bench)

**Flat emotional arcs and tidy positivity**
- [Research, EMNLP 2024; models include Claude 3 Opus, GPT-4, Gemini Pro, Llama 3; via abstract] Tian et al., "Are Large Language Models Capable of Generating Human-Level Narratives?": "While human-written stories are suspenseful, arousing, and diverse in narrative structures, LLM stories are homogeneously positive and lack tension." Explicitly integrating discourse features (story arcs, turning points, arousal/valence) into generation gave "over 40% improvement in neural storytelling in terms of diversity, suspense, and arousal." — [ACL Anthology 2024.emnlp-main.978](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.978)

**Plot-level sameness**
- [Research, PNAS Aug 2025; GPT-4 and LLaMA-3] Xu et al., "Echoes in AI: Quantifying lack of plot diversity in LLM outputs": "LLM-generated stories often contain combinations of idiosyncratic plot elements echoed frequently across generations and across different LLMs, while plots from the original human-written stories are rarely recreated or even echoed in pieces." Their Sui Generis score correlates moderately with human judgements of surprise. — [arXiv 2501.00273](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00273); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12415252)
- [Research, Science Advances July 2024; GPT-4 story ideas] Doshi and Hauser: stories written with access to generative-AI ideas were rated more creative, better written and more enjoyable, most of all for less creative writers, but were more similar to each other. University coverage reports +8.1% novelty and +9% usefulness with the most AI access, and a 10.7% rise in similarity for writers who used one AI idea. Scope: short stories, one model. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532); [University of Exeter](https://news.exeter.ac.uk/?p=13749)

**Expert-judged creativity gap**
- [Research, CHI 2024; old models: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude v1.3] Chakrabarty et al., "Art or Artifice? Large Language Models and the False Promise of Creativity": the Torrance Test of Creative Writing has 14 binary tests across fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration; 48 stories judged by 10 creative writers; "LLM-generated stories pass 3-10X less TTCW tests than stories written by professionals." Snippet figures: expert stories passed 84.7% of tests on average; LLM pass rates ranged from about 9% (ChatGPT) to about 30% (Claude); Claude v1.3 did better on fluency, flexibility and elaboration, GPT-4 on originality. No LLM tested as a judge correlated positively with the expert assessments. — [arXiv 2309.14556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556v2)

**What professional editors actually fix (includes Claude 3.5 Sonnet)**
- [Research, 2024-25; via abstract and summaries] Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu, "Can AI writing be salvaged? Mitigating Idiosyncrasies and Improving Human-AI Alignment in the Writing Process through Edits": the LAMP corpus has 1,057 LLM-generated paragraphs (literary fiction and creative non-fiction tasks) edited by 18 professional writers, yielding 8,035 fine-grained edits. "None of the LLMs used in our study (GPT4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Llama-3.1-70b) outperform each other in terms of writing quality, revealing common limitations across model families." — [arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)
- [Research, via a third-party summary; verify against the paper] The seven edit categories are Cliché, Unnecessary/Redundant Exposition, Purple Prose, Poor Sentence Structure, Lack of Specificity and Detail, Awkward Word Choice and Phrasing, and Tense Inconsistency. Reported frequencies: Awkward Word Choice and Phrasing 28%, Poor Sentence Structure 20%, Unnecessary/Redundant Exposition 18%, Cliché 17%. Purple prose was less common overall, with GPT-4o showing more of it than the others. — [ChatPaper summary of 2409.14509](https://chatpaper.com/paper/60678)

**Stylometric detectability**
- [Research, Nov 2025] O'Sullivan, in Humanities & Social Sciences Communications, uses stylometry on human versus LLM creative writing and finds LLM outputs remain statistically and stylistically identifiable as machine-generated. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/5039b9c9119946a9be39bbcbfec161b3)
- [Research, 2025-26; via abstract] In Chakrabarty, Ginsburg and Dhillon's author-emulation study, text produced by in-context prompting was flagged as AI-generated by state-of-the-art detectors 97% of the time, versus 3% for output from models fine-tuned on an author's complete works. — [arXiv 2510.13939](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v3)

**Practitioner-reported Claude-isms (not measured)**
- [Practitioner] SillyTavern presets carry ban lists aimed at Claude-family prose: one bans "ozone," "hum," "hummed," "murmured," "caught," "anchor" and "grounded" and filters certain dialogue-tag patterns and em dashes; a community anti-slop list names "shivers down spines", "husky voices" and "smelling ozone"; another preset uses regex scripts to strip overused em dashes. One user's verdict, from a SillyTavern subreddit thread I could not pin to a single URL: "you can reduce some of them with presets, but getting rid of them entirely doesn't really seem to be possible." — [Whimsical preset (Rentry)](https://rentry.org/whimsicalpreset); [Marinara's LLM Hub (Rentry)](https://rentry.org/marinara-spaghetti); [Marinara's universal preset (Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/MarinaraSpaghetti/SillyTavern-Settings/commit/3ee7c5cc86c15d5e8e4c2e01cad4a233415791c6)
- [Vendor] Anthropic describes Fable 5.1 as having "few stock phrases" but a residual tendency to "mannered prose" (metaphor and flourish in place of direct statement) and denser, longer sentences with fewer paragraph breaks. — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.1](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)

### Inferences
- The tics in the question map onto measured categories as follows. Stock names: directly measured (Hamilton and Mimno). "A testament to", "barely above a whisper", "something shifted": the over-represented word and trigram lists of the slop score cover this class, though I did not retrieve those specific strings from the lists. "Not X, but Y": directly measured as a slop-score component. Tidy epiphany endings and reflective closers: consistent with "homogeneously positive and lack tension" (Tian et al.). Emotional over-labelling and therapist-style dialogue: these correspond to "Unnecessary/Redundant Exposition" (18% of expert edits). Summarised rather than dramatised conflict: corresponds to "Lack of Specificity and Detail" plus the missing turning points in Tian et al. Repetitive paragraph rhythm: corresponds to "Poor Sentence Structure" (20%).
- The names finding suggests the cause is post-training preference data, not the model's reading of literature. That implies the default is strongest exactly where the prompt leaves a slot open (a name, a job, a place), so the cheapest mitigation is to never leave those slots open.
- Since the three model families in LAMP were indistinguishable in quality and shared the same faults, most slop findings from GPT-family studies should transfer to Claude in kind, though not necessarily in degree. The specific word lists differ by model.

### Gaps
- No measured study was found for em-dash overuse, rule-of-three lists, "barely above a whisper", "something shifted", characters articulating feelings like therapists, scenes ending on reflection, or paragraph-rhythm repetition as individual markers. These rest on practitioner reports and on the broader categories above.
- I could not read the slop-score word lists themselves (they sit in the repository's `data/` folder) or any per-model slop score for Claude 4.x/5.x. Third-party pages quoting Claude leaderboard positions on EQ-Bench were inconsistent and are not reported here.
- The 14 TTCW test names and the exact per-model pass rates in "Art or Artifice" were not retrieved from the paper; the 9%/30%/84.7% figures come from search snippets of an earlier version.
- Most measured studies used 2023-2025 models (Claude v1.3, Claude 3 Opus, Claude 3.5 Sonnet). Only Hamilton and Mimno (2026) and Anthropic's own Fable 5.1 note describe current-generation behaviour, and the four models in the 2026 study were not named in what I could access.

## 3. Which mitigations demonstrably or reportedly work

### Takeaway
The measured evidence favours structure and selection over exhortation: planning before drafting (Re3, DOC, Agents' Room, Tian et al.), injecting explicit turning points and arcs, and choosing among candidates with a trained quality model all show gains in human evaluation, while LLM self-editing helps less than human editing because "LLMs don't inherently know what aspects of the writing needs improvement". Prompt-level banned-phrase lists are the weakest tool: decoder-level suppression works, but prompt bans are reported to be leaky or to backfire, and both Anthropic and practitioners point to positive examples and concrete alternatives instead.

### Cited Findings

**A written style guide with reasons**
- [Vendor] Giving the reason lets the model generalise ("Claude can generalize from the explanation"); absolute rules should "state their scope and reason"; and the documented way to remove a prose habit is to define the anti-pattern with paired examples and the harm it does (the "mannered prose" block). — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2); [Prompting Claude Fable 5.1](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)
- [Vendor] Limit: hard numeric style rules make Fable 5.2's prose "choppy" because it "follows style rules closely". — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)

**Banned-phrase lists and their limits**
- [Research] At the decoder level, suppression works: "The Antislop Sampler successfully suppresses 8,000+ patterns while maintaining quality, whereas token banning becomes unusable at just 2,000." The sampler detects an unwanted word, phrase or regex during generation, backtracks to the pattern's first token, lowers its probability and resamples. Fine-tuning with FTPO "achieves 90% slop reduction while maintaining or improving performance in cross-domain evals including GSM8K, MMLU, and creative writing tasks", whereas DPO "suffers significant degradation in writing quality and lexical diversity despite achieving weaker suppression." These methods need logit or weight access, which the Claude API does not give. — [arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061); [antislop-sampler repo](https://github.com/sam-paech/antislop-sampler)
- [Research] Two papers address the "don't think of an elephant" problem directly: Hwang et al. (2024), "Do not think about pink elephant!", which frames it as an ironic-process effect where naming a concept to avoid it makes the model attend to it more; and "Semantic Gravity Wells: Why Negative Constraints Backfire" (Jan 2026), on why models fail "do not use word X" instructions. I saw only abstracts and summaries, and the Hwang et al. experiments may centre on image-generation models rather than prose, so check before using it as evidence about text. — [arXiv 2404.15154](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2404.15154); [Semantic Gravity Wells summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2601.08070)
- [Practitioner] Counter-example in favour of small, curated lists: a commenter describes a system prompt with about five anti-examples of specific Claude-isms (phrases such as "The key insight") that "worked pretty well", extended one phrase at a time as new tics appeared. — [GreaterWrong comment thread, "Can LLM chat be less prolix"](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/jjZrG25HKzJigmwZG/can-llm-chat-be-less-prolix/answer/52xgLkLzNeRmd3D5F)
- [Practitioner] A Reddit report, relayed in a 2026 blog post, says adding a banned-phrase list made output worse (more of the banned phrases), and that "just 5 curated examples of good output" with no banned list and no "don't do X" instructions worked. Model not named. — [tianpan.co, "When Your Forbidden List Becomes a Recipe"](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-05-13-negative-examples-prompts-forbidden-list-becomes-recipe)
- [Practitioner] The evidence is mixed: another analysis notes modern models follow many prohibitions well when they are short and unambiguous, and that AI companies use negative instructions in their own system prompts. — [16x Eval, "The Pink Elephant"](https://eval.16x.engineer/blog/the-pink-elephant-negative-instructions-llms-effectiveness-analysis)
- [Practitioner] SillyTavern practice with Claude: keep ban lists short ("not to list more than a few words at a time"), add entries as new tics appear, and enforce with regex or an extension rather than the prompt alone. — [Whimsical preset (Rentry)](https://rentry.org/whimsicalpreset); [Marinara's LLM Hub (Rentry)](https://rentry.org/marinara-spaghetti)
- [Vendor] Anthropic's own finding on a persistent default: a generic "don't" tends "to shift the model to a different fixed palette rather than producing variety"; the reliable fixes are a concrete alternative or having the model propose several options first. — [Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)

**Exemplar passages in the target voice**
- [Vendor] Examples are "one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure"; use 3-5, make them diverse so "Claude doesn't pick up unintended patterns". — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Tool] NovelCrafter: starting the passage yourself helps; "The AI-generated prose will be more true to your personal writing style." — [NovelCrafter, improving AI output for scene beats](https://www.novelcrafter.com/courses/ultimate-beginners-guide/improving-ai-output-for-scene-beats)
- [Research, 2025-26; via abstracts, versions differ] Limit of exemplars: in a blind pairwise study of author-style emulation, in-context-prompted AI text was strongly disfavoured by MFA-trained readers for stylistic fidelity (OR=0.16) and writing quality (OR=0.13); after fine-tuning on each author's complete works the preference reversed (OR=8.16 and 1.87). The CHI 2026 version reports experts preferred human writing in 82.7% of cases under in-context prompting and preferred AI in 62% of cases after fine-tuning. — [arXiv 2510.13939](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v3); [arXiv 2601.18353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18353)

**Planning before prose**
- [Research, 2022; older GPT-3-era models] Re3 builds a structured overarching plan, injects plan and story state into each prompt, reranks continuations for coherence and premise relevance, and edits for factual consistency. In human evaluation, more of its stories had "a coherent overarching plot (by 14% absolute increase), and relevant to the given initial premise (by 20%)". — [arXiv 2210.06774](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06774v2)
- [Research, ACL 2023] DOC adds a detailed hierarchical outliner and a controller that keeps passages faithful to it: it "substantially outperforms a strong Re3 baseline on plot coherence (22.5% absolute gain), outline relevance (28.2%), and interestingness (20.7%)." — [arXiv 2212.10077](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.10077v3)
- [Research, ICLR 2025; Gemini-based] Agents' Room decomposes writing into planning agents and writing agents whose contributions an orchestrator consolidates into a shared scratchpad; its stories "are preferred by expert evaluators over those produced by baseline systems." A third-party review adds that the multi-agent variants were about twice as long as end-to-end baselines and that a gap in linguistic diversity and originality versus human authors remained. — [arXiv 2410.02603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02603v2); [ICLR 2025 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/0fbc8a83d93dd8021a4dd8d2d34138eb-Abstract-Conference.html)
- [Research] Supplying arcs and turning points explicitly improved diversity, suspense and arousal by over 40% (Tian et al.). — [ACL Anthology 2024.emnlp-main.978](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.978)
- [Vendor] For thinking, prefer a general instruction over a scripted plan: "Claude's reasoning frequently exceeds what a human would prescribe." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

**Self-critique, revision passes, critic agents**
- [Vendor] Anthropic names draft → review against criteria → refine as "the most common chaining pattern", each step a separate call. — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Research] In LAMP, preference ran "Writer-edited > LLM-edited > LLM-generated, suggesting that edits improve human-AI alignment in the writing process". So LLM editing of LLM prose helps, but less than a human editor. The same paper's stated limit: "LLMs don't inherently know what aspects of the writing needs improvement". Few-shot detection and rewriting of the seven categories showed "promising improvements" but stayed below human editing (third-party summary). — [arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)
- [Research, Salesforce 2025; via abstract] "AI-Slop to AI-Polish?": a writing-quality reward model (WQRM) trained on expert edits was used to select among candidate generations; "human evaluation with 9 experienced writers confirmed that WQRM-based selection produced samples preferred by experts 66% overall". — [arXiv 2504.07532](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07532v2)
- [Research, CHI 2024; old models] Zero-shot LLM judges did not correlate positively with expert creative-writing assessments. — [arXiv 2309.14556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556v2)
- [Research] Critique-and-refine systems for stories: CritiCS (EMNLP 2024) uses a group of LLM critics and a leader to refine the plan and then the story over rounds; PREFINE (Oct 2025) critiques drafts against user-derived rubrics and beat a fixed-rubric Self-Refine baseline under LLM-as-judge evaluation; PLOTTER (2026) runs evaluate-plan-revise on event and character graphs before drafting. A 2026 Writer-Editor loop paper cautions that its improvement score is "a synthetic, editor-relative measure, not an absolute quality measure". — [CritiCS, arXiv 2410.02428](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02428v1); [PREFINE, arXiv 2510.21721](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21721v1); [PLOTTER](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.21253); [Writer-Editor loops, arXiv 2605.29625](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29625)

**Getting variety across many scenes**
- [Vendor] To break a default, "Have the model propose options before building... it produces meaningfully different directions across runs", and Anthropic says this replaces reliance on `temperature`. — [Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)
- [Research] Default names, settings and professions are near-universal when the prompt leaves them open (Hamilton and Mimno). — [arXiv 2605.26492](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492)

**Continuity checks and re-reading**
- [Research, Findings of ACL 2026; via abstract] ConStory-Bench ("Lost in Stories: Consistency Bugs in Long Story Generation by LLMs"): 2,000 prompts, a taxonomy of 5 categories and 19 subtypes, an LLM-as-judge checker that grounds each contradiction in quoted text. Errors are most common in factual and temporal dimensions, cluster around the middle of narratives, occur in higher-entropy segments, and "accumulate linearly as story length increases". — [arXiv 2603.05890](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05890); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.410/)
- [Tool] Automated repetition and slop measurement is scriptable: the slop-score tooling computes slop-list matches, n-gram repetition, not-x-but-y patterns and MATTR-500 lexical diversity on any text. — [sam-paech/slop-score README](https://github.com/sam-paech/slop-score)

### Inferences
- With API-only access to Claude there is no sampler-level ban. The Antislop sampler's logic (detect the pattern after it appears, back up, regenerate) can only be approximated outside the model: a script scans the draft for a slop list and for repeats against recent chapters, and flagged sentences go back for a targeted rewrite. This keeps the list out of the writing prompt, which avoids the priming problem.
- The combined evidence suggests an order of effectiveness for prompt-only work: (1) fill the open slots yourself (names, places, the scene's turn, the ending type); (2) show several varied passages in the target voice; (3) explain each style rule; (4) keep a short, current "do instead" list for the two or three worst tics; (5) post-check mechanically. Long ban lists in the prompt come last.
- Exemplars improve voice but cannot be expected to pass expert scrutiny on their own (OR 0.13-0.16 for in-context prompting). For a serial whose "target voice" is its own earlier chapters, the practical equivalent is to feed back the best recent passages as exemplars and rotate them so their imagery is not re-used.
- Critique loops are supported for plan-level problems (coherence, premise relevance, missing turn) and for the seven edit categories when the critic is given the category definitions. They are weakly supported for taste: zero-shot judges did not track experts in 2023, the EQ-Bench judge is acknowledged to carry a slop bias, and a trained reward model was needed to get a 66% expert preference. A critic with a concrete rubric plus selection among two or three candidate drafts is the closest prompt-only analogue of the WQRM result.
- "Rotating scene modes", "end on action or image", "one image per paragraph", "a repetition ledger" and "re-read recent chapters for repeated openings" have no direct measured support. They are consistent with Anthropic's "specify a concrete alternative" finding and with the n-gram repetition metrics above, and should be presented as reasoned practice.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests prompt-level banned-phrase lists on Claude 4.x/5.x, with or without reasons, against an examples-only prompt.
- No controlled human-judged study isolates the effect of an "editor" or "critic" agent on fiction quality for current Claude models; most critique-loop papers are LLM-judged against their own baselines.
- I found no measured evidence on whether re-reading prior chapters reduces repeated imagery, or on "repetition ledgers".
- Whether the Antislop paper tested prompt-based banning as a baseline could not be checked (full text unavailable).

## 4. Scene-level craft worth encoding in a brief

### Takeaway
The craft sources converge on one testable requirement: something must change in the scene. McKee states it as a value that flips charge between the scene's first and last moment; Swain as goal, conflict, disaster; Kowal as "yes, but / no, and"; Matt Bird as "a compelling collision of competing agendas" that changes the story going forward; Saunders as prose that keeps the story escalating. Palahniuk's ban on "thought" verbs is the most concrete single rule against the emotional over-labelling that the LLM studies flag.

### Cited Findings

**The scene must turn (McKee)**
- [Craft] McKee, as quoted on his own site and in summaries of Story: "Ideally, every scene is a STORY EVENT." "If the value-charged condition of the character's life stays unchanged from one end of a scene to the other, nothing meaningful happens." The practical test is to name the value at stake at the opening, then "turn to the close of the scene and ask, Where is this value now?", and check whether the charge moved from positive to negative or the reverse. A scene with no change is a non-event. — [McKee, "Do Your Scenes Turn?"](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)

**Goal, conflict, disaster; reaction, dilemma, decision (Swain)**
- [Craft] Swain's scene has three parts: goal, conflict, disaster. The sequel that follows is a unit of transition with reaction, dilemma and decision, the decision producing the next goal. Motivation-reaction units are the smaller action-and-reaction blocks inside each. — [Scene and sequel (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Scene_and_sequel); [TCK Publishing, "Scene and Sequel"](https://www.tckpublishing.com/scene-and-sequel/)

**Outcomes that keep a story moving (Kowal, via Sanderson's class and Writing Excuses)**
- [Craft] "Yes-but, no-and. Yes-but, movement towards the goal, but with a consequence. No-and, movement away from the goal, with a consequence." In Kowal's 2020 lecture in Sanderson's class, the cycle is: try, face a consequence, react; and around the 67-75% mark the tools reverse to "yes, and" and "no, but" so that try-fail cycles become try-succeed cycles as the story resolves. Writing Excuses adds that try-fail cycles are "setting up your ending." — [Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/?p=22664); [Writing Excuses 10.29](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/); [lecture thread summary](https://en.rattibha.com/thread/1584330138145067008)

**Unpack thought into observable detail (Palahniuk)**
- [Craft] Palahniuk's "Nuts and Bolts: 'Thought' Verbs" (LitReactor, 12 Aug 2013) asks the writer to give up, for half a year, verbs of inner state: thinks, knows, understands, realizes, believes, wants, remembers, imagines, desires. His diagnosis is that writers open a paragraph with such a verb and then spend the paragraph explaining it; the remedy is to present the physical actions and external details that let the reader reach the thought. Critics note the rule is too absolute and that some interiority is legitimate. — [LitReactor](https://litreactor.com/essays/chuck-palahniuk/nuts-and-bolts-“thought”-verbs)

**Escalation and specificity (Saunders)**
- [Craft] From A Swim in a Pond in the Rain: "A swath of prose earns its place in the story to the extent that it contributes to our sense that the story is (still) escalating." Saunders also describes revising by an internal positive/negative meter, reading each line for whether it pulls the reader on or pushes them out (secondary account). — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)

**Scenes as collisions of agendas (Matt Bird)**
- [Craft] Bird's "Ultimate Story Checklist" (over 200 sub-points in the full version) asks of each scene whether it is "a compelling collision of competing agendas" and whether it changes the story going forward. — [Kill Zone summary of The Secrets of Story checklist](https://killzoneblog.com/2023/09/the-ultimate-story-checklist.html)

**What the LLM research says the brief must force**
- [Research] The measured weaknesses a brief should counter: missing turning points and tension (Tian et al.); redundant exposition, cliché, lack of specific detail, poor sentence structure (Chakrabarty et al.). Supplying turning points explicitly produced the >40% gain cited above. — [ACL Anthology 2024.emnlp-main.978](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.978); [arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)

### Inferences
- A compact, checkable scene contract follows from these sources: (a) who wants what in this scene; (b) what or who stands in the way, with its own want; (c) the value at stake and its charge at entry and at exit; (d) the outcome type (yes-but, no-and, and late in an arc yes-and or no-but); (e) what the reader should be left wanting. A critic can verify (c) mechanically by asking McKee's question of the draft.
- Palahniuk's rule converts directly into a positive instruction of the kind Anthropic recommends: "show the action, object or line of dialogue that lets the reader infer the feeling", with the reason attached (the reader does the work and so feels it).
- The serial-level equivalent of Swain's scene/sequel alternation is a rotation of scene types (confrontation, aftermath, quiet character work, discovery, travel). That gives the model a "concrete alternative" each time rather than its default shape.
- Kowal's reversal late in the story is a useful rule for a serial's chapter climaxes: outcomes early in an arc should cost something even when they succeed; outcomes at the resolution may pay extra.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved in this session, so not cited: "enter late, leave early" (Goldman and others), Le Guin's Steering the Craft on point of view and on "crowding and leaping", Sanderson's "promises, progress, payoffs", dialogue doing more than one job, subtext, and ending on a hook. These are standard craft principles but need their own sourcing before the report quotes them.
- The McKee and Swain material comes from McKee's site and from summaries, not from Story or Techniques of the Selling Writer directly. The Palahniuk essay was confirmed by title, date and verb list; his exact examples were not retrieved.
- No source was found that tests these craft constraints as LLM prompt elements, other than Tian et al.'s arcs and turning points.

## 5. What a per-scene brief should contain

### Takeaway
Commercial tools and research pipelines converge on the same brief: a short ordered list of what must happen, the characters present (named explicitly), the setting and time, the point-of-view character and their goal, restrictions, the standing style and world reference, summaries of what came before, and a target length. The research pipelines add the two things the tools leave to the author: explicit story state carried from scene to scene, and a check of the draft against the plan.

### Cited Findings

**Sudowrite**
- [Tool] "Beats are step-by-step instructions for Story Bible to follow when writing the Prose for your Chapter." Story Bible "looks at nearly everything—your Genre, Style, Synopsis, Outline, Characters, and Worldbuilding—to create a list of Beats". "The more detailed and accurate these other sections, the better your Beats will be. And the better your Beats, the better the resulting Prose". "Beats and Chapter Generator will only look at explicitly mentioned Characters and Worldbuilding elements." One beat typically yields around 200 words; prose is generated two beats at a time. — [Sudowrite docs: Beats / Chapter Prose](https://docs.sudowrite.com/using-sudowrite/1ow1qkGqof9rtcyGnrWUBS/beats--chapter-prose/v7UCtPmdHTU9mkwFKCutw2); [Sudowrite docs: What is Story Bible](https://docs.sudowrite.com/using-sudowrite/1ow1qkGqof9rtcyGnrWUBS/what-is-story-bible/jmWepHcQdJetNrE991fjJC)
- [Tool] Sudowrite's newer Draft tool works in Scenes, described as "distinct moments in your story—each one typically encompassing a single location, timeframe, or character context"; its glossary says Scenes replaced the older Beats system. Both doc pages were live, so the product is in transition. — [Sudowrite docs: Scenes / Draft](https://docs.sudowrite.com/using-sudowrite/1ow1qkGqof9rtcyGnrWUBS/scenes--draft/49p5MTVxTKkVFEC5rVUzpY)

**NovelCrafter**
- [Tool] A scene beat is an instruction read alongside Codex context and existing prose. What to put in it: "Include the setting, time of day, and the characters that are present. Give one of the characters (probably the POV character) a goal." "If you want the character to do something specific, then mention it in the beat." Add restrictions such as no new characters or a tone to avoid. — [NovelCrafter docs: Crafting beats](https://docs.novelcrafter.com/en/articles/8675715-crafting-beats); [NovelCrafter Beats Cookbook: scene beat](https://www.novelcrafter.com/courses/beats-cookbook/scene-beat)
- [Tool] Scope: one beat should cover one moment or a short sequence; "If you pack an entire scene into a single beat, the AI might rush the pacing." Name the characters ("If you know the characters that are present, then mention them!"), which also stops the model inventing new ones. "All mentions within the beat/chat message AND text prior to the beat are added to the context." Keep Codex entries concise because every mentioned entry is pulled in whole. "Scene summaries prior to the scene you are currently working on are used as the context for the AI in the system prompt." The general-purpose prompt defaults to 400 words. — [NovelCrafter: Codex context in prompting](https://www.novelcrafter.com/help/faq/ai-and-prompting/codex-context-in-prompting); [NovelCrafter: improving AI output for scene beats](https://www.novelcrafter.com/courses/ultimate-beginners-guide/improving-ai-output-for-scene-beats)

**Research pipelines**
- [Research] Re3 (2022): a structured overarching plan, then each passage generated by "repeatedly injecting contextual information from both the plan and current story state" into the prompt; candidates reranked for plot coherence and premise relevance; the chosen one edited for long-range factual consistency. — [arXiv 2210.06774](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06774v2)
- [Research] DOC (2023): a brief outline refined into a detailed hierarchical one, plus a controller that keeps each passage faithful to its outline item. — [arXiv 2212.10077](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.10077v3)
- [Research] Dramatron (Mirowski et al., arXiv 2022, CHI 2023): hierarchical prompt chaining from a log line to title and characters, then story beats, location descriptions and dialogue, which the authors say gives greater coherence than flat sequential generation; evaluated with 15 theatre and film professionals who co-wrote scripts with it. — [arXiv 2209.14958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.14958v1)
- [Research] Agents' Room (2024/ICLR 2025): specialised planning agents and writing agents, an orchestrator, and a shared scratchpad that accumulates their outputs. — [arXiv 2410.02603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02603v2)
- [Research] ConStory-Bench (2026): consistency errors are mostly factual and temporal, cluster mid-narrative and grow linearly with length, which argues for carrying explicit facts and timeline in the brief. — [arXiv 2603.05890](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05890)

**Vendor guidance on arranging the brief**
- [Vendor] Long reference material goes at the top in tagged documents, the ask at the end ("up to 30 percent" better in tests on complex multi-document inputs); separate instructions, context, examples and inputs with XML tags; ask for relevant quotes first on long-document tasks. — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

### Inferences
- Common elements across the tools and pipelines, with the question's own list mapped on: premise or standing synopsis (Sudowrite Synopsis; Re3 premise); style and genre reference (Sudowrite Style); POV character and goal (NovelCrafter); setting and time of day (NovelCrafter; Dramatron locations); characters present, named explicitly, each with a concise entry (both tools); what must happen as ordered beats (both tools; DOC outline item); restrictions and what must not appear or be revealed (NovelCrafter restrictions); continuity facts and story state (Re3 story state; ConStory findings); prior-scene summaries and the previous scene's ending (NovelCrafter summaries; Re3); target length (NovelCrafter 400-word default; Sudowrite about 200 words per beat). Tone and "what each present character wants right now" are not explicit fields in either tool; they come from the craft sources in section 4 and are worth adding.
- Granularity matters: both tools size a beat at roughly 200-400 words of prose and warn that a whole scene in one instruction gets rushed. For a single-call scene, the brief should either list several beats or state plainly which one moment the scene dramatises and what is summary.
- Explicit naming does two jobs: it pulls the right reference entries in, and it stops the model inventing new characters, which is also where the stock-name default would strike.
- Following Anthropic's ordering, a brief for Claude would put the standing bible and recent-scene tail first in tagged blocks, then the scene-specific contract, then the actual instruction to write, last.

### Gaps
- NovelAI (Lorebook, Memory, Author's Note) documentation was not retrieved; nothing is cited for it.
- The exact agent roster in Agents' Room and the prompt templates of Re3 and DOC could not be read (arXiv blocked). Dramatron's qualitative findings from the 15 professionals were not retrieved beyond "participants generally felt a collaborative interaction".
- Sudowrite's own list of what makes a "strong beat" was truncated in the search result.
- Only one 2026 long-form generation paper (ConStory-Bench) and one planning paper (PLOTTER) were surfaced; I did not find a 2025-2026 pipeline paper evaluated with a current Claude model.

## 6. NPC and companion characterisation over a long serial

### Takeaway
Anthropic's documented advice is short: put a detailed character description (personality, background, traits, quirks) in the system prompt and list likely scenarios with expected responses. Research confirms the underlying risk, that models drift from an assigned persona over a long conversation (measurably within eight rounds on 2023-24 models, attributed to attention decay), and Anthropic's own 2026 interpretability work finds that emotionally heavy and philosophical conversations pull a model off its default persona. The practical counters on current Claude models are re-injecting character reminders in the user turn and keeping per-character sheets specific enough to act on.

### Cited Findings
- [Vendor] "When setting up the character, provide detailed information about the personality, background, and any specific traits or quirks. This helps the model better emulate and generalize the character's traits." and "Provide a list of common scenarios and expected responses in your prompts. This 'trains' Claude to handle diverse situations without breaking character." — [Increase output consistency: Keep Claude in character](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/increase-consistency)
- [Vendor] In very long conversations, "inject what were previously prefilled-assistant reminders into the user turn", or hydrate context through tools or at compaction. — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Research, COLM 2024; old models: LLaMA2-chat-70B, GPT-3.5] Li et al., "Measuring and Controlling Instruction (In)Stability in Language Model Dialogs" (also titled "Persona Drift"): self-chats between two instructed chatbots showed "significant instruction drift within eight rounds of conversations"; the authors attribute it to attention decay over long exchanges and propose split-softmax, a training-free method requiring model internals. — [arXiv 2402.10962](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10962); [Northeastern coverage](https://news.northeastern.edu/?p=230037)
- [Research, Anthropic, Jan 2026] "The Assistant Axis": persona drift is measured as movement along a direction in activation space. In simulated multi-turn chats, coding conversations stayed stable while "therapy-like and philosophical discussions consistently caused models to drift" from the default persona. The mitigation studied, activation capping, is an internal intervention, not a prompting technique. — [Anthropic research: assistant axis](https://www.anthropic.com/research/assistant-axis); [arXiv 2601.10387](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.10387)
- [Research, 2024] CharacterEval (Chinese role-play benchmark) scores four dimensions with thirteen metrics, including character consistency and knowledge hallucination (inventing facts about the character). Its trained reward model CharacterRM tracked human judgement better than GPT-4 did, and specialised role-play models generally beat general-purpose LLMs. — [arXiv 2401.01275](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.01275v1)
- [Research, Findings of ACL 2024] RoleLLM/RoleBench builds profiles for 100 roles and a 168,093-sample character-level benchmark for eliciting and evaluating role-play. — [arXiv 2310.00746](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.00746v3)
- [Craft] A scene should be "a compelling collision of competing agendas" (Matt Bird), which requires each non-player character to carry an agenda of their own. — [Kill Zone summary of Bird's checklist](https://killzoneblog.com/2023/09/the-ultimate-story-checklist.html)
- [Craft] Dungeon World's GM principles, written for human game masters, include "Make a move that follows" and "Think dangerous"; GM moves take "an element of the fiction" and bring it "to bear against the characters". — [Dungeon World: The GM (Roll20 compendium)](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Rules:The%20GM)
- [Practitioner, vendor and hobbyist blogs; anecdotal, and which page makes which claim was not verified] AI-novel tool write-ups report that secondary characters with sparse character sheets show voice drift across chapters, and that a single context window stops holding voice at around 80,000 words. — [Noveble blog on continuity](https://noveble-www.pages.dev/blogs/novel-continuity-context-management); [bookwriter.vip continuity research page](https://www.bookwriter.vip/research/continuity-memory)

### Inferences
- A character sheet that "drives behaviour" should hold what the vendor tip lists (personality, background, quirks) plus what the craft sources require for conflict: a current want, a fear, something they will not say, how they regard the protagonist right now, and two or three sample lines in their voice. The sample lines are multishot examples and carry the same over-indexing risk, so they should be rotated or marked as register samples rather than lines to reuse.
- Anthropic's "list of common scenarios and expected responses" translates to fiction as short "when X, this character does Y" entries (when flattered, when contradicted, when asked about the secret). These give the model licence for the NPC to refuse or disagree, which a default-agreeable assistant persona otherwise smooths away.
- Drift research on old models and the Assistant Axis result both suggest the risk is highest in long, emotionally intimate exchanges, which is exactly what companion scenes are. Re-stating each present character's current state and want in every scene brief (rather than relying on a sheet loaded once) is the prompt-level equivalent of the documented user-turn reminder.
- Since benchmark work found trained reward models judged character consistency better than a general LLM, an LLM "voice checker" should be given the sheet and asked narrow questions (would this character use this word, did they volunteer something they would hide) rather than a general "is this in character?".

### Gaps
- No measured study of persona or voice drift in long fiction or role-play on Claude 4.x/5.x was found. Li et al. used 2023-era models; the Assistant Axis work concerns drift from the assistant persona, not fidelity to an assigned fictional character.
- Character-LLM (arXiv 2310.10158) surfaced only as a citation; its method and results were not retrieved. Anthropic's 2025 "persona vectors" paper was not confirmed beyond an aggregator mention and is not cited.
- I found no research on keeping multiple NPC voices distinct within one model context, and no sourced practitioner template for relationship-state tracking.

## 7. Second-person and interactive narration

### Takeaway
The firmest sourced rules come from tabletop game-mastering texts and from practitioner GM prompts, which agree: narrate the world and the non-player characters only, never the player's character; end each turn at a point of decision; make every response change the situation; and keep dice and arithmetic outside the model so the model only dramatises the result. For turning a roll into fiction, Kowal's "yes, but / no, and" gives the most usable grammar. I found no reliable source on how long a turn should be or on weaving player input into prose without parroting it.

### Cited Findings
- [Craft] Dungeon World GM rules: "When you describe the situation, always end with 'What do you do?'" The GM principles include "Begin and end with the fiction", "Make a move that follows" and "Never speak the name of your move". "Your move should always follow from the fiction." Play is "never a monologue; it's always a conversation." — [Dungeon World: The GM (Roll20 compendium)](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Rules:The%20GM); [Dungeon World: Playing the Game (Roll20 compendium)](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Playing%20the%20Game)
- [Craft] Outcome grammar: "Yes-but, movement towards the goal, but with a consequence. No-and, movement away from the goal, with a consequence." — [Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/?p=22664)
- [Practitioner; several prompts surfaced by one search, quote-to-page mapping not individually verified] Recurring rules in published AI game-master prompts: "No God-Modding: NEVER speak, act, or think on behalf of the player character. You control the world and NPCs ONLY."; "The final lines of every output should leave the player at a decision point, a moment demanding reaction, or a pause in which they can speak or act. Never end mid-action in a way that requires you to assume the player's next move."; every response must "introduce new information, shift the situation, or escalate tension"; and in one open-source engine "the AI handles narrative, but a deterministic engine handles all the math." — [The Prompt Index: Dungeons and Dragons prompt](https://www.thepromptindex.com/prompt.php?id=778); [AI as role master](https://daviddetorres.github.io/AI-as-role-master/); [The Infinite Narrative Engine](https://skiticusprime.itch.io/he-infinite-narrative-engine-advanced-rpg-framework-for-ai); [flower-engine prompt docs](https://www.mintlify.com/ritz541/flower-engine/api/prompt)
- [Practitioner] A reported failure of unstructured solo play with Claude as GM: after a few turns the model would "forget your inventory and hallucinate plotlines", which is the motivation for external state. The quote came from one of several Reddit snapshots returned by the same search; the link is the most likely match and was not opened. — [Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1s69mwk/snapshots/2026-04-04T00%3A07%3A23.107607Z)
- [Vendor] Reply length is controlled by the prompt, not by effort: "Effort barely changes how long the model's replies are. To change the length and tone of a reply, use your prompt." Fable 5.2 also applies rules "as written", so a rule about what the narrator may not do should name the exact actions it covers. — [Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)

### Inferences
- "Never speak the name of your move" is the tabletop statement of the rule that mechanics stay out of the prose: the dice result is shown as what happens in the world, not announced. Combined with an external dice engine, the narrator's brief only needs the outcome tier and what it must change.
- A three-tier roll maps onto Kowal's grammar: full success = yes (and, late in an arc, "yes, and"); partial = "yes, but" (the goal advances and something is lost, owed or revealed); failure = "no, and" (the goal recedes and the situation worsens in a new direction). Each tier therefore turns the scene in McKee's sense, which answers "dramatise the outcome so it changes the scene".
- "Handing off without a menu" follows from the Dungeon World rule and the decision-point prompt: stop at a moment of pressure in the fiction (a question asked of the character, a door opening, a hand on a sword) rather than a list of options. The stop is the prompt. Whether to append a literal "What do you do?" is a house-style choice; the tabletop text does it every time.
- Because Claude follows prohibitions literally on current models, "never act for the player" needs a stated exception for the action the player just declared, or the narrator may refuse to dramatise the player's own stated move.
- For weaving player input, the nearest sourced principle is Palahniuk's: render the declared action through its observable consequences and the world's reaction rather than restating the player's sentence.

### Gaps
- No reliable source was found for recommended narration length per turn, for weaving real player input into prose without echoing it, or for second-person prose technique specifically. These remain practitioner judgement.
- The Powered-by-the-Apocalypse 7-9 "success at a cost" wording and Blades in the Dark's position/effect and consequence rules were not retrieved, so the partial-success mapping above rests on Kowal alone.
- A 2025 arXiv paper, "Static Vs. Agentic Game Master AI for Facilitating Solo Role-Playing Experiences" (2502.19519), surfaced by title only; its findings were not read and it may contain measured evidence for this section.
- The practitioner prompts are hobbyist artefacts of unknown testing depth; one result in the same search was an "uncensored" jailbreak-style preset and was excluded.

## 8. Multi-agent and multi-pass writing set-ups with Claude

### Takeaway
Anthropic documents draft → review → refine as its standard chain, says current models orchestrate subagents natively, and warns about three costs that apply directly to a writer's room: subagents can be a large share of session cost, reviewer agents' conditions are obeyed closely and accumulate, and rigid style rules make Fable 5.2's prose choppy. Practitioner frameworks for novels in Claude Code all split roles (planner, writer, continuity pass, developmental editor, line editor) and keep state in files; I found design choices meant to stop editors rewriting the voice, but no first-hand measured account of critics flattening prose.

### Cited Findings

**Vendor guidance**
- [Vendor] "Claude's latest models orchestrate subagents natively." Guidance for when to use them: "Use subagents when tasks can run in parallel, require isolated context, or involve independent workstreams that don't need to share state. For simple tasks, sequential operations, single-file edits, or tasks where you need to maintain context across steps, work directly rather than delegating." — [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- [Vendor] "With adaptive thinking and subagent orchestration, Claude handles most multistep reasoning internally. **Research on multi-agent fiction**

- [Research] CritiCS uses several critics plus a leader over multiple rounds on plan and then text. ### Inferences
- The "critic sands the prose bland" pitfall is not directly documented, but three sourced facts make it predictable: LLM judges carry a slop bias and stylistic preferences; LLMs "don't inherently know what aspects of the writing needs improvement"; and Fable 5.2 obeys reviewer conditions and style rules closely. An unconstrained "improve this" pass will therefore move a draft toward the judge's defaults.
- Design consequences that follow from the sources: (1) the critic reports, it does not rewrite; (2) its rubric is concrete and checkable (does the scene turn, which of the seven edit categories occur and where, which phrases repeat from the last N scenes, any continuity contradiction with quoted evidence, as in ConStory-Checker); (3) the original writer revises only flagged spans, keeping the rest; (4) finding and filtering are separate steps, as Anthropic recommends for code review; (5) reviewer notes are not carried forward as standing rules, to avoid "caution piling up".
- Cost control: continuity and slop/repetition checks are well-specified tasks suited to low-effort subagents or plain scripts; the drafting call is where effort and the best model matter. Planning in the main context and drafting in the same context avoids paying to re-brief a writer subagent with the whole bible.
- Isolated-context subagents are a real advantage for the critic (it sees the scene as a reader would, without the plan's intentions) and a real cost for the writer (voice lives in the accumulated context and exemplars). This argues for "writer in the main thread, checker in a subagent" rather than the reverse.
- All the practitioner frameworks converge on files as memory and on injecting only the relevant slice of history per scene, which matches Anthropic's state-management advice and the long-context ordering rule.

### Gaps
- No first-hand, measured practitioner account of over-editing or blandness from critic loops with Claude was found; the search surfaced designs, not post-mortems. Jason Craft's "Style Engineering" post is the most likely source and should be read directly.
- No cost figures (tokens or dollars per chapter) for any Claude multi-agent fiction workflow were found. Anthropic's only quantified statements are for coding (for example, output tokens rising about 20-30% with certain added instructions).
- None of the multi-agent fiction papers used a current Claude model, and their evaluations do not separate the effect of the critic from the effect of planning.
- The "Claude Book" perplexity gate is described only in a summary; how it is thresholded, and whether it correlates with reader preference, is unknown.

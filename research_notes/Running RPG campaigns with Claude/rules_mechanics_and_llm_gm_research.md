## 1. What has research found about LLMs as game masters?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on one architecture: the model narrates and interprets, and code owns dice, rules and state. Studies that add function calling or a symbolic state layer report better consistency, and every long-horizon benchmark found (2025–2026) shows fluent prose with state errors that grow with length: the best model on one narrative benchmark kept only 42% of runs conflict-free after 20 turns. Recurring failure modes are state drift, hallucinated mechanics, giving in to the player's framing, positivity bias and stalling when asked to roll its own dice.

### Cited Findings

**Foundational datasets and assistants (2022–2023)**
- **[measured]** Callison-Burch et al., "Dungeons and Dragons as a Dialog Challenge for Artificial Intelligence", EMNLP 2022, pp. 9379–9393. Dataset: nearly 900 games, 7,000 players, 800,000 dialogue turns, 500,000 dice rolls, 58 million words, automatically annotated with partial game-state information. Task framed as next-utterance generation plus game-state prediction from dialogue history. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.637); [arXiv 2210.07109](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07109)
- **[measured]** In that paper a fine-tuned model reached 58% joint accuracy on game-state tracking, and the authors concluded that accurately tracking the full game state may require additional machinery beyond a fine-tuned LLM. — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.637.pdf)
- **[measured] (unverified secondary)** A third-party review of the same paper reports that conditioning on state gave a 6.75% higher rate of "making sense", 4% more specific responses and a 0.37-point increase in interestingness; these are dialogue-quality figures, not state-tracking figures. — [Liner review](https://liner.com/review/dungeons-and-dragons-as-dialog-challenge-for-artificial-intelligence)
- **[measured]** FIREBALL: Zhu, Aggarwal, Feng, Martin, Callison-Burch, ACL 2023 (long papers), pp. 4171–4193. D&D actual play from Discord with true game state taken from the Avrae bot (earlier work used heuristically built state). Nearly 25,000 unique sessions and 153,829 turns. Reported results: using Avrae state information improves generated narration on both automated metrics and human quality judgments, and LLMs can generate executable Avrae commands, particularly after fine-tuning. Exact metric values not retrieved. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.229); [arXiv 2305.01528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01528v3); [dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/lara-martin/FIREBALL)
- **[measured]** CALYPSO: Zhu, Martin, Head, Callison-Burch, AIIDE 2023 (vol. 19, pp. 380–390). Three LLM-powered interfaces for preparing and running focused monster encounters, studied with 71 D&D players. Findings: LLMs are capable brainstorming partners; DMs used output both as low-fidelity ideas to develop and as high-fidelity descriptions to read out with light edits; the model could fill gaps with thematically relevant common-sense knowledge but only when explicitly prompted to; framed as assistance that leaves the human DM in creative control, not replacement. — [AAAI/AIIDE](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/27534); [arXiv 2308.07540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07540v1)
- **[measured / position]** "Skill Check: Some Considerations on the Evaluation of Gamemastering Models for Role-playing Games", Góngora, Chiruzzo, Méndez, Gervás; arXiv Sept 2023, GALA 2023 (proceedings 2024). Argues human judges are a weak evaluation because ratings are subjective and swayed by fluency; proposes three test categories that apply regardless of theme or technology: commonsense reasoning, tracking items in the world, and designing maps coherently. Tested ChatGPT, Bard and OpenAssistant as out-of-the-box GMs. Per-model results not retrieved. — [arXiv 2309.13702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.13702v1); [UCM listing](https://produccioncientifica.ucm.es/documentos/65911de0ae63c86e421ba015?lang=fr)

**Function calling and symbolic state (2024–2026)**
- **[measured]** Song, Zhu, Callison-Burch, "You Have Thirteen Hours in Which to Solve the Labyrinth: Enhancing AI Game Masters with Function Calling", Wordplay Workshop 2024 (at ACL), arXiv 11 Sept 2024. Testbed: "Jim Henson's Labyrinth: The Adventure Game", with the AI taking all GM duties. Game-specific controls and state are exposed as functions; conditions vary which function sets are available (for example a condition with all state plus only the dice-roll function). Evaluated by human evaluation and unit tests. Reported conclusion: function calling improves narrative quality and state-update consistency, the dice-roll function and the state functions interact, and the combination performs best. — [arXiv 2409.06949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06949); [workshop PDF](https://wordplay-workshop.github.io/wordplay2024/pdfs/11.pdf); [UPenn PDF](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~ccb/publications/labyrinth-ai-gm.pdf)
- **[measured]** The same paper names a "dice roll deadlock": without a dice-roll function the GM does not produce a roll result and waits indefinitely instead of advancing the game; adding the dice function resolves it. It also reports that the GM robustly rejects invalid player attempts in the function-calling setting. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06949); [workshop PDF](https://wordplay-workshop.github.io/wordplay2024/pdfs/11.pdf)
- **[measured] (unverified secondary)** One blog cites p = 0.0064 for consistency and p = 0.7400 for reliability from this study; not confirmed against the paper. — [dunia.gg](https://dunia.gg/blog/ai-game-master)
- **[measured, exploratory]** "World-State Transformations for Neuro-symbolic Interactive Storytelling", Góngora, Chiruzzo, Méndez, Gervás; arXiv 23 May 2026, with an ICCC 2026 version, building on the PAYADOR architecture. Each turn the LLM receives the symbolic world state, the player's free-text input and a list of available transformations and suggests which to apply; the symbolic system executes them with consistency checks. Tested with Llama 3 70B and Gemini 1.5 Flash in English and Spanish. Authors report that transformations appear to keep world-state consistency while still encouraging creative input, improve traceability of LLM errors and enable logic checks. Aggregator sites claim significant coherence gains in user studies; the abstract does not. — [arXiv 2605.24719](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24719); [ICCC26 paper](https://computationalcreativity.net/iccc26/papers/ICCC26_paper_156.pdf)
- **[measured]** "Static Vs. Agentic Game Master AI for Facilitating Solo Role-Playing Experiences" (ChatRPG), Jørgensen, Tharmabalan, Aslan, Hansen, Merritt (Aalborg University), arXiv Feb 2025 (revised March 2025); peer-review status not confirmed. v1 is prompt engineering only; v2 uses the ReAct framework with two agents, a Narrator that writes the story and an Archivist that records and retrieves game state and interaction history. A pilot had 8 participants and the comparative study 12, using the Player Experience Inventory plus interviews. v2 was preferred and rated higher on perceived intelligence, flow and immersion; the abstract says it "significantly" improved modularity and game experience including immersion and curiosity. — [arXiv 2502.19519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19519v2); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.19519v1)
- **[measured] (unverified secondary)** A blog reports the figures as immersion 1.64 (v1) vs 2.42 (v2), p = 0.034, and curiosity 1.83 vs 2.57, p = 0.047. — [LitRPG Reads](https://litrpgreads.com/blog/rpg/static-vs-agentic-game-master-ai-which-is-revolutionizing-solo-roleplaying)
- **[measured, student project]** ChatRPG v3 / SENNA, "Narrative Adherence in LLM-driven Games", Jørgensen and Tharmabalan, Aalborg University, 2025. Five agents (Scribe, Examiner, Navigator, Narrator, Archivist) and a narrative graph keep play aligned with a pre-written adventure module. Finding: structured redirection rooted in world logic (NPC influence, in-world consequences) instead of rejecting player actions outright enhanced narrative coherence without reducing immersion or autonomy; players preferred strategies that preserved immersion and respected agency. Effect sizes not retrieved. — [AAU PDF](https://projekter.aau.dk/projekter/files/784680954/Narrative_Adherence_in_LLM_driven_Games.pdf); [AAU listing](https://projekter.aau.dk/narrative-adherence-in-llm-driven-games-f231bf04.html)
- **[measured]** "TRPG Game Mastering Using LLM-Based Multi-Agent System", EMNLP 2025 (listed as a workshop paper, Wordplay). Specialised agents give feedback to refine GM responses. With experienced TRPG players the multi-agent approach improved scenario progression but increased rule violations and spoilers, which the authors tie to inappropriate feedback from the agents, and slower responses hurt conversational smoothness. — [Underline listing](https://underline.io/lecture/137732-trpg-game-mastering-using-llm-based-multi-agent-system); [workshop PDF](https://wordplay-workshop.github.io/pdfs/20.pdf)
- **[measured]** "Enforcing Narrative Reliability and Epistemic Pacing in LLM-Driven Detective Games via Structured Knowledge Trees", Rahmati and Zhao (University of Calgary), arXiv Sept 2026. A structured knowledge tree with a three-agent pipeline separating knowledge retrieval, dialogue generation and response verification, tested in a user study on an interrogation game. Reported: 64.78% reduction in critical hallucinations and complete prevention of premature narrative disclosure, at a usability cost from forced conversational reveals. In the LLM-only version 10 of 33 participants forced the suspect agent into a full confession because it sycophantically accepted the player's premise. — [arXiv 2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.23043)
- **[system paper]** "CriticaLLM: Using LLMs as Autonomous Game Master for Tabletop Role-Playing Games", Berlaimont et al., arXiv 15 Oct 2026. A fully automated D&D GM without fine-tuning, in which an LLM-as-judge component decides whether a player action is allowed given both the rules and story coherence. It defines sycophancy as preferring to align with the user over the truth or predefined rules, and its player feedback says the GM often failed to understand the intention behind actions, which kept the story from adapting over the long term. No study size retrieved. — [arXiv 2610.21525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21525); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.21525)

**Benchmarks of rule adherence and long-horizon consistency (2025–2026)**
- **[measured]** "Setting the DC: Tool-Grounded D&D Simulations to Test LLM Agents" (the "D&D Agents" simulator), Zeng, Li, Xi, Zhu, Ammanabrolu (UC San Diego); OpenReview lists it as a NeurIPS 2025 workshop paper. LLMs play DM, players and monsters in combat and must act through a function API for movement, attacks, spellcasting and state queries, so decisions are grounded in mechanics and traces are auditable. Scored on six axes: Function Usage, Parameter Fidelity, Acting Quality, Tactical Optimality, State Tracking, Function Efficiency. Main finding: for all models hallucination errors increased with scenario length. Limited to combat. — [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=3Op7kJOvaD); [NeurIPS listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/loc/san-diego/128312)
- **[measured, via press]** Coverage of that study names Claude 3.5 Haiku, a GPT-4-family model (GPT-4o in one report, GPT-4 in another) and DeepSeek-V3 as the models, gives examples of late-game errors (attacking already-defeated enemies, ignoring critical status effects), says a rules engine enforced the rules to curb hallucination, and says Claude 3.5 Haiku was judged the most reliable of the three with DeepSeek-V3 trailing. Per-model numbers were not retrieved and the ranking is not confirmed against the paper. — [TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2026-01-ai-dungeons-dragons-term-decision.html); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/researchers-are-using-dungeons-dragons-to-find-the-breaking-points-of-major-ai-models/); [Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/dnd/artificial-intelligence-game)
- **[measured]** NCP-Bench: "Can LLM Agents Stick to the Script? A Benchmark for Long-Horizon Consistency in Interactive Narratives", Ma et al., arXiv Aug 2026 (an ICML 2026 poster under the subtitle "Modeling Commitment in Interactive Narratives"). 100 narrative environments built from movie synopses, each with a structured specification an automated checker verifies during play. Six narrator models (GPT-5.2, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3.2, Qwen3-235B-A22B, Kimi-K2.5, Grok-4.1-Fast). Results: the best model, GPT-5.2, kept 42% of runs conflict-free after 20 turns; 40% to 68% of interactions across models ended in factual conflict; only isolated runs satisfied all commitments within 100 turns; high linguistic quality did not guarantee commitment preservation. — [arXiv 2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160); [ICML poster](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/64786); [secondary summary](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/ncp-bench-gpt-52-keeps-42-of-stories-intact-at-20-turns)
- **[measured, via review]** In NCP-Bench a memory-augmented agent reduced commitment violations but did not increase full successes; adversarial players made results worse, but cooperative play still ended in conflict in the majority of runs; "Ignoring Player Input" is one of its narrator failure categories. Authors' stated limits: linear narratives, and LLM auditors that can err on ambiguous text. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160); [Hugging Face paper page](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.08160)
- **[measured]** CoC-Seduce: "Seduced by the Narrative: Assessing Rule Adherence in Semi-Open Textual Sandboxes", Chen, Shen, Guo, Zhou, arXiv 2 July 2026. An adversarial benchmark on Call of Cthulhu mechanics where players speak freely but the adjudicator must apply fixed rules. GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 3.5 Flash generated 5,376 adversarial samples across 4 world settings and 16 skill categories; 20 adjudicator models were tested (one version's table caption says 22). The attack is "rhetorical injection": narrative framing such as pseudo-logical reasoning and authoritative coercion. Findings: neither model scale nor explicit reasoning reliably conferred robust adjudication; pseudo-logic was the dominant attack vector; a robust adjudicator should hold a 0% failure rate regardless of how good the player's narration is. Per-model failure rates not retrieved. — [arXiv 2607.02802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02802); [project repo](https://github.com/answerrtx/CoC-Seduce)
- **[measured]** DungeonBench: "A Benchmark for Rules-Rich Tactical Reasoning in Dungeons & Dragons Combat", arXiv July 2026. A rules engine over the 2014 SRD (561 combatants including 93 spellcasters, 229 spells, 275 items) enumerates the legal state-dependent options and the model ranks them; rule text is available through lookup tools; effects that need DM adjudication are excluded. Headline: frontier models often win single encounters, but linked "encounter days" (persistent hit points, spell slots, consumables, rest timing) expose weaknesses in resource budgeting, rest timing and rule-aware discipline. Per-model numbers not retrieved. — [arXiv 2607.29577](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.29577)
- **[measured]** TextQuests (Phan, Mazeika, Zou, Hendrycks; Center for AI Safety), arXiv July 2025 (v3 Aug 2025): 25 Infocom text adventures run through Jericho with the full history kept in context. Without clues no model completed any of the 25 games. A review of the paper gives the top model, GPT-5, about 70.0% progress with official clues versus 37.8% without. This measures models as players, not as GMs. — [arXiv 2507.23701](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23701v3); [Hugging Face blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/textquests); [Liner review](https://liner.com/review/textquests-how-good-are-llms-at-textbased-video-games)

**Narrative quality, emergence and authored structure**
- **[measured]** Peng, Quaye et al. (Microsoft Research), "Player-Driven Emergence in LLM-Driven Game Narrative", IEEE Conference on Games 2024. 28 gamers played the mystery text adventure "Dejaboom!" with GPT-4 NPCs; GPT-4 converted logs into narrative graphs. Players created emergent story nodes absent from the original design that looked fun and engaging, and the players who produced the most emergent nodes were those who enjoy discovery, exploration and experimentation. — [arXiv 2404.17027](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17027)

- **[measured]** Positivity bias in LLM stories. One study found over 51% of GPT-4 stories follow a "Man in a Hole" arc versus 30.7% of human stories, with flatter emotional intensity and fewer setbacks. A network study found LLM story character relationships had mean edge weight 0.235 to 0.659 against −0.061 for human stories, a strong bias to positive relationships. A games paper (Cala et al., ICCE 2023) found ChatGPT biased toward positive endings even when told to write neutral ones. — [arXiv 2407.13248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13248); [arXiv 2510.18932](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18932v1); [ICCE 2023 paper](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/1346080)
- **[doctrine, earlier research]** An earlier design paper on procedural interactive stories ("To Create a Game Master: A Decalogue for Procedural Generation of Interactive Stories"; year not confirmed) argues that success should not be consistently expected from the player and that failure should not mean the end of the story. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/352358243_To_Create_a_Game_Master_A_Decalogue_for_Procedural_Generation_of_Interactive_Stories)

### Inferences
- The consistent result across Labyrinth, PAYADOR, D&D Agents, DungeonBench and the detective study is that reliability comes from narrowing what the model can do to a set of validated operations, not from better prompting. The model picks a tool or transformation; code executes it and can refuse.
- Fluency is not evidence of consistency (NCP-Bench, Skill Check). An AI-run campaign therefore needs a mechanical check of state, because neither the player nor the model will reliably notice drift from the prose.
- The "dice roll deadlock" and the practitioner reports of fudged rolls point the same way: dice must be an external call whose result the model receives, never something the model is asked to imagine or wait for.
- Adding agents is not automatically an improvement. One study (ChatRPG v2) found a narrator/archivist split helped; another (EMNLP 2025) found reviewer agents raised rule violations and spoilers and slowed the game. A small number of roles with a hard code boundary appears safer than many LLM critics.
- Rule adjudication under persuasion is an open weakness (CoC-Seduce, the detective game's 10 of 33 confessions). Rules that the player can argue with in prose should be decided before the roll and enforced by code, so a persuasive paragraph cannot change the odds.
- Positivity bias means an LLM narrator left to itself will under-deliver setbacks. Mechanics that hand the narrator a mandated negative or mixed outcome (tiers, costs, clocks) compensate for a measured tendency, not just a stylistic one.
- Redirection through the fiction (SENNA) fits with the tabletop doctrine in section 5: answer an off-plan action with an in-world consequence, not a refusal.

### Gaps
- No full texts were readable, so the Labyrinth paper's per-condition human-evaluation scores and unit-test pass rates, FIREBALL's metric values, ChatRPG's tables, CoC-Seduce's per-model failure rates, DungeonBench's and D&D Agents' per-model scores could not be reported. The two sets of p-values quoted above are from blogs only.
- No paper with "Triangulating" in the title relevant to game mastering was found; the brief's item was treated as Peng et al. 2024.
- A title "Guiding, Not Railroading: Design and Evaluation of a Multi-Agent System for Narrative Redirection in Role-playing Games" appears in the ACM Digital Library (doi 10.1145/3742413.3789218) and is probably the published form of SENNA, but this was not confirmed.
- Leads seen by title only, not summarised: "Large Language Models and Games: A Survey and Roadmap" (arXiv 2402.18659); "Co-Creativity at the Table: A Qualitative Analysis of Creative Interactions in the Podcast 'Adventure AI'" (arXiv 2606.18010); "Towards LLM-Agents That Play Dungeons & Dragons Using Iterative Prompting" (CEUR-WS vol. 4097); "First Steps Towards Overhearing LLM Agents: A Case Study With Dungeons & Dragons Gameplay" (arXiv 2505.22809); "A Text-to-Game Engine for UGC-Based Role-Playing Games" (arXiv 2407.08195); SNAP, a plan-driven redirection framework (arXiv 2601.11529).
- No benchmark was found that scores an LLM GM on a full non-combat session (pacing, fairness of difficulty, honouring of earlier consequences). The existing benchmarks cover combat tactics, rule adjudication under attack, or adherence to a fixed script.

## 2. LLMs and randomness / arithmetic: are they biased dice and unreliable bookkeepers?

### Takeaway
Yes on both counts, with measurements. In the largest study found, 10 of 11 models passed none of 15 distributions when each sample was a separate request, and step-by-step state tracking degrades with length even when the procedure is fully specified. The implication the authors themselves draw is to sample with an external tool; the same logic applies to totals, levels and any running state.

### Cited Findings
- **[measured]** Zhao, Du, Wang (Harvard), "Large Language Models Are Bad Dice Players: LLMs Struggle to Generate Random Numbers from Statistical Distributions", arXiv 8 Jan 2026, accepted at ACL 2026. 11 models across 15 distributions under two protocols: batch generation (1,000 samples in one response) and independent requests (1,000 stateless calls). Batch generation reached a 7% median pass rate; with independent requests 10 of 11 models passed none of the distributions. — [arXiv 2601.05414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05414); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1051/); [summary](https://paperswithcode.co/paper/2601.05414)
- **[measured]** The same paper attributes the better batch result to within-context self-correction; removing the shared context pushes models back onto systematic internal biases. It shows downstream harm (answer-position bias when generating multiple-choice questions despite instructions to randomise; demographic skew in constrained prompts) and concludes current LLMs lack a functional internal sampler, so applications needing statistical guarantees should use external tools. — [arXiv HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05414v3); [alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/replicate/2601.05414)
- **[measured]** Hopkins, Renda, Carbin, "Can LLMs Generate Random Numbers? Evaluating LLM Sampling in Controlled Domains", ICML 2023 workshop: asked for uniform two-digit numbers in [0, 1), the induced distributions were far from uniform. — [paper page](https://people.csail.mit.edu/renda/llm-sampling-paper); [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/icmlw/2023/hopkins2023icmlw-llms)
- **[measured]** Van Koevering and Kleinberg, "How Random is Random? Evaluating the Randomness and Humaness of LLMs' Coin Flips" (arXiv May 2024): GPT-4 and Llama 3 exhibit and exacerbate nearly every human bias tested in coin-flip sequences, while GPT-3.5 behaved more randomly. — [arXiv 2406.00092](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00092v1)
- **[measured]** Coronado-Blázquez (2025), "Deterministic or probabilistic? The psychology of LLMs as random number generators": models often give deterministic responses when asked for random numbers. — [arXiv 2502.19965](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19965)
- **[measured, secondhand]** A news report of a Google DeepMind paper (arXiv 2604.06543, said to be at COLM 2026) says models performed much better when handed random seeds from an external source such as code-generated numbers. Not confirmed against the paper. — [Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/google-deepmind-llms-random-sampling-flaws/)
- **[measured]** Samiei, Mansouri, Soleymani Baghshah (Sharif University of Technology), "The Illusion of Procedural Reasoning: Measuring Long-Horizon FSM Execution in LLMs", arXiv 5 Nov 2025. Models are given an explicit finite-state machine and must step through it turn by turn. Even the largest model (Qwen3-235B) reached only about 50% whole-task accuracy; early local mistakes carry forward (a self-conditioning effect); rule lookup with many branches per state is much harder than a long memory span (2 states with 40 actions is much harder than 40 states with 2 actions); writing out intermediate steps in a scratchpad partly helps. — [arXiv 2511.14777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14777v1)
- **[measured]** Panda et al., "When LLMs Stop Following Steps: A Diagnostic Study of Procedural Execution in Language Models" (arXiv 2605.00817): on a fully specified arithmetic procedure, average first-answer accuracy is 63% at 5 steps and falls as steps increase, and the authors attribute many failures to weak procedural state tracking, not arithmetic slips alone. The endpoint figure was not retrieved. — [arXiv 2605.00817](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.00817)
- **[measured]** A 2025 preprint reports that models make more mistakes once their own earlier errors are in context, and that scaling model size does not fix this. — [arXiv 2509.09677](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.09677v1)
- **[measured]** In game settings the same pattern appears: fine-tuned state tracking at 58% joint accuracy on D&D dialogue (2022), hallucination errors increasing with scenario length for every model in D&D Agents (2025), and 40% to 68% of narrative runs ending in fact conflicts in NCP-Bench (2026). — [EMNLP 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.637.pdf); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=3Op7kJOvaD); [arXiv 2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)
- **[anecdote]** A reviewer who ran D&D in plain ChatGPT reports it forgot their armour class, skipped a class feature until prompted, and over a long session "forgets hit points, skips class features and fudges rolls". — [StoryRoll](https://storyroll.app/blog/tested-every-ai-dungeon-master)

### Inferences
- Roll every die in code. The independent-request result is the relevant one for a campaign, because each roll in play is effectively a separate request with no batch to self-correct against.
- Keep every total in code and have the model read it, never recompute it. XP, levels, attributes, approval, clocks and consequences are running state of exactly the kind the procedural-execution studies show degrading with length and with accumulated errors in context.
- Many-branch rule lookup is the hard case (FSM study), which argues for few outcome branches per decision and for code selecting the branch from the roll.
- Because a model's earlier errors make later ones more likely, a wrong number that reaches the context is worse than a missing one. A validation step that rejects inconsistent state before it is committed is cheaper than repair.
- If the model must contribute variety (names, complications), give it a random draw from code to interpret, on the same logic as the seed result.

### Gaps
- A search summary mentioned a study applying the full NIST randomness test suite in which the best LLM passed fewer than one-third of tests against over 87% for Python's pseudo-random generators; the summary did not attribute it to a specific paper, so it is not cited.
- No study was found that measures an LLM narrator fudging dice toward a preferred story outcome; the evidence for that is anecdotal.
- No measurement specific to current Claude models on dice or long-session bookkeeping was found, other than Claude Sonnet 4.6 serving as an attack generator in CoC-Seduce and Claude 3.5 Haiku being reported (via press) as the most reliable of three models in D&D Agents.

## 3. Engineering patterns: moving dice, numbers, state and rule enforcement into code

### Takeaway
Strong implementations give the model an intent-level interface (roll, look up, propose a change) and let an engine validate, execute, log and refuse. The model decides interpretation, tone, which fictional consequence fits a mechanical result, and which tool to call; code decides randomness, arithmetic, legality and what is stored. Anthropic's guidance supplies the matching primitives: schema-constrained tool inputs, detailed tool descriptions, mistake-proofed parameters, ground truth from tool results at each step, and hooks that run whether or not the model cooperates.

- **[doctrine]** Strict tool use: setting `strict: true` on a tool definition uses grammar-constrained sampling so tool inputs match the JSON Schema; JSON outputs (response format) and strict tool use solve different problems and can be combined. — [Strict tool use](https://platform.claude.com/docs/agents-and-tools/tool-use/strict-tool-use); [Structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)
- **[doctrine]** Tool definitions: "Provide extremely detailed descriptions. This is by far the most important factor in tool performance"; aim for at least 3–4 sentences per tool covering what it does, when to use it and how it behaves, and consider input examples for complex or format-sensitive inputs. — [Define tools](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/define-tools)
- **[doctrine]** "Building effective agents" (Dec 2024): the most successful implementations use simple, composable patterns instead of complex frameworks; the base unit is an LLM augmented with retrieval, tools and memory. Secondary summaries agree that the guide says agents should obtain "ground truth" from the environment at each step (tool results, code execution) to judge progress, that tools should be mistake-proofed ("poka-yoke", with the example of requiring absolute file paths after relative ones caused errors), and that the agent-computer interface deserves as much design effort as a human interface. Exact wording not verified against Anthropic's page. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents); [secondary on poka-yoke](https://www.agentpatterns.ai/tool-engineering/poka-yoke-agent-tools/)
- **[doctrine]** "Writing effective tools for agents": tools are a contract between deterministic systems and non-deterministic agents; build them for agents instead of copying developer APIs; choose which tools to implement and which not to; namespace them; prototype, evaluate and iterate. — [Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)
- **[doctrine, via secondary]** Claude Code hooks are user-defined commands that run at fixed points in the lifecycle and provide deterministic control; instructions in a project file are advisory, hooks execute whether or not the model cooperates; a blocking policy hook must exit with code 2 (exit 1 is treated as a non-blocking error); a pre-tool-use hook that returns deny blocks the tool even in bypass-permissions mode. From a third-party explainer quoting the docs. — [Hooks explainer](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/claude-code-hooks-explained)

### Inferences
- A workable division of labour, assembled from the sources above. **Code decides:** every random result (seeded and logged); all arithmetic and totals; whether a proposed state change is legal; what the stored state is; which outcome tier a roll falls in; the odds or difficulty once inputs are fixed. **The model decides:** how to read the player's free-text intent; which tool to call with which arguments; which of the fictionally appropriate consequences fits a mechanically mandated tier; all prose. **The model proposes and code disposes** for anything that changes stored state.
- Compute the stakes before the roll and hand them to the model (the four pre-computed tiers pattern). This prevents post-hoc rationalising of a result and is the mechanical equivalent of setting position and effect before rolling (section 5).
- A structured record of what a narrated block made true, validated by code, is the common denominator of the Archivist, the PAYADOR transformations, the combat trace fed back each turn and the append-only event log. Two variants exist: the model emits the record alongside the prose and code validates it, or a second pass extracts facts from prose. The first gives a check point before commit; the second inherits the model's extraction errors, as the audit counts above illustrate.
- Schema-constrained output guarantees shape, not truth (a record can match its schema and still carry values that contradict each other or the stored state; a search summary attributed this caution to a third-party guide that could not be pinned to one page), so a separate consistency "check" step that can refuse is still needed. Returning a specific rejection reason to the model matches both RPG-MCP's design and Anthropic's tool-design advice.
- Rule lookups should be a tool, not recall: both Claude Code write-ups independently arrived at "never answer a mechanical question from memory".
- Hooks and deny rules are the right place for rules that must never be broken (for example blocking a commit while checks fail), because prompt instructions are advisory.
- Keeping GM-only information physically separate from player-facing output addresses the information-separation failure the Xebia author observed; a prompt instruction alone did not hold there.
- Event sourcing in the strict sense (state as a projection rebuilt from the log) was not found as a documented, evaluated pattern in LLM-GM projects; RPG-MCP logs events but does not claim replay. It is a reasonable extension of the append-only log, but the evidence base for it here is general software practice, not LLM-GM literature.

### Gaps
- No published comparison of MCP server versus CLI scripts called from Claude Code versus in-process function calling for GM reliability was found.
- Anthropic's own pages could not be opened; the wording on "ground truth" and "poka-yoke" comes from secondary summaries, and the official hooks reference was not retrieved.
- Limitations of Claude structured outputs (which schema features are unsupported) were not retrieved.
- AI Dungeon's and Friends & Fables' internal handling of dice and state was not researched (search budget exhausted).
- The later parts of the Xebia series, which the repository says explain the multi-agent architecture, were not read.

## 4. Which tabletop rule families suit an LLM referee, and why

### Takeaway
No study compares rule families head to head with an LLM referee. The indirect evidence favours systems with few outcome branches, a mandated consequence on every result and little per-entity bookkeeping (Powered by the Apocalypse, Forged in the Dark, Fate, Ironsworn), and it documents repeated failures with crunchy systems (D&D 5e, Call of Cthulhu adjudication) unless a full rules engine does the work. Pure referee-judgment play (FKR) depends on a consistent, trusted referee, which is the property LLMs are measured to lack.

### Cited Findings

**Rules-light narrative families (designer doctrine)**
- **[doctrine]** Powered by the Apocalypse: all resolution has three tiers. "On a 10+, the best happens. On a 7-9, it's good but complicated. On a miss, it's never nothing, it's always something worse." A 7–9 is a hit, not a miss. A miss gives the MC the right to make one move of their own, as hard as they like. The MC is bounded by 3 agenda items, 11 principles and 14 moves. — [Daily Apocalypse: moves and dice](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/6-moves-and-dice); [Indie Game Reading Club](https://indiegamereadingclub.com/indie-game-reading-club/apocalypse-world-2); [Daily Apocalypse: the MC](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/34-mc-apocalypse-worlds-gm)
- **[doctrine]** The Apocalypse World 3rd edition preview gives the canonical shape of a move: "When you do something under fire, or dig in to endure fire, roll+cool. On a 10+, you do it. On a 7-9, you flinch, hesitate, or stall: the MC can offer you a worse outcome, a hard bargain, or an ugly choice." — [lumpley.games preview PDF](https://lumpley.games/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Apocalypse-World-3rd-Ed-Preview-Moves-Ref.pdf)
- **[doctrine / community]** Vincent Baker has said there are no separate hard and soft MC moves, only moves "as hard and direct as you want"; a forum reading stresses that many moves are "choose this many options" at each tier, not a bare good / mixed / bad scale. — [EN World thread](https://www.enworld.org/posts/10005207/)
- **[community]** PbtA rolls are described as good at pushing the narrative forward on their own, especially combined with failing forward, which suits improvisation-heavy play more than prep-heavy play. — [EN World: grade the PbtA system](https://www.enworld.org/threads/grade-the-powered-by-the-apocalypse-pbta-system.700132/)
- **[doctrine]** Forged in the Dark (Blades in the Dark): the GM sets position and effect before the roll; position governs what happens on a failure and effect what happens on a success. On a 6 you get your effect without a position-based consequence, on a 4–5 you get both, on a 1–3 only the consequence. A devil's bargain gives a bonus die, is always a free choice and takes effect regardless of the roll; the alternative bonus is pushing yourself for 2 stress. A resistance roll reduces the severity of a consequence at a stress cost of 6 minus the highest die. — [community play aid](https://community.bladesinthedark.com/t/notecard-play-aid-getting-a-handle-on-position-effect/1169); [Blades cheat sheet](https://www.thealexandrian.net/creations/bladesinthedark/bladesinthedark-cheat-sheet-v3.pdf); [resistance and armor](https://keeper.farirpgs.com/resources/one-seven-design/blades-in-the-dark/resistance-and-armor); [action roll](https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Action%20Roll)
- **[doctrine]** Fate Core: four outcomes instead of pass/fail. Fail means you fail or succeed at a major cost; a tie gives what you wanted at a minor cost or a lesser version; succeeding by 1–2 shifts is a clean success; by 3 or more is success with style and an added benefit. Invoking an aspect costs a fate point for +2 or a reroll; accepting a compel (a complication from one of your own aspects) earns a fate point; refusing a compel costs one. — [Four outcomes](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/four-outcomes); [Fate points](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/fate-points); [Invoking and compelling](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/invoking-compelling-aspects)
- **[doctrine]** Ironsworn: action die plus stat is compared with two challenge dice; beating both is a strong hit, one a weak hit, neither a miss. Strong hits commonly grant momentum; misses commonly end with "Pay the Price". Progress moves roll only the challenge dice against a filled progress track. It is built for solo, co-op without a GM, or guided play, with an Oracle of random tables that answers questions in GM-less play. — [Ironsworn SRD](https://tedtschopp.github.io/Ironsworn-SRD/Ironsworn%20SRD.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironsworn); [Ironsworn product page](https://www.ironswornrpg.com/products-ironsworn)
- **[doctrine / review]** Into the Odd is described by reviewers as a rules-light tribute to early D&D play focused on light rules and fast play; its designer's "ICI doctrine" names Information, Choice and Impact as the three parts of player agency (players cannot make a real choice without enough information; options must be real; what they do must matter). — [Giant Brain review](https://giantbrain.co.uk/2025/11/22/into-the-odd-gms-review/); [Tabletop Gaming review](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/?p=3245); [Bastionland, Sept 2018](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09)
- **[doctrine / community]** Free Kriegsspiel Revolution: "play worlds, not rules". Players may attempt any tactic, subject to the referee's adjudication, and the referee extrapolates outcomes from the imagined situation instead of charts. Commentators call it a high-trust style that succeeds only if players trust the referee to make fair, just and consistent rulings, and note the historical version worked when umpires had considerable experience and their rulings were plausible to players. Critics say its principles amount to complete GM authority with consistency negotiable. — [d66 Kobolds](https://d66kobolds.blogspot.com/2020/09/free-kriegsspiel-worlds-not-rules-etc.html?m=1); [Murkdice](https://murkdice.substack.com/p/do-you-know-what-fkr-is); [EN World: system matters and free kriegsspiel](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=8422122)

**Crunchy systems with an LLM referee (measured and anecdotal)**
- **[measured]** Even with a rules engine enforcing D&D 5e combat, every tested model made more errors as fights lengthened; and frontier models that win single 5e encounters show weak resource budgeting, rest timing and rule discipline across linked encounter days. — [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=3Op7kJOvaD); [arXiv 2607.29577](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.29577)
- **[measured]** In Call of Cthulhu, where rules are explicit, neither model scale nor explicit reasoning made any of 20 adjudicator models reliably robust to players arguing in narrative form. — [arXiv 2607.02802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02802)
- **[anecdote]** D&D 5e builders report invented spell damage and saving throws from memory, wrong class-resource recharge rules, re-narrating a miss as a hit, forgotten armour class and hit points, skipped class features and fudged rolls; each fix was a lookup tool, a machine-readable trace or an engine. — [Xebia](https://xebia.com/blog/building-a-dnd-dungeon-master-out-of-claude-part-i/); [DEV Community](https://dev.to/austin_amento_860aebb9f55/prompt-architecture-for-a-reliable-ai-dungeon-master-d99); [StoryRoll](https://storyroll.app/blog/tested-every-ai-dungeon-master)
- **[anecdote]** An EN World thread and a vendor post both report models losing uploaded reference material and earlier campaign content over long play, with retrieval over a dedicated dataset as the usual workaround. — [EN World: LLMs as a GM](https://www.enworld.org/threads/llms-as-a-gm.714126/); [DungeonsDeep](https://dungeonsdeep.ai/blog/why-ai-game-masters-forget-your-campaign-and-how-dungeonsdeepai-doesnt)
- **[measured]** The one tabletop system with a published function-calling GM study, Labyrinth: The Adventure Game, is itself a light system with table lookups and simple rolls. — [arXiv 2409.06949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06949)

**People running light systems with LLMs (anecdote)**
- **[anecdote / project]** Ironsworn has a dedicated Claude-as-GM tool with active development and a community ChatGPT prompt; a solo player reports pairing an oracle system with an LLM narrator. No source found reports a PbtA or Forged in the Dark campaign run by an LLM in enough detail to assess. — [Ironsworn VTT devlog](https://indiejoee.itch.io/ironsworn-using-claudeai/devlog/1704878/v143-live-scenes-that-just-work-ai-portrait-prompts-and-end-of-session-awards); [Ironsworn ChatGPT prompt](https://www.rpgprompts.com/post/ironsworn-chatgpt-prompt); [My Solo RPG Setup](https://kingcounty.substack.com/p/my-solo-rpg-setup)

### Inferences
- Why light, tiered systems fit: (1) three or four outcome bands are a low-branching lookup, the easy case in the FSM study; (2) every band carries narrative content, so there is no "nothing happens" for a positivity-biased narrator to hide in; (3) state is a handful of tracks, clocks and tags instead of per-entity hit points, slots and conditions, which is where the documented drift occurs; (4) the GM side is already written as a constrained list of moves and principles, which maps directly onto a prompt plus a small tool set.
- Why crunchy systems are costly: correctness depends on hundreds of exact values and exception rules that models misremember, so the only reliable route is a complete engine and data set (DungeonBench's rule surface is 561 combatants, 229 spells, 275 items), and long-run resource management stays weak even then.
- FKR-style pure adjudication is the worst fit for the referee half and a good fit for the player half. Its precondition is a consistent, trusted, experienced referee; LLM adjudicators are measurably inconsistent and persuadable. But "the player engages with the world, not the rules" is achievable if code holds the rules and the model only narrates outcomes: the player gets an FKR experience on top of a hidden deterministic engine.
- A practical synthesis for an AI-run campaign: PbtA/Fate-style tiered outcomes computed in code from a real roll; Forged-in-the-Dark-style stakes (position, effect, any bargain) fixed and recorded before the roll; Ironsworn-style progress tracks and oracle tables implemented as code; a short closed list of GM moves the narrator chooses from for each tier.
- Ironsworn's design for GM-less play (oracles answer questions) is the closest tabletop precedent to an LLM table: the oracle supplies the randomness and surprise the model cannot, and the model supplies the interpretation the oracle cannot.

### Gaps
- No controlled or even systematic comparison of rule families under an LLM referee exists in what was found; the ranking above is inference from adjacent measurements and anecdotes.
- Cairn was not found in results, and Into the Odd's specific mechanics (attacks that skip the to-hit roll and go straight to damage; saves rolled only to avoid danger) are from the researcher's background knowledge and were not confirmed in this session.
- Pathfinder 2e with an LLM: nothing found.
- The official Blades in the Dark SRD and the Apocalypse World text were not opened; wording above is from play aids, cheat sheets, a preview PDF and commentary. The phrases "to do it, do it" and "play to find out what happens" were not located in a retrievable primary source.
- The Alexandrian's "Why Powered by the Apocalypse?" article was found by title only.

## 5. Mechanics that drive story

### Takeaway
Designer doctrine is consistent across families: roll only when both outcomes are interesting and failure costs something; fix the stakes before the roll; make every result change the situation (cost, complication or advancing threat); let one roll stand; and never alter a result after the fact, because trust in the referee is hard to regain. For an LLM referee these are not only good taste: they offset measured weaknesses (positivity bias, persuadability, drift).

### Cited Findings

**When to roll**
- **[doctrine]** The Angry GM's rule: roll only when the action can succeed, can fail, and failure carries a risk or cost. If it cannot succeed, narrate the failure; if it cannot really fail, skip the roll. Often the real question is whether the party succeeds within a constraint (in five rounds, before the water rises), which is something they can fail at. — [The Angry GM](https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system)
- **[doctrine]** Matt Colville's version, as reported: call for a roll only when the GM can provide an interesting result for both success and failure. A Gnome Stew writer rolls only when failure would be at least as interesting as success. If a player can reroll a failure without consequence, the roll was not needed. — [Komodo Games](https://komodogames.substack.com/p/gm-thoughts-when-should-players-roll); [Tribality](https://www.tribality.com/2021/02/24/when-should-gms-ask-for-dice-rolls/); [Gnome Stew: worry about failure](https://gnomestew.com/worry-about-failure/); [Gnome Stew: rerolling failures](https://gnomestew.com/hot-button-rerolling-failures/)
- **[doctrine]** Burning Wheel: the player states intent (what they hope to achieve) and task (what they do); on success both happen; on failure the intent is not achieved and the GM narrates what happens instead. "Let It Ride": one check applies to the entire task, with no re-rolling the same question. The book quotes Vincent Baker's "Say yes or roll the dice." — [EN World: how does Burning Wheel play](https://www.enworld.org/threads/how-does-burning-wheel-play.226603/page-5); [EN World: roll for effect or intent](https://www.enworld.org/threads/roll-for-effect-or-intent.715094/latest)

**Partial success, fail-forward, success at a cost**
- **[doctrine]** PbtA: 7–9 is a success with a complication, offered as a worse outcome, a hard bargain or an ugly choice; a miss is "never nothing". — [lumpley.games preview PDF](https://lumpley.games/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Apocalypse-World-3rd-Ed-Preview-Moves-Ref.pdf); [Daily Apocalypse](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/6-moves-and-dice)
- **[doctrine]** Fate: a failed roll can become success at a major cost and a tie success at a minor cost. — [Fate SRD: four outcomes](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/four-outcomes)
- **[doctrine]** D&D 5e (2014) Dungeon Master's Guide option "Success at a Cost": when a character fails by only 1 or 2, the DM can allow success at the cost of a complication or hindrance. Forum users note it is optional. — [EN World discussion](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=8278455)
- **[doctrine / community]** Fail forward is described as letting a failed roll still move toward the goal with undesirable consequences; 13th Age is among the games that build it into resolution, and one player notes that failing forward does not always mean you got what you wanted. — [Gnome Stew](https://gnomestew.com/thou-shalt-not-fudge/); [EN World: failing forward](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=6786088)

**Stakes before the roll, bargains, meta-currencies**
- **[doctrine]** Blades in the Dark sets position (how bad failure is) and effect (how much success achieves) before the roll, and consequences can be traded as reduced effect or worse position. A devil's bargain is offered before the roll, is always the player's free choice and happens whatever the dice say. — [community play aid](https://community.bladesinthedark.com/t/notecard-play-aid-getting-a-handle-on-position-effect/1169); [Blades cheat sheet](https://www.thealexandrian.net/creations/bladesinthedark/bladesinthedark-cheat-sheet-v3.pdf)
- **[doctrine]** Meta-currencies found: Fate points (earned by accepting a complication on your own aspect, spent for +2 or a reroll); Blades stress (spent 2 at a time to push for a bonus die, and paid to resist a consequence); Ironsworn momentum (gained on strong hits). — [Fate SRD: fate points](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/fate-points); [Blades resistance](https://keeper.farirpgs.com/resources/one-seven-design/blades-in-the-dark/resistance-and-armor); [Ironsworn SRD](https://tedtschopp.github.io/Ironsworn-SRD/Ironsworn%20SRD.html)
- **[doctrine]** ICI doctrine (Bastionland): Information, Choice, Impact. Without enough information a choice is not real; "in RPGs, questions are gameplay". The author renamed the third term from Consequences to Impact because the former leaned negative. — [Bastionland](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09)

**Clocks, tracks, delayed consequences, standing**
- **[doctrine]** Progress clocks in Blades track ongoing effort against an obstacle or the approach of impending trouble; the playsheet frames them as tracking broken relationships, new enemies and problems on the horizon. A GM reports keeping clocks visible, sometimes with deliberately vague labels ("It All Goes Wrong"), to telegraph threats. A listed GM action is "Telegraph trouble before it strikes", paired with following through. — [Evil Hat playsheets](https://evilhat.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/blades_core_playsheets.pdf); [EN World: constellation of complications](https://www.enworld.org/threads/constellation-of-complications.682277/)
- **[doctrine]** Dungeon World fronts: each danger has grim portents (a single event or a chain of steps from bad to worse) and an impending doom that arrives when all portents come to pass, after which the setting has changed meaningfully. Community guidance says the portents must be made visible to the characters or they do not do their job, and dooms should be serious but not so dire that players have no other choice. The structure descends from Apocalypse World's countdown clocks. — [Dungeon World fronts](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts); [fronts wiki](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Fronts); [fronts lineage](https://save.vs.totalpartykill.ca/grab-bag/fronts/)
- **[doctrine]** Ironsworn progress tracks: long undertakings are a track that is filled by play and then tested with a single progress roll against the track, not the action die. — [Ironsworn SRD](https://tedtschopp.github.io/Ironsworn-SRD/Ironsworn%20SRD.html)
- **[doctrine]** Faction standing in Blades: status with each faction runs from −3 to +3, starting at 0; an operation costs −1 or −2 status with factions it hurts and can give +1 with one it helps; an operation kept completely quiet changes nothing. — [The faction game](https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/The%20Faction%20Game)

**Letting the dice fall**
- **[doctrine]** Justin Alexander ("GM Don't List #9: Fudging") treats fudging as a form of railroading because it overrides a player's choice to force a preset outcome, argues that lost trust is very hard to win back and undermines everything else the GM does, calls fudging "a betrayal of trust" elsewhere, and names failing forward as one of the easier ways to avoid any need to fudge. — [The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/43708/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-9-fudging); [The Alexandrian: fudging tag](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/tag/fudging)
- **[community, mixed]** In a long forum thread some players say they would leave a group on learning the GM fudged, because they could no longer trust its fairness; others say trust rests on more than dice and that some GMs fudge in order to be fair. — [EN World: in praise of dice](https://www.enworld.org/posts/8172783)

**LLM-specific evidence bearing on these mechanics**
- **[measured]** LLM narrators lean positive (more "man in a hole" arcs, positive endings even when told otherwise) and yield to a player's framing (10 of 33 forced confessions; pseudo-logic attacks on rule adjudication). — [arXiv 2407.13248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13248); [arXiv 2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.23043); [arXiv 2607.02802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02802)
- **[anecdote]** One Claude Code DM pre-computes the outcome tiers before each non-trivial roll so that the narration for each is fixed before the die lands. — [Testing in Production](https://www.testinginproduction.co/blog/playing-dnd-with-claude-as-dm)
- **[measured, student project]** Redirecting through in-world consequences and NPC influence preserved coherence without reducing felt autonomy. — [AAU PDF](https://projekter.aau.dk/projekter/files/784680954/Narrative_Adherence_in_LLM_driven_Games.pdf)

### Inferences
- A rule set for an AI-run campaign that follows the doctrine and offsets the measured weaknesses:
  1. **Roll gate in code or in a checklist the brief enforces:** a roll exists only if success and failure are both possible and both change the situation.
  2. **Stakes recorded before the roll:** what success achieves, what failure costs, any bargain accepted. Recording them makes them immune to later argument and to the narrator softening them.
  3. **Tiered result from code:** at least three bands with a mandated cost or complication on the middle band and a mandated advance of some threat on the low band.
  4. **One roll stands** (Let It Ride): no re-roll on the same question unless the situation changes.
  5. **No fudging is structural, not a promise:** the roll is produced and logged by code, so the narrator cannot alter it; publishing odds on request supports trust further.
  6. **Consequences as tracked objects with due dates:** clocks, fronts and faction status are exactly the delayed-consequence state a model forgets, so they belong in a ledger that code surfaces when due.
  7. **Telegraph, then follow through:** show the clock or portent in the fiction before it lands, then make it land. This satisfies ICI's information requirement without exposing numbers.
- Meta-currencies that pay the player for accepting trouble (Fate compels, devil's bargains) turn setbacks into chosen plot turns. This is the tabletop precedent for "missed days are plot turns, not lessons" style designs: the cost produces story and is not a punishment.
- Conditions, stress and tracks instead of hit points reduce numeric bookkeeping and give the narrator a word to write from; a tag such as a named condition is harder to drift on than a number.
- "Setting difficulty honestly" has an LLM-specific meaning: difficulty must be a function of recorded inputs evaluated by code, since the adjudication benchmark shows prose pressure moves model rulings.

### Gaps
- The official Blades in the Dark text for "don't roll twice for the same thing" and the stress, trauma and harm ladder was not retrieved (search budget exhausted); harm levels and trauma are therefore not described here.
- Masks: A New Generation's conditions (in place of hit points) were not researched for the same reason.
- No source was found on reputation or companion-approval systems as such (the video-game lineage) or on design guidance for them; the Blades faction-status rule is the only standing mechanic sourced.
- No empirical study links any of these mechanics to player-reported story quality; they are designer doctrine and community experience.
- No measurement exists of whether tiered outcomes actually reduce LLM positivity bias in play.

## 6. Gamification that ties real-world behaviour to a fictional character

### Takeaway
Gamified activity interventions show a small-to-medium effect that shrinks at follow-up (g = 0.42 falling to 0.15 in the main meta-analysis). What specific elements do is better established than whether "gamification works": performance feedback supports competence, story and avatars support relatedness, and punishment mechanics and broken-streak displays are the documented ways it backfires.

### Cited Findings
- **[measured]** Mazeas et al., Journal of Medical Internet Research, 2022: meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials (2,407 participants). Gamified interventions raised physical activity with a pooled Hedges g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14–0.69), and g = 0.58 against inactive controls. At follow-up averaging 14 weeks after the intervention ended the effect was g = 0.15 (95% CI 0.07–0.23). The authors read the persistence as evidence against a pure novelty effect but call the long-term benefit small; no subgroup differences were significant. — [JMIR 2022](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)
- **[measured]** A 2025 JMIR Serious Games meta-analysis in cardiovascular disease (6 trials): short-term g = 0.32 (95% CI 0.19–0.45; prediction interval 0.02–0.62); maintenance g = 0.20 (95% CI 0.12–0.29; prediction interval −0.01 to 0.41) from 3 studies with follow-up averaging 2.5 months. — [JMIR Serious Games 2025](https://games.jmir.org/2025/1/e64410/PDF)
- **[measured]** "Counterproductive effects of gamification: An analysis on the example of the gamified task manager Habitica" (International Journal of Human-Computer Studies). Study 1, an interpretative phenomenological interview study with a single user, yielded seven themes of distinct counterproductive effects and seven more about the reward/punishment system and psychological reactions. Study 2 explored them in a two-week quantitative field study with 45 users. Later work citing it says the point system was found to be actively harmful and that Habitica's mechanics reward procrastination under certain circumstances. Quantitative results not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581918305135); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327451529_Counterproductive_effects_of_gamification_An_analysis_on_the_example_of_the_gamified_task_manager_Habitica)
- **[measured]** Habit-formation research outside games: pleasure and intrinsic motivation aid habit formation by producing a greater increase in habit strength per repetition. — [PMC6302524](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6302524/)
- **[measured]** Sailer et al. (2017): badges, leaderboards and performance graphs positively affect competence need satisfaction and perceived task meaningfulness; avatars, meaningful stories and teammates affect social relatedness; perceived decision freedom (autonomy) could not be affected as intended. Conclusion: gamification is not effective per se; specific elements have specific psychological effects. — [reference summary](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Sailer-et-al.-(2017)); [open-access copy](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109059/109059.pdf)
- **[measured]** Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers & Education): across a 16-week semester, students in a gamified course showed less motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time than those in the non-gamified course. A secondary summary links this to rewarding tasks that were already interesting (the overjustification reading). — [dblp record](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/ce/HanusF15); [Psychology of Games](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2016/10/the-overjustification-effect-and-game-achievements/)
- **[measured]** A 2023 meta-analysis of 35 gamified-learning interventions found a significant but small overall effect and describes the evidence on intrinsic motivation as inconsistent. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-023-10337-7)
- **[measured]** Silverman and Barasch, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", Journal of Consumer Research 49(6), April 2023. Highlighting an intact streak in a behaviour log increases subsequent engagement relative to highlighting a broken one. In an exercise-app study 66.23% continued the same activity with an intact streak against 57.86% with a broken one. The drop is larger when people attribute the break to themselves, smaller when the streak can be "repaired", and it occurs even when the break was caused by something outside the person's control. Almost half of participants would do something undesirable, such as watch an advertisement, to keep or repair a streak. — [UDSpace](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [University of Delaware](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- **[doctrine from that research]** The authors' design advice: do not draw attention to broken streaks ("Hey, you broke your streak!"), offer alternate ways to keep a streak going, and define streaks broadly so several activities count. — [University of Delaware](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)
- **[measured, qualitative]** Zombies, Run!: interviews with 30 users (Farič et al., Games for Health Journal, 2021) found gamification and narrative appeal drew people in, and the narrative motivated longer sessions and long-term use, possibly by changing how exertion is perceived. A lab study with 20 college-aged runners found they felt more inspired to run with the app than without audio. Two pilot studies in adolescents (fewer than 51 participants) are cited as finding no significant effects. No controlled long-duration trial result was found. — [PubMed 34813376](https://www.pubmed.gov/34813376/); [UCL repository](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10139156/3/Potts_ZR_users_interviews_paper_Games%20for%20Health_Last%20version_NF.pdf)
- **[opinion, not research]** Product blogs claim punishment mechanics suit users who have already shown commitment more than users in early habit formation, and that they backfire for many people. No data accompanies these claims. — [Trophy](https://trophy.so/blog/productivity-gamification-examples)

### Inferences
- Design guidance consistent with the evidence, for XP, levels and attributes tied to real behaviour:
  1. **No loss mechanics.** The documented counterproductive effects centre on reward/punishment systems; a missed day should cost nothing in the game layer.
  2. **Never display a broken streak.** Show cumulative totals or recent consistency; if any streak exists, make it repairable and define it broadly. An externally caused break demotivates as much as a self-caused one unless it is reframed, which supports treating plan-directed rest as excused and as not a break at all.
  3. **Reward running the plan as written, not exceeding it.** This follows from the overjustification concern (extrinsic reward for already-valued activity) and from the Habitica finding that a point system can reward the wrong behaviour; a cap removes the incentive to overdo it. This is inference: no study found tests capped rewards directly.
  4. **Lean on narrative and avatar, not leaderboards.** Story and avatars feed relatedness and, in the Zombies, Run! interviews, session length and long-term use; performance feedback (graphs, attribute growth) feeds competence.
  5. **Protect autonomy by choice inside the story, not by the mechanics.** Gamification elements failed to raise perceived decision freedom, so the player's sense of choice has to come from real decisions offered in the fiction.
  6. **Expect the effect to fade and plan content, not bigger rewards.** A small long-term effect suggests novelty carries part of the benefit; an evolving story is a renewable source of novelty.
- Keeping real targets out of the fiction's control (the story never sets training loads) is consistent with the finding that point systems can drive behaviour in unintended directions.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research was found on "streak anxiety" as such, on LitRPG-style progression, or on any app named Fitness RPG; streak effects are covered only through the consumer-research paper.
- Avatar identification and the Proteus effect were not researched (search budget exhausted).
- The Habitica paper's authors and year were not shown in results (the researcher recalls Diefenbach and Müssig, 2019, but this is unconfirmed), and its Study 2 figures were not retrieved.
- An arXiv paper, "An Exploratory Study of Health Habit Formation Through Gamification" (1708.04418), was found by title only.
- No study was found on gamification in injury rehabilitation specifically, or on designs that deliberately hide their mechanics from the user.
- The Hanus and Fox overjustification interpretation is from a secondary summary, not the paper's own wording.

## 7. Player agency rules for an AI GM

### Takeaway
Published AI-GM prompts converge on one hard rule (never write the player character's actions, speech or thoughts; present the situation and stop) and commonly end on an open question. Research adds that LLM GMs fail agency in the opposite directions too: ignoring input, misreading intent, and agreeing with whatever the player asserts. No study was found that compares open prompts with enumerated options, or that tests how much to resolve per turn.

### Cited Findings

**How published prompts encode agency (community practice)**
- **[anecdote / practice]** A guide to RPG prompts calls agency rules the most commonly missing piece and says that without them the AI ends up playing your character within a dozen messages; the GM should describe the world and wait for the player to act. The search summary did not tie this quote to a single page; the guides returned were the two linked here. — [Arcanum RPG prompts](https://arcanumrpgs.com/blog/rpg-prompts/); [Feathered Fiction solo AI GM guide](https://featheredfiction.com/2026/01/05/solo-rpg-ai-gm-guide/)
- **[practice]** A solo-RPG GM prompt instructs: do not narrate the player character's thoughts, words or actions; the player's character is off-limits; when an NPC makes a proposal, present it and stop so the player can respond. — [Feathered Fiction](https://featheredfiction.com/2026/01/05/solo-rpg-ai-gm-guide/)
- **[practice]** A game-master agent prompt published on Hugging Face bans numbered or bulleted action lists and tells the GM to close with an open-ended question such as "What do you do?" — [LLM-GameMaster-Agent prompt](https://huggingface.co/spaces/Agents-MCP-Hackathon/LLM-GameMaster-Agent/blob/main/prompt.txt)
- **[practice]** A roleplay guide reduces it to one rule: never write the player's character's actions, dialogue or thoughts. A Janitor AI prompt tells the model to stop immediately when it is the user's turn to act or respond. A further guide treats the problem under the roleplay term "godmodding". — [Dunia guide](https://dunia.gg/blog/guide-to-roleplaying-with-ai); [Janitor AI prompt](https://janitorai.com/characters/60722baa-7745-4bd8-ae4f-5c755fbae40f_character-the-prompt-i-use); [Jenova on godmodding](https://www.jenova.ai/en/resources/ai-roleplay-godmodding)

**What research says about agency with LLM narrators**
- **[measured]** "Ignoring Player Input" is a named narrator failure category in NCP-Bench. — [arXiv 2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)
- **[measured, qualitative]** CriticaLLM player feedback: the GM often failed to understand the intentions behind player actions, which stopped the story adapting over the long term. — [arXiv 2610.21525](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.21525)
- **[measured]** Over-agreement is also an agency failure: 10 of 33 players forced a full confession from an LLM-only suspect, and narrative-framed arguments degrade rule adjudication across 20 models. — [arXiv 2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.23043); [arXiv 2607.02802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02802)
- **[measured, student project]** Players preferred redirection that works through the world (NPC influence, in-world consequences) and preserves immersion over outright rejection of their action. — [AAU PDF](https://projekter.aau.dk/projekter/files/784680954/Narrative_Adherence_in_LLM_driven_Games.pdf)
- **[measured]** In the function-calling Labyrinth GM, invalid player attempts were rejected robustly. — [workshop PDF](https://wordplay-workshop.github.io/wordplay2024/pdfs/11.pdf)
- **[measured, qualitative]** Elsewise (arXiv Jan 2026): authors who built LLM interactive stories rated the resulting player agency as only moderate; one noted that open-ended chatting can run indefinitely without the plot advancing, and the paper argues that permitting an action does not confer meaningful agency unless the underlying model of the world supports it. — [arXiv 2601.15295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15295)
- **[measured, qualitative]** IF:CARGO (24 participants): opinions on free-form input split between loss of freedom or achievement if it were removed and reduced cognitive burden, a trade-off between expressivity and control. — [arXiv 2608.12195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12195)
- **[measured, qualitative]** Eternagram (ChatGPT-driven text adventure): free-text play made plot progression hard to control, and the authors added trigger-based clues to restore some control. — [arXiv 2403.18160](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18160)
- **[measured]** Free-form conversation with LLM characters let players create story nodes the designers had not written, most often players who enjoy exploration and experimentation. — [arXiv 2404.17027](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17027)
- **[measured]** A multi-agent GM that added reviewer feedback produced more spoilers, a failure of withholding information the player should discover. — [Underline listing](https://underline.io/lecture/137732-trpg-game-mastering-using-llm-based-multi-agent-system)
- **[anecdote]** A Claude DM mixed what the characters can perceive with DM-only information in a single output stream. — [Xebia](https://xebia.com/blog/building-a-dnd-dungeon-master-out-of-claude-part-i/)

**Tabletop doctrine on choice**
- **[doctrine]** Player agency needs information, real options and impact (ICI). — [Bastionland](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09)
- **[doctrine]** PbtA's 7–9 hands the player a decision (accept a worse outcome, a hard bargain or an ugly choice), and a Blades devil's bargain is always a free choice the player may decline. — [lumpley.games preview PDF](https://lumpley.games/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Apocalypse-World-3rd-Ed-Preview-Moves-Ref.pdf); [Blades cheat sheet](https://www.thealexandrian.net/creations/bladesinthedark/bladesinthedark-cheat-sheet-v3.pdf)
- **[doctrine]** One roll covers the whole task (Let It Ride), which bounds how much a single resolution settles. — [EN World: how does Burning Wheel play](https://www.enworld.org/threads/how-does-burning-wheel-play.226603/page-5)

### Inferences
- Agency for an AI GM has three separate rules, each addressing a different measured failure:
  1. **Do not author the player character** (prompts): narrate the world's response to what the player said, stop at the next decision.
  2. **Do take the player's input seriously** (NCP-Bench, CriticaLLM): restate or act on the intent, and when an action cannot work, answer with an in-world consequence instead of ignoring or refusing it.
  3. **Do not be talked out of the rules** (CoC-Seduce, detective game): the player controls the character, not the odds or the facts of the world; those are fixed in code.
- Open prompt versus enumerated options: the evidence is suggestive only. Open input produces emergence and felt freedom but can stall the plot and raises cognitive load; options keep the plot moving but narrow expression. A reasonable hybrid consistent with all sources is an open prompt by default, with enumerated options only at authored decision points where the stakes of each option have already been set, and with free-text answers still accepted.
- A turn should end at the first point where the player has a real decision with information and stakes. This follows from the "present the proposal and stop" prompts and from ICI; it is not a tested result.
- Structural separation of GM-only material from player-facing text protects discovery (spoilers, premature disclosure) better than an instruction, by analogy with the knowledge-tree and information-separation findings.
- Because "permitting an action is not agency unless the world model supports it", agency depends on the consequence ledger: a choice matters only if code remembers it and brings it back.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head study of open-ended prompts versus suggested or enumerated choices in LLM-run role-play was found.
- No source, academic or practitioner, was found that tests how much to resolve per GM turn or ideal response length.
- AI Dungeon, SillyTavern and NovelAI documentation on agency instructions was not retrieved; the prompts cited are community examples, and their effectiveness is asserted by their authors, not measured.
- The effect of an agency rule in the prompt alone versus a mechanical check (for example validating that a block contains no player-character actions) has not been studied.

## 8. Making rules discoverable in play instead of explained up front

### Takeaway
Design writing supports teaching mechanics through play and treating a system's rules as something worth discovering, with two cautions: players still need enough information to make real choices, and discovery of a specific hidden feature should never be required to progress. Tabletop practice squares these by showing consequences and threats in the fiction (visible clocks, portents) while keeping the arithmetic behind the screen. Evidence here is design criticism and doctrine; no empirical study was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[doctrine]** The developer of Starseed Pilgrim left out instructions deliberately; Electron Dance reports his term "discoverable systems" for rulesets that are interesting to find on your own and that instruction would spoil. The same article notes most players do not come to such games as complete newcomers. — [Electron Dance: Developers won't hold your hand](https://www.electrondance.com/developers-wont-hold-hand/)
- **[doctrine]** Puzzle designer Alan Hazelden separates controls from mechanics: controls need tutorials, mechanics are usually better taught through in-game examples and constrained level design. — [Electron Dance: Figure it out](https://www.electrondance.com/figure-it-out/)
- **[doctrine]** A developer essay on the strategy game Darknet describes such games as "a big pile of secrets", with uncovering them a delight. — [Game Developer: Hiding Secrets](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/hiding-secrets)
- **[blog opinion, weak source]** Game-development blog posts argue for lessons woven into play (citing Breath of the Wild's opening plateau as a controlled space for learning core mechanics) and warn against forcing discovery of specific features in order to progress; multiple paths should exist. These are generic posts with little evidence. — [Wayline: invisible tutorials](https://www.wayline.io/blog/invisible-tutorials-game-onboarding); [Wayline: hidden game features](https://www.wayline.io/blog/hidden-game-features-player-engagement)
- **[doctrine]** FKR's aim is for players to "play the world, not the rules": the player says what the character does and the referee handles resolution out of sight. — [d66 Kobolds](https://d66kobolds.blogspot.com/2020/09/free-kriegsspiel-worlds-not-rules-etc.html?m=1)
- **[doctrine]** The counterweight: without enough information a choice is not a real choice, and asking questions is itself gameplay. — [Bastionland](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09)
- **[doctrine / practice]** Threats are made visible in the fiction before they land: Blades GMs keep clocks on the table, sometimes with vague labels, and Dungeon World's grim portents only work if characters become aware of them. — [EN World: constellation of complications](https://www.enworld.org/threads/constellation-of-complications.682277/); [Dungeon World fronts](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts)
- **[measured]** In an LLM-driven game, the players who generated the most emergent content were those who enjoy discovery, exploration and experimentation, which indicates the appeal of discovery is player-dependent. — [arXiv 2404.17027](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17027)
- **[measured]** Extrinsic reward structures made explicit can reduce motivation over time (Hanus and Fox), which is at least compatible with not foregrounding the reward arithmetic. — [dblp record](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/ce/HanusF15)

### Inferences
- A design rule that reconciles "discoverable" with "informed choice": hide the exchange rates, show the consequences. The player should always be able to see what is at stake in a decision and what has changed because of it, in the story's own terms, without being told which input moved which number.
- Unlocks should be visible as a silhouette (something exists, in fiction) with the trigger undisclosed until asked or achieved; progress should never depend on guessing a hidden trigger. This follows from the "no forced discovery" caution.
- An explicit "show me the sheet" request should always be honoured: discovery is a default, not a lock, and since the appeal of discovery varies by player, an opt-in to full transparency removes the downside.
- Discovery by play is a particularly good fit for an AI-run campaign with a code engine: because the engine is deterministic and logged, patterns the player notices are real and repeatable, which is the precondition for a "discoverable system" to be satisfying. A model improvising mechanics would produce nothing stable to discover.
- For real-world behaviour change there is a second reason to keep exchange rates out of view: explicit point-chasing is where gamification has been shown to misdirect behaviour (section 6). This is an inference; no study tests hidden versus shown mechanics.

### Gaps
- No academic literature on diegetic user interfaces was retrieved (the planned search could not run). The researcher recalls a 2009 Chalmers thesis, "Beyond the HUD" by Fagerholt and Lorentzon, as the usual citation for the diegetic / non-diegetic interface taxonomy; this is unverified in this session.
- No empirical study was found comparing hidden with disclosed mechanics for engagement, trust or behaviour change, in games or in habit apps.
- No tabletop design writing was found specifically on hiding a progression system from the player while keeping play fair; the sources cover hidden resolution (FKR) and visible threats (clocks, portents), not hidden advancement.
- Writing on knowledge-gated progression in video games (games where what the player learns is the only unlock) was not retrieved.

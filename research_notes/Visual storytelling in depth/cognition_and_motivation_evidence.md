## 1. Pictures, comprehension and memory: seductive details, contiguity, signalling, picture functions

### Takeaway
Most of the earlier pass's multimedia numbers hold at abstract level. Two corrections change how they apply. First, the 2020 seductive-details meta-analysis found **no significant overall harm from a picture-only seductive detail**: the harm came from seductive *audio* and from *text-plus-picture* additions. Second, Harp & Mayer's end-placement result *reduced* the harm; it did not abolish it. Pictures that represent or interpret the text still help, decorative ones still don't, and "emotional design" (appealing colour and faces in *relevant* graphics) helps learning a little without much changing mood.

### Cited Findings
**Verification of the earlier pass's numbers**
- **Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), *Educ. Psych. Review* 32(3):707–734, CONFIRMED and CLARIFIED.** The study-count conflict (58 vs 68) is resolved: **58 papers, 68 effect sizes, 7,521 participants**. Retention: g = −0.37 (19 effect sizes, 2,147 participants). Transfer: g = −0.41. A citing paper gives the overall benefit of *removing* seductive details as g = 0.33, i.e. overall ≈ −0.33. [META k=68] [abstract only, search-engine summary of reviews of the paper] — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339511797_Keep_it_Coherent_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Seductive_Details_Effect); [ScienceDirect topic page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/seductive-detail)
  - **Key moderator the earlier pass missed:** the meta-analysis "confirmed an overall negative effect of seductive details on learning outcomes but also pointed out that such was only the case when the seductive details were in the audio or the text and picture format overall. **There was no overall significant effect from the mere presence of a pictorial seductive detail.**" [META] [via citing full text: Frontiers in Psychology 2024] — [Frontiers 2024, fpsyg.2024.1393113](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1393113/full)
  - Other moderators reported in press coverage (larger in short lessons under 5 min, on paper, for novices, for static details beside relevant diagrams) are still press-release level. [abstract only] — [WSU press release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning); [Learning and the Brain (secondary)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/)
- **Newer meta-analyses: smaller effect.** One reports 50 studies, 177 effect sizes, overall g = −0.16 (comprehension −0.19, recall −0.17, transfer −0.12). An ECER 2025 abstract from another team also reports g = −0.16 and calls it smaller than earlier meta-analyses. [META k=177] [abstract only] — [AERA 2026 paper summary](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/); [ECER 2025 contribution](https://eera-ecer.de/ecer-programmes/conference/31/contribution/65030)
- **Rey (2012), *Educ. Research Review*, PARTLY VERIFIED.** Later papers describe it as a small-to-medium negative effect in laboratory settings, **stronger when study time is limited (system-paced) than when it is unlimited**. Its exact retention and transfer d values could not be confirmed. One citing source gives d = 0.27 (retention) and 0.65 (transfer) for seductive *text*; treat as unverified. Rey also reviewed design strategies under which seductive details keep their benefit for motivation or persistence. [META] [abstract only + via citing full text] — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1393113/full); [Tislar et al. 2021, PMC record (blocked)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8442593/)
- **Harp & Mayer (1998), *J. Educ. Psych.* 90(3):414–434, CORRECTED.** **Four** experiments (the Wikipedia summary's "six" is wrong). Adding highlighting of main ideas (Exp. 1), learning objectives (Exp. 2) or signalling (Exp. 3) did **not** remove the harm. Placing the seductive details at the **start** made the harm worse. Placing them at the **end reduced** it (the earlier pass said performance then "matched" the no-detail group; the abstract says "reduced"). Mechanism: diversion, i.e. the details activate an inappropriate frame of prior knowledge. [CTRL ×4, students] [abstract only] — [scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/162a7b78ef56a04497da909fb84d0592); [Wikipedia (secondary, has the count wrong)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seductive_details)
- **Coherence principle, CONFLICT.** The earlier pass cited a median d = 0.97. Another summary of Mayer's coherence chapter gives a median d = 1.32 across 11 comparisons. The two numbers probably come from different editions; neither was checked against the book. [REVIEW] [abstract only] — [Cambridge chapter page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/coherence-principle/4E80B70CB76E2166B76E5653EBDE7D3E); [Growth Engineering (secondary)](https://www.growthengineering.co.uk/seductive-details/)
- **Signalling, CORRECTED.** Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), *Educ. Research Review* 23:1–24: **103 studies** (not "~145"), N = 12,201. Retention g = 0.53; transfer g = 0.33 (the transfer value rests on one source). [META] [abstract only] — [LearnTechLib record](https://www.learntechlib.org/p/204443/); [TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/CHU_KR-22_-Schneider_2017_Lernmaterial_Hervorhebungen.pdf)
  - **Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016)**, *Educ. Research Review* 17:19–36 (signalling text–picture relations): about 27 studies per one summary. Effects on comprehension and transfer, **moderated by prior knowledge: larger for low-prior-knowledge learners**, possibly harmful for high-prior-knowledge learners. Its pooled r was not confirmed. [META] [via citing full text: Albus & Seufert 2022] — [Frontiers in Education 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.916105/full); [Utrecht signalling chapter (van Gog)](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/424007/the_signaling_or_cueing_principle_in_multimedia_learning.pdf?sequence=1)
- **Spatial contiguity, UNVERIFIED.** Schroeder & Cenkci (2018), *Educ. Psych. Review*, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9. The g = 0.63 / 58 comparisons / n = 2,426 figures could not be checked (the Springer page was blocked and searches gave only the citation). Keep them as secondary (TUM Clearinghouse). [META] [abstract only] — [TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)
- **Picture functions, PARTLY VERIFIED.** Carney & Levin (2002), *Educ. Psych. Review* 14(1):5–26: illustrations with specific functions (representational, organisational, interpretational, transformational) improve text-dependent learning; decorative ones do not. The earlier Levin, Anglin & Carney (1987) meta-analysis covered **150 units** and found **no beneficial effect of decorative pictures**. Per-function effect sizes were not recovered. Evidence mostly from 1970s–90s school-age samples. [META + REVIEW] [abstract only + via citing full text] — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1393113/full); [DOI record](https://www.doi.org/10.1023/A:1013176309260)

**New studies**
- **Emotional design (appealing colours, faces, anthropomorphism in *relevant* graphics):** two meta-analyses (Brom et al. 2018; Wong & Adesope 2020/2021) report **weak effects on learners' affect but substantial effects on retention, comprehension and transfer**. Individual studies are inconsistent, and pleasant colour that does not match the learning goal is null or harmful. [META ×2] [via citing full text] — [Lenski & Großschedl, Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.966287/full); [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1393113/full)
- **Colour in relevant vs decorative pictures:** a 2 × 2 study (colour of learning-relevant background × colour of an irrelevant decorative picture), run with 128 university students in a lab and 140 secondary-school students in class. The interplay between relevance and colour affected cognitive load, metacognitive judgements and affect [full paper read: abstract and introduction]. A search-engine summary adds that making the decorative picture salient with colour did not produce the usual seductive-detail harm [abstract only; not checked against the results section]. [CTRL n=268, partly adolescent] — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1393113/full)
- **Signals in a busy visual scene:** in 360° desktop VR, signals guiding attention to relevant content improved learning and changed cognitive load (N = 96). [CTRL n=96] [full paper read: abstract and introduction] — [Albus & Seufert, Frontiers in Education 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.916105/full)
- **Redundancy needs two definitions:** a 2023 review argues "redundancy" mixes two different cases: identical information in two modes, and logically redundant information. The findings differ by case. [REVIEW] [full paper read: abstract] — [Trypke, Stebner & Wirth, Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1148035/full)

### Inferences
- The corrected Sundararajan & Adesope moderator weakens the case against **purely decorative art on a story page**. A picture-only seductive detail did not reliably hurt learning. The harm came from interesting extra *text* or *audio*, or text-plus-picture asides. For the site, the bigger risk is **extra prose** (in-world trivia, captions that add lore) placed in the reading flow. A quiet image near a passage is a smaller risk.
- Harp & Mayer's placement result is the most usable rule: extra material **after** the scene does least harm; extra material **before** it does most. Art, songs or lore that open a chapter, before its text, frame it most strongly. Put them at the end or behind a tap.
- Emotional-design evidence supports making relevant images (the place being read about, the person speaking) attractive. Attractiveness pays off only when the image carries something the text has said.
- Signalling helps low-prior-knowledge readers most and may hurt experts. The author knows his own world well, so heavy cueing (highlighting, arrows, "remember this") is more likely to be noise than help for him.

### Gaps
- Sundararajan & Adesope's full moderator table (static vs dynamic, paper vs digital, placement) is still press-release level; the Springer full text was blocked.
- Schroeder & Cenkci (2018) and Richter et al. (2016) pooled values are unverified; Schneider et al.'s transfer g rests on one source.
- Carney & Levin's per-function effect sizes were not recovered.
- No study found of seductive details in *fiction* read for pleasure; all of this is learning outcomes in expository lessons.

## 2. Adult illustrated narrative, imagery, aphantasia and order of exposure (imagination and immersion)

### Takeaway
The n = 212 study is confirmed as **Cohen, Wasserman, Schlue, Keely & Russell**. It is a narrative-*persuasion* study (a health topic, graphic novel vs text-only), not a fiction-enjoyment study. Its chain (images → easier imagining → engagement → less counter-arguing) stands at abstract level. Newer adult studies are mixed. Comics beat text for recall in one n = 90 study. An AI-illustrated graphic novel did no better than text in two small 2025 studies, and in one the text-only group felt *more* emotion. Aphantasia lowers absorption and sympathy but not appreciation. The earlier pass's claim that "imagery propensity predicted transportation for readers but not viewers" in Green et al. (2008) could **not** be confirmed; the accessible summaries report a need-for-cognition moderator instead.

### Cited Findings
**Verification**
- **Cohen et al., CONFIRMED.** Cohen, Wasserman, Schlue, Keely & Russell, "Seeing is believing: the role of imagery fluency in narrative persuasion through a graphic novel," *Psychology of Popular Media* 9(2):176–183 (online 2018, issue April 2020). **212 MTurk adults** were randomly assigned to read a story about people with **schizophrenia** as a graphic novel with images or as text only. Images raised imagery fluency, which raised narrative engagement and lowered counter-arguing against the story's sympathetic portrayal. The imagery-fluency path to story-consistent beliefs was the largest indirect effect. [CTRL n=212] [abstract only] — [RTI publication page](https://www.rti.org/publication/seeing-believing); [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/5ce2d084ced107d4c6389741/seeing-is-believing-the-role-of-imagery-fluency-in-narrative-persuasion-through)
- **Green, Kass, Carrey, Herzig, Feeney & Sabini (2008), *Media Psychology* 11(4):512–539, PARTLY CORRECTED.** Design: a **field study plus a laboratory experiment** comparing a book with its film adaptation. Confirmed: people who read the novel *before* the film were more transported into the film. **Not confirmed:** the earlier pass's "imagery propensity predicted transportation for readers but not viewers". The accessible summary instead reports that **high need-for-cognition people were more transported when reading, low need-for-cognition people when watching**. Treat the imagery claim as unverified until the full text is read. [CTRL + FIELD] [abstract only] — [Buffalo record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/transportation-across-media-repeated-exposure-to-print-and-film/); [OnFiction research bulletin (secondary)](https://www.onfiction.ca/2009/03/research-bulletin-repeating-narrative.html?m=1)
- **Speed, Eekhof & Mak (2024), *Consciousness and Cognition* 118:103645, CONFIRMED with wording fixed.** 47 aphantasic readers vs 51 matched controls, one short story. Aphantasic readers were less engaged with, interested in and absorbed in the story, and felt less emotional engagement with and **sympathy for the characters**. Story **appreciation did not differ**, nor did general reading habits. Descriptions of scenery and actions were appreciated less. Test statistics were not recovered. [CTRL quasi-experimental n=98] [abstract only] — [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/80824de1-1604-439a-8276-3a651c3f2058); [Radboud news](https://www.ru.nl/en/research/research-news/lack-of-visual-imagery-does-not-lead-to-less-pleasure-in-reading)

**New studies**
- **Comics vs text, adults:** Aleixo & Sumner (2016/2017), *J. Graphic Novels and Comics* 8(1):79–88. 90 participants read seven comic pages on sleep biology, the same content as text only, or the comic with its images replaced by **incongruous** pictures. Memory (10 multiple-choice items) was highest for the comic. **Text-only beat the incongruous-image version.** [CTRL n=90, adults] [abstract only] — [Sheffield Hallam repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/12833)
  - Counter-evidence: a UNLV dissertation on science comics found null effects across its experiments, and a 2018 review concluded comics can equal text when readers must memorise. [CTRL + REVIEW] [abstract only] — [UNLV dissertation record](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/thesesdissertations/4755); [JCOM 2018](https://jcom.sissa.it/archive/17/01/JCOM_1701_2018_Y01)
- **AI-illustrated fiction, adults (2025):** a short literary story was turned into a graphic novel with GPT-4 and Midjourney. With **76 ESL readers**, there were **no significant differences** in comprehension or narrative engagement vs plain text. 70% recognised AI involvement; attitudes to AI as illustrator were generally positive. A thesis version (**80** proficient ESL readers, pre/post design) also found no differences in comprehension, enjoyment or overall engagement, but the **text-only control reported significantly higher emotional engagement**. Readers spotted AI through image texture and **inconsistencies**. [CTRL n=76 / n=80] [abstract only] — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:2ef58fcd-4119-4584-b42f-bb7f4ce56c69); [Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/48521)
  - GenAIReading (AHs 2025): adding AI-generated summaries and **images** to reading material raised post-reading test scores (+7.50% for image summaries) with **24** participants. This is learning, not pleasure reading; tiny sample. [CTRL n=24] [full paper read: abstract] — [arXiv 2503.07463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07463)
  - NC State (Nov 2025): in parent–child reading of AI-illustrated stories, children were more likely than parents to notice when the **emotion of the image did not match the text**; AI labels were mostly unnoticed or found distracting. (CHILD, partly) [abstract only, press] — [NC State news](https://news.ncsu.edu/2025/11/parents-kids-ai-images/)
- **Aphantasia, more:** a 2025 *Psychophysiology* study (Abdelrahman, Melcher & Ripollés) found a group difference in **emotional** but not cognitive engagement with stories. [CTRL] [abstract only] — [aphantasia.com research listing](https://aphantasia.com/research/10.1111/psyp.70100). A bioRxiv preprint notes the literature is split on whether imagery is *necessary* for emotional engagement. [CTRL, preprint] [abstract only] — [bioRxiv 2025.01.09.632075](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.01.09.632075.full.pdf). Aphantasic college students (Study 1 n = 450 survey; Study 2, 14 interviews) compensate by externalising, e.g. anchoring new characters to familiar references (academic texts, not fiction). [survey + qualitative] [full paper read: abstract] — [Cavazos et al., Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1615860/full)

### Inferences
- Pictures that **match** the text help memory; pictures that **mismatch** it do worse than no picture (Aleixo & Sumner's incongruous condition). For per-Book portraits and AI-generated art, **consistency with the prose and between images** is the active ingredient. The 2025 AI studies show readers notice inconsistencies, and an AI-illustrated version earned no engagement gain.
- The text-only group's higher emotional engagement in the 2025 thesis, Green et al.'s book-first effect and the need-for-cognition split together support the earlier "**text first, picture after**" rule. A reader who likes prose is transported by prose; the picture should follow it.
- If the reader is a weak imager, portraits and place art plausibly restore some of the absorption and character sympathy aphantasic readers lose (Speed et al.). This is inference; no study gave pictures to aphantasic readers.

### Gaps
- Green et al. (2008) imagery claim: needs the full text (T&F blocked).
- No adult study found that gives illustrations to *long-form prose fiction* (not graphic novels) and measures transportation or recall over weeks.
- No study found that tests pictures specifically for low-imagery or aphantasic readers.

## 3. Maps, spatial situation models and conjoint retention

### Takeaway
A new key finding: **readers do not spontaneously build detailed spatial models during ordinary story reading** (Zwaan & van Oostendorp 1993). They do so when told to, at a cost in reading speed. Classic spatial-gradient effects (Morrow, Bower) appear when readers have *memorised a map first* and follow the protagonist. So a map can supply spatial structure readers otherwise skip; it is not something they would build anyway. Map-text recall benefits (conjoint retention) are real in expository texts but contested as to *why* (layout vs recognisable icons). No controlled study of maps in fiction reading was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Zwaan & van Oostendorp (1993), *Discourse Processes* 16:125–143 (new):** under normal reading, readers did not build precise spatial representations of a naturalistic story. **Instructions to build a spatial representation slowed reading but improved spatial representation.** The authors conclude spatial representations are not essential to naturalistic story comprehension. Later reviews cite it as showing that readers track space only under particular conditions. [CTRL] [abstract only] — [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/do-readers-construct-spatial-representations-in-naturalistic-story-comprehension/811822596671668225-5684); [scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/d47f19babac006a62feca6e2aec6605f)
- **Morrow & Bower spatial gradient: no new verification.** The earlier pass's description (map memorised first, accessibility highest in the protagonist's room and falling with distance, dependent on tracking the protagonist) stands at abstract level. [CTRL, multiple] [abstract only] — [Memory & Cognition 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0329-2)
- **Conjoint retention, PARTLY VERIFIED.** Kulhavy, Lee & Caterino (1985) proposed that maps studied with text raise text recall because maps are encoded spatially, a corollary of dual coding. **Griffin & Robinson (2000, 2005)**: no recall advantage for maps over feature lists, and maps were not processed more spatially. Schreiber, Verdi et al. (2002): flat maps beat perspective maps, and map plus well-organised text beat map plus randomised text. A tactile-map test replicated the recall effect for sighted participants who studied the map for 10 minutes, but doubted that map *structure* was the cause. [CTRL, multiple] [abstract only] — [Georgia Southern record](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/does-spatial-or-visual-information-in-maps-facilitate-text-recall/); [Arizona Regents record](https://experts.azregents.edu/en/publications/differing-map-construction-and-text-organization-and-their-effect/); [Unpaywall chapter record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F978-1-4471-0109-3_8)
- **Verdi & Kulhavy (2002), *Educ. Psych. Review* 14(1) ("Learning with maps and texts: an overview"):** a review covering prior knowledge, individual differences and **order of presentation** in map-and-text learning. Its specific findings were not recovered. [REVIEW] [abstract only, attribution uncertain] — [Bohrium author page](https://www.bohrium.com/scholar/5s5253373/Raymond_W._Kulhavy)

### Inferences
- Zwaan & van Oostendorp sharpens the design case for the map. Readers of a serial will **not** reliably build a geography from the prose. A map that shows *where the protagonist is now and the route travelled* gives them a spatial frame they lack, which matches the conditions under which spatial-gradient effects appear (a learned layout plus protagonist tracking). The cost is attention: instructed spatial processing slowed reading. So the map belongs **beside** the reading (a tap away, or between chapters), not inside the sentence flow.
- Griffin & Robinson still point to **recognisable icons** (a tower, a ford) as the likelier memory aid than precise layout. An icon-led, stylised map is evidence-consistent.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on fantasy maps, progressively revealed maps or route traces with fiction readers.
- Morrow/Bower/Rinck details and Verdi & Kulhavy's conclusions remain abstract-level (Springer and T&F blocked).
- No study found on maps' effect on memory across weeks of serial reading.

## 4. Avatars and self-representation: Proteus effect, virtual self-modelling, customisation, idealised vs realistic, exercise and rehabilitation

### Takeaway
The core numbers stand. Ratan et al.: **46 of 83 candidate studies**, r = .22–.26. Its moderator tests were inconclusive; customisation and user control were only *qualitatively* linked to stronger effects. Newer exercise studies make the picture more conditional. Avatar effects on exercise are often **sex-dependent and temporary**, and a **muscular or idealised avatar can reduce movement**. The most relevant new study (Frontiers in VR, Sept 2026; patients with chronic low back pain): an avatar that **gains muscle gradually in real time in response to the user's own exercise** produced larger acute strength gains and a stronger sense of agency than one that **switched instantly** at the end, with medium effects. Customisation's benefits are confirmed (n = 126; n = 317). The Bath idealised-avatar result (CHI 2020 Best Paper) still lacks a verified n, and a 2021 follow-up found idealised avatars can either raise or lower power.

### Cited Findings
**Verification**
- **Ratan, Beyea, Li & Graciano (2020), *Media Psychology* 23(5):651–675, CONFIRMED and EXTENDED.** 83 studies were identified; **46** quantitative experiments with randomly assigned avatar characteristics were included. The effect fell between r = .22 and .26 depending on the subset (r = .24 is the PET-PEESE estimate cited later). **Moderator differences were not statistically clear (overlapping confidence intervals).** The authors offered *qualitative* evidence that factors raising user–avatar closeness (**user control, avatar customisation**) strengthen the effect. [META k=46] [abstract only] — [T&F record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/15213269.2019.1623698); [author-hosted PDF listing](https://www.benjy.li/pdfs/Ratan%20Li%20Graciano%20-%20Avatar%20characteristics.pdf)
  - Follow-up: Beyea, Ratan et al. (2022/2023), *PRESENCE*, "A new meta-analysis of the Proteus effect: studies in VR find stronger effect sizes" (56 studies), written partly because the 2020 analysis could not examine moderators well. [META k=56] [abstract only] — [MIT Press record](https://direct.mit.edu/pvar/article/doi/10.1162/pres_a_00392/116650/A-New-Meta-Analysis-of-the-Proteus-Effect-Studies)
- **Fox & Bailenson (2009), *Media Psychology* 12:1–25, PARTLY VERIFIED.** Study 1: participants were randomly assigned to (a) a virtual self that gained or lost weight in line with their real exercise, (b) an unchanging virtual self, or (c) no virtual self. Group (a) **exercised significantly more in a voluntary phase**. Sample: a publisher note says an initial N = 97, reduced to a **final N = 63** after a technical failure (with random down-sampling to balance conditions). It is unclear whether that note covers Study 1 only. The observed avatar was third-person in immersive VR. [CTRL ×3] [abstract only] — [T&F abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213260802669474); [Stanford VHIL PDF listing](https://vhil.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj29011/files/media/file/fox-mp-selfmodeling.pdf)
- **Birk, Atkins, Bowey & Mandryk (2016), CHI, pp. 2982–2995, CONFIRMED.** **126 participants** played a custom endless-runner. Identification (three forms) was associated with higher autonomy, immersion, invested effort, enjoyment and positive affect. A later 11-day study by the same group found that effort and enjoyment **waned faster for players who identified less**. [CTRL n=126] [abstract only] — [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/57d063bdac4436735428f563/fostering-intrinsic-motivation-through-avatar-identification-in-digital-games); [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/BirkABM16)

[META k=11; REVIEW; CTRL pilot] [abstract only, publication year of the first unconfirmed] — [PMC12723139 record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12723139/); [PMC12221897 record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12221897/)

### Inferences
- The Wardrobe's strongest evidence-based form is **effort-contingent, gradual change of the self-figure**. Hu et al. found a gradual, effort-linked transformation beat an instant reward-style switch on strength and agency in a patient group, and Fox & Bailenson's virtual self changed with real exercise. Pieces that open *as a consequence of logged work*, with the link visible ("this came from that"), fit this better than a store of items unlocked at once. The analogy is loose: theirs is real-time, body-tracked and acute; the site is asynchronous, a fantasy knight on a phone.
- Idealisation is a **mixed** risk, not a simple harm. Six-pack and idealised avatars lowered movement or power in some studies, raised strength or self-efficacy in others, and the effects were sex-dependent. That supports the earlier rule to keep the knight's *physical capability* in step with reality and let gear and adornment carry the aspiration.
- Several studies (Lin & Wu 2021; Mazeas follow-up, §5) show avatar and gamification effects are **temporary or fade**. Expect the Wardrobe's pull to need renewal (new pieces per Book) rather than one strong early effect.
- Customisation raised identification and engagement (Birk 2016; 2019). Birk & Mandryk 2019 also found customisation *without* a meaningful task reduced resilience to a low mood. Customisation should sit inside a purposeful loop, not stand alone.

### Gaps
- Koulouris et al. (2020) n and statistics; Fox & Bailenson per-study n and measures; Ratan's moderator table. All are behind T&F/ACM blocks.
- No study found of fantasy-character (non-likeness) self-avatars and real-world rehab adherence, or of AI-generated full-body self-images.
- No long-term (weeks–months) study of effort-linked avatar change.

## 5. Progress visuals and motivation: measurement, rewards, gamification, goal gradient, endowed progress, streaks

### Takeaway
One important correction: Deci, Koestner & Ryan's three negative d values belong to **three different reward contingencies**, not three analyses of one. Tangible rewards also hurt **children** more than college students. The physical-activity gamification numbers are confirmed (g = 0.42; **0.23 vs an active non-gamified programme**; 0.15 after it ends). New and strong: monitoring goal progress raises attainment (**k = 138, d = 0.40**), more when progress is recorded or reported. But progress *bars* do not reliably reduce quitting, and slow-looking bars *increase* it (k = 32). A natural experiment on 433,138 developers shows a visible **streak counter changes behaviour**: removing it ended long streaks, and people had been doing minimal "keep-alive" work.

### Inferences
- **Streak displays are powerful and double-edged.** The GitHub data show a visible counter drives minimal keep-alive actions and weekend work. In rehabilitation, where rest days and doing *exactly the prescribed dose* matter, a visible daily streak would reward "doing something every day", which is the wrong behaviour. Silverman & Barasch add that showing a broken streak demotivates. Both support the project's existing no-streak, no-penalty design.
- Harkin et al. support **recording progress somewhere visible** (the Ledger already does; the story reflecting it is a form of "reporting"). Etkin and Villar et al. warn that *how* progress is shown matters. A raw count on an enjoyable activity lowers enjoyment, and a bar that seems to crawl increases quitting. On the story side, show progress as **meaning and change** (a scene, a piece of gear, a place reached), not as a number or a filling bar. If bars are used (e.g. a technique's rank), early movement should feel quick (fast-to-slow) rather than slow.
- Deci et al.'s child/adult split slightly *reduces* the expected undermining effect for an adult. Its main lesson still holds: expected, contingent tangible rewards undermine; informational positive feedback enhances.
- Jahn et al. (an other-oriented narrative raised intrinsic motivation) is weak but suggestive support for framing the knight's progress around **who he is helping**, not loot.

- No study found comparing a story-wrapped progress display with a numeric one on exercise adherence.

## 6. Curiosity and hidden information: information gaps, uncertainty, teasing, unfinished business, hidden vs visible mechanics

### Takeaway
The curiosity findings hold at abstract level, and three additions strengthen them. Creating and then resolving an uncertainty ("teasing") yields a net positive experience (7 studies). Curiosity can drive people to resolve uncertainty even at a cost (Pandora effect, 4 experiments). A 2025 meta-analysis finds **no memory advantage for unfinished tasks** (Zeigarnik fails) but a **general urge to resume them** (Ovsiankina holds). That is the right mechanism for cliffhangers and greyed-out pieces. **No controlled study of hidden vs visible game mechanics was found**; design commentary disagrees.

### Cited Findings
- **Kang et al. (2009), *Psychological Science* 20(8), CONFIRMED + detail.** fMRI with **19** participants and 40 trivia questions. Curiosity correlated with caudate activity and followed an inverted U with confidence. In the behavioural follow-up, **higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1–2 weeks later**, and people **spent scarce resources (tokens, waiting time)** to learn answers they were curious about. The follow-up's n was not recovered. [CTRL n=19 + follow-up] [abstract only] — [Berkeley page](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/)
- **Shen, Fishbach & Hsee (2015), *J. Consumer Research* 41(5):1301–1315, CONFIRMED.** Water-drinking task: more people finished when the reward was a coin flip between $1 and $2 than a sure $2. A Godiva-chocolate bidding study (138 students per one summary) was not verified. The effect holds only when attention is on the *process*. The "walked farther" claim from marketing sites is unverified. [CTRL ×4] [abstract only] — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/762247)
- **Teasing effect (new):** Ruan, Hsee & Lu (2018), *J. Marketing Research* 55(4):556–570. **Seven studies.** Creating an uncertainty and then resolving it ("teasing") produced a net **positive** experience, though laypeople don't seek it out. Uncertainty builds curiosity; resolution satisfies it. An ad with a creation-and-resolution sequence raised attitude and willingness to try. [CTRL ×7] [abstract only] — [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Teasing-Effect-An-Underappreciated-Benefit/9984380448802771)
- **Pandora effect (new):** Hsee & Ruan (2016), *Psychological Science* 27(5):659–666. **Four experiments**: curiosity led people to expose themselves to aversive stimuli (even electric shocks) with no benefit, to resolve uncertainty. [CTRL ×4] [abstract only] — [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Pandora-Effect-The-Power-and/9984380412002771); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/04/160408101933.htm)
- **Zeigarnik vs Ovsiankina (new):** Ghibellini & Meier (2025), *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12:962. A meta-analysis of interrupted-task recall and resumption (about 59 publications per secondary coverage) found **no memory advantage for unfinished tasks** (the Zeigarnik effect "lacks universal validity") but **a general tendency to resume** interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect). [META] [abstract only] — [University of Bern PDF listing](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
- **Hidden vs visible mechanics: no experiment found.** Design commentary conflicts. One industry analysis claims hidden achievements motivate more and mainly exist to avoid spoilers; a UX guide argues hidden or tucked-away rewards lose much of their value. Both are opinion. [OPINION] [abstract only] — [Game Developer / Gamasutra](https://gamasutra.com/design/do-players-really-need-trophies-achievement-system-and-game-motivations)
- **Spoilers: no change** from the earlier pass (Leavitt & Christenfeld 2011 n = 819 vs later work finding harms for TV). Not re-verified.

### Inferences
- Greyed-out Wardrobe pieces that name what opens them, and an open story choice, are **Ovsiankina devices**: they create an urge to resume, not a memory advantage. Each needs a near, reachable resolution. The teasing effect requires that uncertainty **be resolved** for the net positive; a mystery left open indefinitely loses that benefit.
- The Pandora effect is a caution for a rehab context. Curiosity can push people to resolve uncertainty even at a cost. A hidden mechanic must never make *more training* the way to find out what happens. The engine's rule that overshooting earns nothing is the right guard.
- "Discover, don't study" remains untested as such. The evidence supports *partial* visibility: enough is shown to open an information gap (inverted U), and the gap closes on a schedule the person can see coming.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of hidden vs visible game rules, unlock conditions or progress on motivation or enjoyment.
- Kang et al.'s follow-up n; Shen et al.'s per-study statistics.
- No curiosity study over months-long serials.

## 7. New: narrative transportation and images, faces and identification, serialised fiction and recaps

### Takeaway
Transportation is best predicted by stories with identifiable characters and plots that readily evoke imagery (meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes). Adding a **photo of the story's character** to narrative text raised message effectiveness in a health-persuasion experiment (n = 299). No study tested portraits in prose fiction. For serials, the evidence is thin and conflicting: one small study found binge viewing lost more memory over months than spaced viewing; a replication found the reverse. **No study of recaps was found.**

### Cited Findings
- **Transportation meta-analysis:** van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014), *J. Consumer Research* 40(5):797–817, "The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model". **132 effect sizes from 76 articles.** Per the press release: consumers were most transported by realistic stories with **identifiable characters** and plots that readily produce **mental imagery**. More transportable traits: familiarity, attention, ability to fantasise, education, female gender (press wording, not checked against the moderator tables). [META k=132] [abstract only] — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/611188)
- **Photo of the character + narrative:** Ma & Yang (2022), *J. Health Communication*. 2 × 2 online experiment (**N = 299**) crossing narrative vs non-narrative text with exemplar vs non-exemplar visuals. **Narrative text paired with a photo of the character improved message effectiveness.** Related: matching the people pictured in health messages to the audience raised identification. ### Inferences
- Ma & Yang and van Laer support **one consistent face per character**, shown with their story. The Barcelona and AI-graphic-novel findings warn that faces carry unintended judgements, and inconsistencies are noticed. Per-Book portrait changes should keep the face recognisably the same and change only what the prose has changed.
- For a serial read in short phone sessions over months, the binge-vs-spaced evidence is too thin to justify either pacing. A short visual recap (the map's route, the cast's faces) at a chapter's start is plausible as retrieval support, but untested. It should also respect Harp & Mayer's placement finding: keep a recap brief, so it does not frame the new chapter with extra material.

### Gaps
- No experiment found on character portraits in prose fiction and identification, empathy or parasocial attachment.
- No study found on recaps, "story so far" summaries or visual recaps in serialised fiction.

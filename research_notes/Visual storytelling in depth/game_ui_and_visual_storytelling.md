# How narrative-heavy games use visuals, UI and graphics to carry story, stakes and growth: the in-depth pass

Research notes compiled 2026-11-17. This pass builds on `research_notes/Visual storytelling for the Chronicle/game_ui_and_visual_storytelling.md` (the "earlier pass"). That pass could open almost nothing. This one read 50+ primary pages in full: Game Developer, PC Gamer, the Failbetter and inkle blogs, GDC Vault talk pages, Frontiers, PMC, arXiv and NN/g. It does not repeat the earlier pass. It verifies the earlier pass's key claims (section 1), then adds new designer material, closes the listed gaps where it could, and reports academic work with methods, n and effect sizes.

**How to read the tags.** Each finding carries two tags.
- **Evidence:** **[D]** designer statement in their own words (blog, talk page, first-person interview); **[D via C]** designer quoted inside a journalist's article; **[C]** critic or journalist's own words (including paraphrase of a designer); **[B]** community blog on Game Developer (not staff, not the designer); **[S n=…]** study with sample size; **[W]** wiki or guide; **[F]** forum.
- **Read status:** **FULL** means the page was read in full this session. **SUMMARY** means only a search-engine summary was seen: the host was blocked, or the page sat behind a bot check.

## 2. Primary-source designer reasoning: how journals, maps, character screens, dice and portraits carry story

### Takeaway
Read in full, the designers converge on four principles. (1) **Show the pressure, hide the consequence table.** Darkest Dungeon exposes stress as hard numbers but deliberately does not list what an affliction does. Inkle hides whether qualities matter at all. Meier and Solomon quietly bend displayed odds toward the player's feelings. (2) **Prove you are paying attention, fast and visibly.** Inkle's map, the trait adjectives of 80 Days, Hades' reactive events and Telltale's notification all do this, and Vanaman warns that a notification creates the expectation that it will always appear. (3) **The record is a book the player fills in.** Obra Dinn's late-added book, Heaven's Vault's timeline and Failbetter's reminders for players who play in spurts all treat the record this way, and the "empty page" itself pulls the player forward. (4) **Abstract, few, thematic tokens beat many numbers.** Citizen Sleeper's dice and clocks, Failbetter's quality parsimony and Ingold's "+3 nice points" warning all point this way.

### Cited Findings

**Inkle: 80 Days and Heaven's Vault (journal, map, timeline)**
- Ingold on proving attention through the map: "The drawback when it comes to call-backs though is that you don't know until that call-back happens that it has been paying attention… If you want to prove to players that you are paying attention you have to do it really really fast. The way we did that is with the map. It proves to you that your choices do matter, as you can see which way you're going." [D via C · FULL] [Game Developer, "'T.M.I.': Preserving the suspension of disbelief through ambiguity", 10 Mar 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-t-m-i-preserving-the-suspension-of-disbelief-through-ambiguity)
- Ingold on 80 Days' trait adjectives: the game occasionally announces at the end of a chunk of story that the character has gained a trait like "suave" or "zesty". The article describes these as words standing for three numerically tracked traits. Ingold: "there are a lot of choices in the text about the kind of person you are and want to be… just having a little thing at the bottom that tells the player that we are paying some attention is just enough to make it feel significant." [D via C · FULL] [same article](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-t-m-i-preserving-the-suspension-of-disbelief-through-ambiguity)
- Ingold against visible morality numbers: "If you have a morality system just based on +3 nice points, it sort of undermines you from the start. It encourages the player to min-max." And on hidden state: "Do your items and qualities matter? Sometimes they do and sometimes they don't. Do we tell you that? Of course not. We make it up as we go to fit the story… Interactive fiction is a magic trick." [D via C · FULL] [same article](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-t-m-i-preserving-the-suspension-of-disbelief-through-ambiguity)
- The same article has the counterpoint from Sean Vanaman (writer and lead, The Walking Dead Season One) on "Clementine will remember that": "I didn't agree with that, because I thought it would produce the feeling that, any time that notification doesn't come up, people were going to know that 'Oh, I'm on a linear path, nothing has changed.'" And: "You never really know whether exposing the possibility space… or keeping that stuff hidden is going to take the player out of it… so much of it is gut." [D via C · FULL] [same article](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-t-m-i-preserving-the-suspension-of-disbelief-through-ambiguity)
- 80 Days' globe was also a way to see others' journeys: "you browse the globe watching other players - beta-testers, and your own past selves - as they race from train to steamer…" [D · FULL] [inkle blog, 14 Jul 2014](https://www.inklestudios.com/2014/07/14/approaching-journeys-end.html). The GDC China 2014 postmortem abstract: "Our initial design for 80 Days was simple - a choice-based story, laid out across a globe map, with a resource management element… [it] expanded… to become… a board-game, which narrates itself… We'll show… how the various game-elements - the art, the in-world economy, and the multiplayer - work together to create the narrative momentum." [D · FULL, abstract only; talk is members-only] [GDC Vault, Ingold](https://gdcvault.com/play/1021666/80-DAYS-Post-mortem-Letting)
- **Heaven's Vault timeline, in Ingold's own words:** "the player is an archaeologist, who records all her theories and discoveries on a timeline, covering everything from recent moment-to-moment events, to semi-mythological events that occurred millennia ago. Events on the timeline are, of course, tagged with the states in the knowledge model that unlock them. But we can re-use that tagged information to feed the relevance engine: so when a player looks up a piece of lore in the timeline, on returning to the game the characters may - without any further options or button presses - proceed to talk about it." The underlying model "tracks everything the player has done, seen, or theorised about the game-world". [D · FULL] [Jon Ingold, "Ideas and dynamic conversation in Heaven's Vault", Game Developer featured blog, 22 Aug 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/ideas-and-dynamic-conversation-in-heaven-s-vault)
- Critic on the same timeline: "Your record of accumulated sentences and translations is folded into an otherwise beautiful and fascinating timeline interface (which documents every move you make in the game and the entire historical record you begin to accumulate). The timeline is gorgeous and can prompt long stretches of perusal as you begin to satisfyingly fill in those gaps of decades and centuries. But it's a horrible way to store your translations." Also: "Throughout the game you see Aliya scribbling in a notebook. It should be a diegetic part of the interface, complete with a dictionary of the words you learn." [C · FULL] [Katherine Cross, Game Developer, "Boldly she rode", 22 Apr 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/boldly-she-rode-how-i-heaven-s-vault-i-reinvents-adventure-games)
- Ingold on puzzles: "We wanted to find a way to make puzzles that weren't abstract, but were narratively meaningful." [D via C · FULL] [Game Developer preview, 2 Mar 2017](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-heaven-s-vault-i-inkle-s-game-about-a-nebula-riding-archaeologist)
- Ingold's later GDC talk on deduction UIs: "Having uncovered a complex crime full of twists and turns, how can the player show the game they've solved it?… Overboard!'s deduction system… tackles the problem of proving specific feedback without removing the need for player creativity." [D · FULL, abstract only] [GDC Vault, "The Burden of Proof: Narrative Deduction Mechanics for Detective Games" (the fetched text did not show the year; Overboard! came out in 2021)](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027684/The-Burden-of-Proof-Narrative)

**Failbetter: Fallen London, Sunless Sea, Sunless Skies (qualities, maps, fog)**
- Quality parsimony and the UI: "Quality parsimony means using as few unique qualities as possible while maintaining clarity and stability. Excess qualities complicate the economy, cause bugs, and clutter up the UI for us and the players." [D · FULL] [Failbetter, "Fallen London writer guidelines part II", 15 Apr 2015](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-ii)
- Visible consequence on a character: "When you can see consequence from a character while interacting with them, it immediately makes them more tangible. Menaces are good for this - like Wounds or Scandal. Be cautious - a string of quality changes in a result makes each one less important." [D · FULL] [same](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-ii)
- Players who play in spurts: "Remember that people play FL in spurts, and sometimes take leaves of absence. Provide reminders in content so players can reorient themselves." Also: "It's fine (often preferable) to hold off giving rewards for a while, then grant them in a chunk at the end of a content strand." [D · FULL] [same](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-ii)
- Telling players how long hardship lasts: "If we're making you suffer, you want to know how long you're going to be up on that cross. It's no fun to just be up there and not know when we're going to let you down. So we're making a point in our more robustly unsympathetic content to let you know just how much more of this shit you're going to have to take." The same post says few qualities "minimises the number of things players have to remember to make sense of stories". [D · FULL] [Failbetter, "Don't poke that: narrative engineering principles part 1", 9 Jul 2011](https://www.failbettergames.com/dont-poke-that-narrative-engineering-principles-part-1)
- Fog of the unknown as a record of discovery: in Sunless Sea, inheriting "your mentor's map" "will keep the map as it was with your last character… It will also leave fog-of-unknown cleared - which is a mixed blessing, because you can't claim Secrets from clearing it twice." The West of the map "is always stable, but the unknown reaches can change entirely." [D · FULL] [Failbetter, "Designing in the open part 1: maiden voyage", 13 Oct 2013](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/designing-in-the-open-part-1-maiden-voyage). A 2019 primer adds that in Sunless Skies experience from map discovery passes to the successor captain, and "Captains lock parts of the map they've discovered by docking at a major port." [D · FULL] [Failbetter, 31 Jan 2019](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/sunless-sea-vs-sunless-skies-death-legacies-and-repetition)
- Drawing a world that existed only in words: "we are building a world that previously only existed as words and ideas, both in our minds and yours. So there's inevitably going to be some dissonance between the mental picture we've built up and what appears in the game. 'So wait, the Iron Republic is there?' 'Whoa, Mutton Island is bigger than I imagined.'" Scale had to be "internally coherent" rather than realistic. [D · FULL] [Failbetter (Paul Arendt), "Mapping the Sunless Sea", 19 Nov 2013](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/mapping-the-sunless-sea-geography-spoilers)
- Maps need landmarks: "Because one group of rocks or bronzewood looks much like another, it was difficult for players to build up an internal geography of the space in their heads, and so their captains were spending too long flying through featureless fog in the general direction of an icon on their map." The fix gave players "two or three paths to choose from" with known risk and reward. Also: "Our focus in survival design is anecdote generation… our most enthusiastic reviews tend to be first-person accounts of disaster and mutiny… We've never been particularly interested in empowering players." [D · FULL] [Paul Arendt, "Redesigning the Reach", Failbetter, 10 Oct 2018](https://www.failbettergames.com/redesigning-the-reach)

**Darkest Dungeon: stress meters, afflictions, barks (Red Hook)**
- Exposing the meter was a deliberate reversal: "Although we wanted parts of the Affliction system to be purposefully opaque at first glance, we realized early on that we needed to completely expose the Stress meter and display stress increases and decreases directly as numbers -- for example, +20 Stress!… while this presentation is something we initially tried to avoid, the meter provides a tangible thing for players to try and 'game.' This is a good thing: It provides clear feedback and consequences, while still allowing an organic narrative to develop." [D · FULL] [Bourassa & Sigman, "Game Design Deep Dive: Darkest Dungeon's Affliction System", Game Developer, 28 May 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-deep-dive-i-darkest-dungeon-s-i-affliction-system)
- Consequences were deliberately hidden: "In contrast to the transparency of the Stress meter, we purposefully don't list all the behaviors that any given Affliction may have. We want you to have to observe and react to your hero as it happens… In this way your time and veteranship are rewarded." [D · FULL] [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-deep-dive-i-darkest-dungeon-s-i-affliction-system)
- Staging the check as a moment: "Since drama is the name of the game, we sunk additional time and effort into the presentation of these moments. ### Inferences
- **For the flame meter, Arts and Wardrobe** (Darkest Dungeon's split): make the pressure or progress visible and legible, but let what it *does* be discovered. Show the flame and the Art cards plainly. Never list what the next threshold unlocks. This is exactly Red Hook's "transparent stress, undisclosed afflictions", which they say rewards "time and veteranship". It fits the author's wish to discover the mechanics, not study them.
- **For companion regard** (Ingold, Vanaman): words and glyphs instead of numbers avoid the "+3 nice points" min-maxing trap. But a regard change must appear **reliably at the moment it happens**, like 80 Days' adjective "at the bottom". Vanaman's warning: once readers learn that a glyph appears when someone's view of the knight shifts, its absence will read as "nothing changed".
- **For the map** (inkle, Failbetter): the route line is the fastest proof that the story remembers. Arendt's "featureless fog… in the general direction of an icon" warns that a painted map needs **distinct landmarks** where the route bends, not uniform terrain. Sunless Sea's cleared fog is a clean model for "only what the prose has shown": the fog lifts only where the chapters have gone.
- **For the Codex** (Obra Dinn, Heaven's Vault, Cross): use the book metaphor (chapters, bookmarks, glossary, contents), which readers parse "without detailed instructions". Keep the **chronological record** (chapters, timeline) separate from the **reference** (Codex entries). Cross's complaint is that Heaven's Vault mixed the two. Ingold's tagging of each timeline entry to the knowledge state that unlocked it is the same as tagging each Codex entry with the chapter that revealed it.
- **For a weekly serial read in spurts on a phone** (Failbetter): "Provide reminders in content so players can reorient themselves." The NOW or recap page and the opening of each chapter are where this belongs. Failbetter's "you want to know how long you're going to be up on that cross" suggests the rope of knots should show **how many knots a Book holds**, so a hard stretch has a visible shape. That is structure, not dates or targets.
- **For dice glyphs** (Citizen Sleeper, Darkest Dungeon, Solomon): treat a roll as a staged *moment* (banner → tense pause → reveal), and let abstract tokens carry meaning ("success at a cost"). Do not show odds: Solomon's "players have invested a lot of emotion in" the number, and Meier's "paranoia sets in", both argue against inviting readers to audit probabilities.
- **For restraint in images** (Pope, Martin, Rostov): "more empty space for them to fill in the blanks", and "you are asked to fill in the details". Art that leaves things out fits the rule that a picture shows only what the prose has shown. Rostov's "design the entire game as if it was a painting" supports composing each page or sheet as one image with a guided eye path, which suits a phone screen.
- **For "pointless" flourishes** (Kasavin, Sawyer): both lead designers defend small unnecessary details as what makes a game special ("dumb stuff", "maximum viable product"). This licenses a few non-functional touches (a glyph that settles, ink that dries) provided they stay redundant with the text, as Pentiment's fonts do.

## 4. Academic work: UI and narrative comprehension, diegetic UI, visible vs hidden information, customisation and identification

### Takeaway
The strongest controlled evidence is on **customisation → identification**, and it is consistent. Choosing an avatar's look raises identification with large effects in small lab studies (d = 0.69, n = 24; ηp² = .69, N = 44) and small-to-medium effects in a large preregistered online study (ηp² .01–.09). Effects on **performance or fun are null or mixed**. In exergames, **idealised** avatars raised wishful identification but **lowered** physical performance, while **realistic avatars enhanced with idealised traits** had no such cost (CHI 2020, summary only). Diegetic-UI evidence remains small and conditional. There is still **no controlled study** linking codex, journal or progression-screen design to story comprehension. The nearest work (ClueCart, N = 40) concerns players organising fragmented lore with an external tool.

### Cited Findings

**Customisation and identification (read in full)**
- **Böffel et al. 2022**, *Frontiers in Psychology* (League of Legends). Within-subjects lab experiment, **n = 24** (14 female, 10 male, aged 19–32). Participants played a 10-minute "last-hit" task with a self-chosen cosmetic skin vs the default. Identification was higher with the customised avatar: **t(23) = 3.391, p = 0.003, d = 0.692**. There was no effect on fun (d = 0.230, n.s.), perceived competence (d = 0.162, n.s.), estimated performance (d = 0.149) or actual performance (d = 0.014). Identification correlated with fun and competence. The authors note that lab reactivity (feeling judged for cosmetic choices) may have suppressed fun. [S n=24 · FULL] [PMC8765231](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8765231/)
- **Rheu, Ratan, Sah, Cherchiglia & Day 2022**, *Frontiers in Virtual Reality* 3:873689, "Jogging in Your Avatar's Footsteps". 2 (customised vs randomly given avatar) × 2 (intuitive vs scrambled controls) within-subjects design, **N = 44** students, runner game followed by a real treadmill run. Customisation raised identification: **F(1,43) = 95.12, p < .001, ηp² = .69** (M = 3.06 vs 2.17). It also raised embodiment: F(1,43) = 4.13, p = .05, ηp² = .09. Unintuitive controls reduced identification with the *customised* avatar (interaction F(1,43) = 5.67, p = .02, ηp² = .12) and slowed the subsequent real run (F(1,43) = 6.62, p = .01, ηp² = .13). The predicted mediation (identification → physical activity) was **not** found. [S n=44 · FULL] [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/virtual-reality/articles/10.3389/frvir.2022.873689/full)
- **Kao et al., CHI 2022**, "Audio Matters Too". Preregistered 2 × 2 (visual choice vs assignment × voice choice vs assignment), online on MTurk, Java programming game. The power analysis targeted **N = 1,500**. Visual choice raised identification and autonomy, raised time spent, and indirectly raised intrinsic motivation, immersion and willingness to play again. Voice choice helped only when visual choice was also available. Effect sizes: **ηp² 0.01–0.09** for visual-choice main effects, about 0.01 for interactions. [S, planned N=1,500 · FULL (arXiv version)] [arXiv 2202.05315](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.05315v1)

**Customisation in exergames (summary only)**
- **Koulouris, Jeffery, Best, O'Neill & Lutteroth, CHI 2020**, "Me vs. Super(wo)man: Effects of Customization and Identification in a VR Exergame" (CHI Best Paper). Three studies on a self-competitive VR exercycle game. **Study 1:** customisation "significantly increased identification and intrinsic motivation, as well as physical performance". **Study 2:** "idealised avatars increased wishful identification but decreased exergame performance compared to realistic avatars". **Study 3:** "'enhancing' realistic avatars with idealised characteristics increased wishful identification, but did not have any adverse effects". The authors propose "intrinsic" and "extrinsic" identification. Sample sizes were not visible in the summaries. [S, n not seen · SUMMARY; ACM blocked] [DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376661](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376661); [project page](https://clutteroth.github.io/reveal/CHI2020-Super(wo)man/index.html)
- **Ratan, Beyea, Li & Graciano 2020**, *Media Psychology*, Proteus effect meta-analysis: **46** experimental studies; avatar characteristics induce behavioural conformity with **small-to-medium** effects, at the high end of media-effects meta-analyses. Studies of interactions (customisation, user control, gender) explain more variance. [S, meta-analysis k=46 · SUMMARY; Taylor & Francis returned 403] [tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2019.1623698)

**Diegetic UI and immersion**
- **Iacovides et al., CHI PLAY 2015** (pp. 13–22; dblp key IacovidesCKCJ15), "Removing the HUD". Two versions of the same FPS (Battlefield 3), with and without the HUD and other non-diegetic elements, measured with the Immersive Experience Questionnaire. Removing non-diegetic elements raised cognitive involvement and sense of control, and so immersion, **in expert players**. The sample size and expert/novice split were not visible in the summaries. [S, n not seen · SUMMARY] [White Rose open access record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/130581/); [DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2793107.2793120)
- Smaller studies, summary only: a VR within-subjects study (n = 14) found a 3D diegetic store more joyful and immersive but a 2D list "slightly more efficient". A VRCHI 2025 usability study (n = 15) found diegetic displays "may reduce usability". A mixed-reality fitness game found diegetic elements raised engagement, but two users reported cognitive overload when many elements appeared at once. [S small n · SUMMARY] [ACM 10.1145/3656650.3656670](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3656650.3656670); [DCU VRCHI 2025 PDF](https://doras.dcu.ie/32086/1/VRCHI2025-CameraReady-v1.pdf); [ACM 10.1145/3706599.3720061](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706599.3720061)
- A new 2026-era ACM paper, "How Diegetic Maps Shape Wayfinding, Storytelling, and Immersion", exists, but only its title was seen. [S? · TITLE ONLY] [ACM 10.1145/3815598.3815655](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3815598.3815655)

**Visible vs hidden information; randomness**
- Input vs output randomness in a purpose-built collectable card game, four conditions: "input randomness can significantly impact game satisfaction negatively". Input randomness is randomness *before* the decision, such as the draw; output randomness comes after, such as a roll on resolution. [S, n not in abstract · FULL abstract only] [arXiv 2107.08437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08437)
- "The Role of Uncertainty in Moment-to-Moment Player Motivation", CHI PLAY 2019. The authors (Kumari, Deterding & Freeman) are from memory; the search result did not show them. Some "perceived lack of certain knowledge about what is the case, what to do, or what will happen" is core to the motivational pull of play. It separates content uncertainty (Costikyan's "hidden information") from outcome randomness. [S, qualitative · SUMMARY] [ACM PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3311350.3347148)
- No controlled study was found that compares *visible vs hidden relationship values* or *displayed vs hidden odds* on story attention. The designer evidence (Solomon, Meier, Red Hook, Ingold) remains the main source.

**UI and narrative comprehension**
- **ClueCart (arXiv 2503.06098, 2025):** "Supporting Game Story Interpretation and Narrative Inference from Fragmented Clues". A formative co-design workshop (14 participants) built a taxonomy of narrative clues in games that use indexical storytelling (Elden Ring, Dark Souls). A **between-subjects study with N = 40 creators** compared the tool against Miro. ClueCart "significantly improved creators' efficiency in organizing and retrieving clues". The participants were lore-video creators, not ordinary players, and comprehension was partly self-reported. [S n=40 · FULL] [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06098v1)

**Interface usability (applies to glyphs and progressive disclosure on a phone)**
- NN/g on icons: "A user's understanding of an icon is based on previous experience. Due to the absence of a standard usage for most icons, text labels are necessary to communicate the meaning and reduce ambiguity." Heart and star icons in particular "are hard to memorize and interpret precisely". In one study a clock icon for history was clicked by "not a single test participant". [Usability research summary · FULL] [NN/g, Aurora Harley, "Icon Usability", 27 Jul 2014](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)
- NN/g on progressive disclosure: "Disclose these secondary features only if a user asks for them"; "Deferring secondary material is also a key guideline for mobile design"; "people understand a system better when you help them prioritize features". The secondary link must have strong "information scent". [Usability research summary · FULL] [NN/g, Jakob Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure", 3 Dec 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)

### Inferences
- **Wardrobe and portrait images.** The evidence says letting the author choose the knight's look will raise identification (large effects in the lab, consistent across studies). It does not say that this raises enjoyment or real-world performance. The exergame result (Koulouris, summary only) is directly relevant to a story driven by real training: **idealised** bodies raised wishful identification but **lowered** exercise performance, while **realistic bodies with a few idealised touches** did not. That supports keeping the knight's images grounded and changing per Book, rather than heroic from the start. It also matches the sheet's framing of being measured "against his former self". This is an inference from VR-exercise studies to static images and needs that caveat.
- **Regard glyphs.** NN/g's finding is that most icons are ambiguous without labels, hearts and stars especially. Pair each regard glyph with its word on first sight, or always on the sheet. The glyph can stand alone in the prose margin once readers have learned it.
- **Odds behind a tap.** Progressive disclosure, Iacovides' expert-only immersion gain and the input/output randomness result together support this layout: the default view is the result glyph and the stakes framing; odds and modifiers sit one tap deeper, with clear "scent". Rolls should come **after** the decision in the story (output randomness), never as random constraints imposed before the reader has chosen.
- **Codex.** ClueCart suggests that dedicated readers of fragmented lore go to outside tools (Miro, wikis) to organise it. A Codex that cross-links entries to the chapters that revealed them does that organising work on the site.

### Gaps
- There is still no controlled study on codex, journal or progression-screen design and story comprehension or recall. ClueCart is the closest and studies creators, not readers.
- No study tests visible vs hidden relationship values, or displayed vs hidden odds, against attention to story.
- Full texts of Iacovides 2015, Koulouris 2020 and Ratan 2020 were not reachable (bot challenges on ACM, Taylor & Francis and Springer). Their n and effect sizes are not reported here.
- Springer's "Character identification is predicted by narrative transportation, immersive tendencies, and interactivity" (Current Psychology, 2022) was blocked by a Springer client challenge. [SUMMARY] [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-022-03048-4)

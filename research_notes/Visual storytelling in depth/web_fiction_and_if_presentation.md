# Web fiction, interactive fiction and visual-novel presentation: the in-depth pass (primary pages)

Compiled 17 Nov 2026. This pass re-reads the earlier note (`research_notes/Visual storytelling for the Chronicle/web_fiction_and_if_presentation.md`) against full pages, corrects it, closes its listed gaps where possible, and adds new primary material.

**How each claim is marked.** Every bullet carries a bracket: evidence type · how it was read · date of the source. Evidence types: **Study** (peer-reviewed or controlled, with n where known), **Data** (measured figures from a platform or publisher), **Designer** (statement by the people who made the thing), **Docs** (official documentation, policy or spec), **Press** (journalism, review, criticism), **Community** (forum, fan wiki content, reader opinion). Reading status: **full page read** means the whole page was downloaded and read in this session; **summary only** means only a search engine's extract was seen.

**Method and limits.** Pages were fetched with curl and converted to text. All of these were read in full: inklestudios.com, failbettergames.com, nngroup.com, gamedeveloper.com, gdcvault.com (session page only; the talk is a video), wiki.wanderinginn.com (through its MediaWiki API, current revisions as of 17 Nov 2026), frontiersin.org and arxiv.org. Three hosts on the "allowed" list did not serve readable pages: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov returned a reCAPTCHA page, link.springer.com returned a JavaScript challenge, and www.tandfonline.com returned 403. WebFetch could not resolve either PMC or Taylor & Francis. royalroad.com returned 403 on its search page. Claims from those hosts are marked summary only. Hosts outside the list (developer.chrome.com, emshort.blog, wanderinginn.com, Vice, PC Gamer, Kotaku, GamesRadar, Wikipedia) are summary only as well.

## 1. Verifying the earlier pass: what held, what was wrong

### Takeaway
Most of the earlier note's designer quotes are real, but five are wrong in ways that matter. The "reads the text by accident" line describes 80 Days, not Overboard!, and it is a journalist's paraphrase, not an inkle quote. "Swiftly rendered in a naturalist's notebook" comes from Sunless *Skies* (2017), not Sunless Sea. The "camera so far away, much was left to interpretation" quote could not be found anywhere on Failbetter's site. Mask of the Rose shows alliance by which way characters face, not by how close they stand. The Wandering Inn wiki's Patreon ban is official policy, not a draft. The NN/g and Failbetter figures that were checked are correct, and they now have exact dates and authors.

### Cited Findings
- **80 Days, "part interactive fiction, part globe-based board-game": verified, but the source was wrong.** The phrase is from inkle's own blog: "we described it as 'part interactive fiction, part globe-based board-game'". It is not in the Game Developer postmortem. [Designer · full page read · 15 Apr 2014] — [inkle: So what is 80 Days?](https://www.inklestudios.com/2014/04/15/so-what-is-eighty-days.html)
- **80 Days, "visit any city on the globe, and build their own route": verified** in a different inkle post: "for our version of 80 Days, we wanted the player to be able to go anywhere, visit any city on the globe, and build their own route. - **80 Days, "showed almost all the routes on the globe": verified, and the fix is the useful part.** Postmortem by Jon Ingold and Joseph Humfrey: "In our original design, we showed almost all the game routes on the globe… about halfway (and two hundred thousand words) into development, we realized that… it simply wasn't fun." Players "were skipping through city content without reading it". The fix was twofold: "First, we locked all the routes on the globe. Secondly, we introduced the Explore mechanic", and routes became rewards ("Talked to a merchant? Have a camel!"). [Designer, postmortem · full page read · 14 Oct 2015] — [Game Developer: Postmortem: Inkle's 80 Days](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-inkle-s-i-80-days-i-)

The figures differ by date and by whether planned or shipped cities are counted. [Designer · full page read · 2014/2015] — [postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-inkle-s-i-80-days-i-); [inkle blog](https://www.inklestudios.com/2014/04/15/so-what-is-eighty-days.html)

### Inferences
- The 80 Days postmortem points the opposite way from how the earlier note read it. The lesson is not only "show the route taken". It is also that **showing every possible route made people stop reading**, and that hiding routes and handing them out as rewards brought the reading back. For a serial map, that supports drawing roads only as the story travels them.
- Failbetter's "the imagination of the player… we don't want anything interrupting that line of communication" (2012) is the clearest designer statement in favour of a quiet, decoration-light reading surface.

### Gaps
- The source of "much was left to interpretation" is unknown. It may come from a talk or an interview, not the blog.
- Vice's 2014 80 Days UI interview (Ingold's "graphic design problem" quote) could not be re-read (host not allowed). It stays summary only.
- No Game Developer reprint of the Sunless Sea postmortem was found. The "415 illustrations" figure surfaced only in a forum comment (summary only) and is unverified.

## 2. inkle and Failbetter on presentation: maps, timelines, text, cards, art and wardrobe (primary writing)

### Takeaway
Both studios design the screen around keeping the reader reading. Text comes in small, re-readable portions. Interfaces show only what the player has earned or learned: 80 Days' locked routes, Fallen London's Unterzee map that "will fill in slowly as they learn about each location", and Heaven's Vault's timeline, whose entries are "tagged with the states in the knowledge model that unlock them". Art is briefed from the writing and carries meaning in its details. Failbetter's Mandrake portraits are composed "a bit like tarot cards", with details that later turn out to be clues. Failbetter also found that a painted map of a world first met in words always causes "some dissonance" with readers' mental pictures. Mask of the Rose's wardrobe describes in the character's own voice how an outfit will come across, instead of showing stat numbers.

### Cited Findings
**inkle**
- **The route as story; content gated by where you are.** 80 Days is "inherently episodic - much as what happens in Vegas stays in Vegas, so does what happens in Tehran…". Only money, health, time and three traits (skilfulness, style, "properness") are tracked "from city to city", which let the writer "write scenes entirely out of order". After the design change, "the story content for cities became something you wouldn't necessarily ever see", and the game "suddenly felt player-driven, slick, and quick". [Designer, postmortem · full page read · 14 Oct 2015] — [Game Developer: Postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-inkle-s-i-80-days-i-)
- **Art direction keyed to the platform.** inkle chose an Art Deco travel-poster style over "typical" steampunk, to "sit well with iOS 7" (flat design, primary colour highlights). The art "was really telegraphing the tone of the game". [Designer · full page read · 14 Oct 2015] — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-inkle-s-i-80-days-i-)
- **Text UX talk (Humfrey, GDC 2018).** The session description: inkle's games "rely on interactive text as their core mechanic". The techniques make text "frictionless to read and interact with": "principles of good typography and typesetting across a range of devices". Animation "can play a critical role in leading the eye", and "Eliminating distractions helps the player focus on the act of reading". There are "two major pillars of text UX design, focus and pacing". The talk is listed as free content but is a video, which this pass did not watch. [Designer · full page read (description only) · GDC 2018] — [GDC Vault: Designing Text UX for Effortless Reading](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025104/Designing-Text-UX-for-Effortless)
- **Heaven's Vault: a timeline that only knows what you know.** Ingold: "the player is an archaeologist, who records all her theories and discoveries on a timeline, covering everything from recent moment-to-moment events, to semi-mythological events that occurred millennia ago. Events on the timeline are, of course, tagged with the states in the knowledge model that unlock them." Each line is tested twice: are its requirements met, and has anything made it "redundant"? There are "all 10,000+ lines of dialogue". Opening a timeline entry feeds a "relevance engine", so "when a player looks up a piece of lore in the timeline, on returning to the game the characters may… proceed to talk about it." [Designer · full page read · 22 Aug 2018] — [Game Developer: Ideas and dynamic conversation in Heaven's Vault](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/ideas-and-dynamic-conversation-in-heaven-s-vault)
- **Heaven's Vault's visual presentation goal.** Humfrey: what inkle is proudest of is "the way the narrative flows… and part of it is about the surface layer - how we visually present the story, and how the player interacts with it." After "a series of predominantly text-based games… we wanted to try something more visual." Humfrey was lead programmer and "responsible for art direction and UI design". [Designer · full page read · 24 Jan 2020] — [Game Developer: Road to the IGF: Heaven's Vault](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-inkle-s-i-heaven-s-vault-i-)
- **Heaven's Vault timeline: how it works (reviews).** Each object you find adds a short text box to a timeline you can review at any time. Zooming out shows major events over about 1,000 years, and zooming in shows the protagonist's life. A small pop-up tells you when something mentioned in conversation has been added. [Press · summary only · 2019] — [GamesRadar review](https://gamesradar.com/heavens-vault-review); [Emily Short on Heaven's Vault](https://emshort.blog/2019/07/23/heavens-vault-inkle/)
- **Overboard!: a "Questions" list that advertises unseen content without spoiling it.** Ingold: "The game tracks things Veronica has perceived but not fully seen across games, and opens them as a question: things like 'Who is inside Carstairs cabin?'… fed into a tick-box menu that pops up at the start of the run… if they want to." It "simply advertises that the game is still wider than you think it is". ink also drives "the pop-up notifications when you learn new information". The interface and the fast-replay system were built in Unity. [Designer · full page read · 1 Mar 2022] — [Game Developer: How to get away with murder in Overboard!](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-get-away-murder-overboard)
- **Overboard! UI credits (secondary).** Recorded playtests "informed so much of the story and interface", and the fast-forward mode "arose from playtests" (PC Gamer). One mirror credits Tom Kail with the UI, Humfrey as art director and Anastasia Wyatt with the cast art. [Designer via press · summary only · 2021–22] — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/how-the-studio-behind-80-days-made-one-of-the-best-games-of-2021-in-just-3-months); [Blogspot mirror of "Behind the Design"](https://appleinfo00.blogspot.com/2022/09/behind-design-overboard.html)
- **A Highland Song: tell only about what the player has seen.** Ingold: "I might write a wonderful series of remarks on the subject of wild Scottish heather, but if the player hasn't seen any, doesn't know what it is, and anyway, is up in the snow-capped mountains… it's not going to be very relevant." What Moira can ask about is "highly contextual, driven by what you've found and what you've seen". Story arrives as remembered letters "as you settle down to sleep". [Designer · full page read · 3 May 2022] — [inkle: Letters](https://www.inklestudios.com/highland/2022/05/03/letters.html)
- **Stories as wayfinding.** Ingold draws on Chatwin's *The Songlines*: the "traveller recalls a tale, the major beats of which take place at major landmarks along the route… The stories are the journey, and the journey repeats and reinforces the story." He hedges that this account "may or may not be true". [Designer · full page read · 8 Feb 2021] — [inkle: The Songlines](https://www.inklestudios.com/highland/2021/02/08/the-songlines.html)
- **Letting readers leave a scene.** inkle's "autocut" lets players walk out of any conversation, and "ink can still track which bits of content you've seen and which bits you've skipped". [Designer · full page read · 11 May 2023] — [inkle: Autocut](https://www.inklestudios.com/highland/2023/05/11/autocut-for-responsive.html)
- **Tales chosen by context (Pendragon).** inkle commissioned 23 campfire tales from more than 400 submissions. Which ones you hear "will depend on who's in your party to do the telling… A cheerful tale might follow a victory; a sad tale might follow a defeat." [Designer · full page read · 12 May 2020] — [inkle: Pendragon campfire tales](https://www.inklestudios.com/2020/05/12/pendragon-campfire-tales.html)
- **"Seamless" text and conditional wording.** inkle's 2012 essay contrasts storylets ("cards in a deck") with a "smooth, endless stream of text". It notes that repeated reminders ("The man in the hat told you to knock twice") read as clumsy in continuous prose, and recommends conditional text that shortens a second mention to "That man" or "He". [Designer · full page read · 6 Jun 2012] — [inkle: Seamless storytelling](https://www.inklestudios.com/2012/06/06/seamless-storytelling.html)

**Failbetter**
- **Writing for re-reading: "exactly short enough".** "Very tiny amounts of text aren't rich enough to support this, and very large blocks of text just invite the eye to skip over them altogether. We aim for exactly short enough." Detail should work "like focal points in a painting… We want to aspire to Rembrandt or Vermeer… not Van Gogh or Picasso." "We're telling the player what's happening, but we shouldn't be telling them how to feel about it." [Designer · full page read · 20 Jun 2011] — [Failbetter: Points of light, pools of shadow (part I)](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/points-of-light-pools-of-shadow-part-i)
- **Text-first is a choice.** At an awards show, Echo Bazaar's footage was "a looong pan across mostly beige, mostly text". [Designer · full page read · 13 Mar 2010] — [Failbetter: Mostly beige, mostly text](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/mostly-beige-mostly-text)
- **Rich text formatting matters even in engines.** Game engines default to "One font size, no emphasis through bold or italic text, a paragraph break if you're lucky". For Sunless Sea, Failbetter split differently formatted runs into separate objects so writers keep italics and the like. [Designer · full page read · 5 Mar 2014] — [Failbetter: Text in games](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/text-in-games)
- **Maps of worlds first met as words.** Arendt: "we are building a world that previously only existed as words and ideas, both in our minds and yours. So there's inevitably going to be some dissonance between the mental picture we've built up and what appears in the game. 'So wait, the Iron Republic is *there*?'" On scale: boats are realistic because "they will tend to be the player's point of reference", while "geography is heavily scaled down". [Designer · full page read · 19 Nov 2013] — [Failbetter: Mapping the Sunless Sea (geography spoilers!)](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/mapping-the-sunless-sea-geography-spoilers)
- **A map that fills in as you learn.** Fallen London's Unterzee map: "Experienced characters will be able to see all or most of the map immediately. For new players, the map will fill in slowly as they learn about each location." [Designer · full page read · 15 Sep 2020] — [Failbetter: Map for the Unterzee](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/map-for-the-unterzee)
- **Map beta testing for continuity and accessibility.** For Fallen London's new city map (its 10th birthday), Failbetter asked about "places not appearing where you'd expect" and was "particularly interested to hear from players who use a screen reader". [Designer · full page read · 27 Jan 2020] — [Failbetter: Fallen London Map Beta](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/a-matter-of-cartographical-import-fallen-london-map-beta)
- **One responsive site instead of apps.** The 2018 Fallen London redesign aimed to "Improve the overall mobile play experience and responsiveness of the site", and Failbetter "recently retired our mobile apps" so it maintains "one product". [Designer · full page read · 2 Oct 2018] — [Failbetter: Fallen London has a new look](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-has-a-new-look)
- **Reading UI and type options (Mask of the Rose).** Failbetter "consulted with Joseph Humfrey of Inkle" when redesigning the narrative interface. They departed "from Visual Novel staples like text boxes and bubbles… whilst maintaining readability". Options include "adjustable text speed to sans-serif and non-cursive text variants. We have three separate font sizes". "With reading at the centre of the game it's vital that players can tailor text appearance." [Designer · full page read · 18 Aug 2022] — [Failbetter: Production Update and UI Revamp](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/production-update-and-ui-revamp)
- **A wardrobe that talks back, not +1 stats.** Séamus Ó Buadhacháin: "baldly describing a given hat as +1 Coquettish didn't seem to fit the theme… when you put on a set of outfit items, the wardrobe view talks back to you instead: your own player character telling you, in English, how you're likely to come across." Descriptions come from a template grammar, because combinations run into "the thousands" (Fallen London has "around 120 billion outfit combinations"). [Designer · full page read · 10 Mar 2022] — [Failbetter: Dressing Outrageously for Success in Mask of the Rose](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/dressing-outrageously)
- **Outfits that cannot change mid-scene (Fallen London).** Outfit changes were restricted "where your character wouldn't reasonably be able to access their wardrobe". The rule was scaled back after controversy, because backing out to change clothes added clicks. [Designer · full page read · 29–31 Jul 2020] — [Failbetter: Update to Outfits](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/update-to-outfits-in-fallen-london)
- **Portraits as "tarot cards" whose details become clues (Mandrake).** Narrative director Chris Gardiner: "We started talking about the illustrations a bit like tarot cards, where the details suggest a bigger story… details that just seem like decoration at first, but as the player gets to know the character more, those details are revealed to be clues to their past, or a secret they keep." Artist Erion Makuo: "Their clothes often say a lot about what they do in the village, their social station". She flipped portraits because they sit left of the dialogue and "the composition needs to lead the eye to the right… whatever we draw in the bottom right… has to be *really* good." Gardiner: "when I see the final illustration, I always find it easier to write the character". One illustration "added something entirely new to our lore" (a character's tattoos), which the writers adopted. [Designer · full page read · 2 Dec 2025] — [Failbetter: Designing characters for Mandrake](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/character-illustrations-in-mandrake)
- **Environment detail as story.** Makuo on a family's home interior: "Because you're asking those questions, the details have meaning. There's something worthwhile for the players to discover." Structural beams were cut "to make the interior read better at a glance". [Designer · full page read · 22 Jul 2025] — [Failbetter: Home and Hearth](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/home-and-hearth)
- **Art briefed from character profiles.** Mask of the Rose's cast was designed from conflicts ("one internal conflict and one external conflict for each character") before the art. The art post says painterly rendering was chosen "to distinguish the look of the game from other visual novels". [Designer · full page read · 10 Mar 2021 / 4 Mar 2021] — [Failbetter: Designing Characters for Mask of the Rose](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/designing-characters-for-mask-of-the-rose); [The Art of Mask of the Rose](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/the-art-of-mask-of-the-rose-bring-the-characters-to-life)
- **Sunless Skies art requirements.** The look had to "be recognisable but different", "evoke a late Victorian, fin-de-siecle period", "encourage exploration and a spirit of discovery" and "suggest a new frontier, only partially explored". Fog was chosen because "you can suggest giant shapes that may or may not repay closer inspection". Each region gets "its own distinctive colour palette" for navigation. [Designer · full page read · 5 Jan 2017] — [Failbetter: Looking to the Skies](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/sunless-skies-pre-production-looking-to-the-skies)

### Inferences
- **The reveal rule has strong designer precedent.** Heaven's Vault tags every timeline event with the knowledge states that unlock it. The Unterzee map "fills in slowly as they learn". 80 Days hides routes until earned. Highland refuses to talk about heather you haven't seen. Together they are a close professional parallel to "a picture or entry may only show what the prose has already shown". A Codex, timeline rail or map built from per-chapter records, rather than hand-kept, is the same architecture as Heaven's Vault's knowledge model.
- **A tension worth deciding on purpose.** Failbetter's Mandrake portraits deliberately hide clues in details ("seem like decoration at first… revealed to be clues"). Under a strict "only what the prose has shown" rule, a portrait may not seed future secrets. The safe form of the tarot idea is the reverse: details in a later Book's portrait can **call back** to things the prose has already shown, so re-looking rewards readers without foreshadowing.
- **Expect map dissonance and plan for it.** Arendt's "So wait, the Iron Republic is *there*?" suggests painting places only after the prose has fixed them, and keeping relative scale anchored to something the reader knows (the travellers), with distances compressed.
- **The Wardrobe should talk, not tabulate.** Mask of the Rose's in-voice wardrobe description matches a serial where the reader doesn't study mechanics. A line in the character's voice per look ("how you're likely to come across") is better precedent than a stat list.
- **A spoiler-free "open questions" list.** Overboard!'s Questions menu shows only things "perceived but not fully seen". That is a model for listing a serial's open threads by what the prose has raised, never by what is planned.
- **Text options are part of reading UX.** Failbetter offers font size steps, a non-cursive variant and a sans-serif variant. Any display face used for songs or epigraphs should have a plain fallback.

### Gaps
- The GDC 2018 text-UX talk itself (the video) was not watched, so its specific typographic rules (measure, size, animation timing) are unknown here.
- No inkle or Failbetter source was found stating a per-scene or per-chapter illustration budget, or Fallen London's total icon or illustration count.
- No primary inkle writing on Pendragon's board presentation was found.

## 3. Nielsen Norman Group in full: phone reading, previews, disclosure, images, targets, dark mode

### Takeaway
NN/g's own studies show that **easy linear reading is no worse on a phone**, but hard text costs time there (about 30 ms more per word). Its guidance for a phone reading site is consistent across articles. Use overlays only for short, dismissible extras. Never stack sheets, and always allow Back and a visible Close. Make tap targets at least 1 cm × 1 cm. Collapse long reference pages so readers see their structure first. Show at most five frames in a carousel and never auto-advance on mobile. Use images only when they carry information, because decorative ones are ignored. Give long scrolling lists landmarks. On dark mode, light mode measures better for normal vision, but users expect apps to follow their system setting.

### Cited Findings
- **Phone reading comprehension.** "For linear content like articles, especially easy-to-read content, comprehension on mobile appears to be on-par with larger devices." For hard texts, "readers will probably need to exert more effort… on mobile". Brevity rules "may be relaxed somewhat" when content "Serves an entertainment, time-killing, or informative purpose". Participants "liked the lack of 'distractions' on the mobile device. For sequential reading, like articles, mobile may have the advantage." [Study, n = 276 · full page read · 11 Dec 2016] — [NN/g: Reading Content on Mobile Devices](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)
- **Dark vs light (review).** Piepenbrock et al. (*Ergonomics* 2013): "light mode won across all dimensions" for acuity and proofreading, at all ages. In a follow-up, the light-mode advantage "increased linearly as the font size was decreased". Dobres et al. (MIT AgeLab, 2017): no polarity effect in simulated daylight, but "during nighttime, light mode led to better performance". A 2018 study with **7** participants linked an hour of light-mode reading to choroid thinning (a myopia marker). Recommendation: don't make dark mode the default for a general audience, but "we strongly recommend that designers allow users to switch to dark mode". [Study review · full page read · 2 Feb 2020] — [NN/g: Dark Mode vs. Light Mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)
- **What users do with dark mode.** In a survey of 115 mobile users, "roughly 1/3 said dark mode, 1/3 said light mode, and 1/3 said a combination of both". Users "think about it at the system level, not the application level"; the eye-strain benefit is widely believed but weakly supported. [Study, survey n = 115 + mobile usability sessions · full page read · 27 Aug 2023, modified 11 Jun 2026] — [NN/g: Dark Mode: How Users Think About It](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode-users-issues/)
- **Bottom sheets.** These are "a form of progressive disclosure… they aren't suited for displaying always needed information". Unlike a separate page, which "requires spatial reorientation… and may also force them to hold information in their working memory", a sheet "preserves some of the user's current context". Problems seen in testing: "Lack of a clear way to dismiss", "Stacking several bottom sheets", "Obscuring relevant background content". Rules: support Back; "include a visible a Close (or X) button", because swipe-down is error-prone and inaccessible; "Do Not Stack Bottom Sheets"; "don't use a sheet to display an ecommerce product-detail page"; use sheets "Only for Short Interactions". On reachability, "the middle of the screen represents the most easily tappable area". [Study/practitioner · full page read · 11 Jun 2023] — [NN/g: Bottom Sheets](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/bottom-sheet/)
- **Touch targets.** "Interactive elements must be at least 1cm × 1cm (0.4in × 0.4in)", based on Parhi, Karlson & Bederson (MobileHCI 2006). Fingertips are 1.6–2 cm wide and a thumb's contact area is about 2.5 cm. Crowding causes slips. "Maps on touchscreens often cause touch-target errors… users immediately make a 'focus face'", and a list view should ideally be the default. Carousel dots are an example of "view–tap asymmetry". [Study-based guideline · full page read · 5 May 2019] — [NN/g: Touch Targets on Touchscreens](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)
- **Progressive disclosure.** "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options"; "the very fact that something appears on the initial display tells users that it's important"; "label the button or link in a way that sets clear expectations". "Hypertext provides a simple implementation of progressive disclosure." [Practitioner · full page read · 3 Dec 2006, modified 15 Jul 2022] — [NN/g: Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- **Tooltips (relevant to tap-previews).** "Don't use tooltips for information that is vital to task completion"; avoid "obvious or redundant text"; hover-only tooltips are "inaccessible for users that rely on keyboards"; be consistent, because erratic tooltips go undiscovered. [Practitioner · full page read · 27 Jan 2019] — [NN/g: Tooltip Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tooltip-guidelines/)
- **Accordions on mobile.** They let users "get the big picture before focusing on details" and act like a table of contents. Expanding the first section by default hid the page's structure. Jumping an opened section to the top made users think "they navigated to a new page". [Study-based guideline · full page read · 31 May 2015] — [NN/g: Accordions on Mobile](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-accordions/)
- **Carousels.** "Include 5 or fewer frames… it's difficult for users to recognize topics they have already viewed when a set exceeds about five." "Do not auto-forward on mobile devices." For auto-forwarding elsewhere, allow "3 words per second". Put important carousel content elsewhere too. [Study-based guideline · full page read · 14 Sep 2013, last reviewed 7 Aug 2026] — [NN/g: Carousel Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)
- **Which images people look at (eyetracking).** "Users pay close attention to photos and other images that contain relevant information but ignore fluffy pictures used to 'jazz up' web pages." On a team page, a user spent 10% more time on portraits than on bios that took 316% more space. When users ask for an enlargement it "should be at least twice as big". [Study, eyetracking · full page read · 31 Oct 2010, last reviewed 13 Aug 2026] — [NN/g: Photos as Web Content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)
- **Big images vs. the task.** "Devoting this much emphasis to any single design element necessarily means that users are less likely to notice other elements." A "balanced approach" is recommended. [Practitioner · full page read · 28 Sep 2014] — [NN/g: Image-Focused Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/image-focused-design/)
- **Scroll attention.** Data: 130,000+ fixations from 120 participants. "57% of their page-viewing time above the fold"; "74%… in the first two screenfuls"; people "rarely go beyond the third screenful". "People will scroll if they have a reason to do it." [Study, eyetracking · full page read · 15 Apr 2018] — [NN/g: Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **Infinite scroll and landmarks.** Drawbacks include "Difficulty refinding content", the "Illusion of completeness" and "Inability to access the end of the page". A Back press often returns users "at the top of the list". Pages "serve as valuable landmarks for orientation". [Practitioner · full page read · 4 Sep 2022] — [NN/g: Infinite Scrolling](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/infinite-scrolling-tips/)
- **Legibility.** "Use a reasonably large default font size and allow users to change the font size"; prefer a plain background; "strangely shaped fonts (e.g., emulating handwriting or gothic style) have reduced legibility". [Practitioner · full page read · 15 Nov 2015] — [NN/g: Legibility, Readability, and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/legibility-readability-comprehension/)
- **Hover timing (desktop only).** Hidden content needs "distinct timing to avoid accidental activations". The best cue of intent is that the pointer "actually stops on the element", and there should be a slight delay before hiding on mouse-out. [Practitioner · full page read · 11 Jan 2015] — [NN/g: Timing Guidelines for Exposing Hidden Content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/timing-exposing-content/)

### Inferences
- **Name previews on a phone:** a nonmodal or short modal bottom sheet holding one or two sentences and a thumbnail, with a visible Close, Back to dismiss, and no link inside it that opens a second sheet. "Open the page" should navigate properly, not stack. This follows the bottom-sheet and tooltip guidance directly.
- **Codex and character pages:** collapsed sections with clear labels, so the structure shows on the first screen (accordions). Don't expand the first section by default, and don't jump-scroll on expand.
- **Per-Book portraits:** a swipeable set works if it stays at five or fewer, has visible labels rather than tiny dots (view–tap asymmetry), and never auto-advances.
- **Images must carry information.** The eyetracking evidence (decorative images ignored, information-bearing ones scrutinized) supports images that show something the prose established: a face, a place, a route. Mood fillers would be ignored. Tap-to-enlarge should at least double the size.
- **Light default, system-following dark.** NN/g's two dark-mode articles together support following `prefers-color-scheme` (users think at the system level), with a manual switch.
- **Chapter rail and long lists need landmarks** (Book and chapter headings) and must restore the reader's place on Back.
- **Hard text costs more on a phone.** Recaps, Codex entries and sheet lines should be written short and plain. Story prose is "entertainment" content, where NN/g relaxes the brevity rule.

### Gaps
- No NN/g article dedicated to **line length** was read in this pass. Butterick's 45–90 characters stays the working reference (summary only from the earlier pass).
- No NN/g article on **reading-progress indicators** for long-form reading was found (see §5).
- NN/g has published no fiction-specific reading study.

## 4. Research on illustrated and multimodal fiction, maps, recaps and spoilers

### Takeaway
The controlled evidence is thin for adult fiction and stronger for children's story apps. The strongest finding is in children: **congruent** multimedia (pictures, music and sound that match the text) helps story comprehension about as much as an adult reading along. Interactive extras such as games and hotspots distract. Qualitative work with older children suggests illustrations can either scaffold or override a reader's own mental pictures. Map research is about how readers build spatial models, not about whether book maps help. Spoiler studies conflict, and the stronger replications find spoilers reduce enjoyment. Recap research is new: retrieving the right earlier passage is treated as a hard task even for language models.

### Cited Findings
- **Multimedia stories ≈ an adult reading along (children).** Meta-analysis: 29 studies, 1,272 children. Multimedia stories beat traditional stories read without an adult for comprehension (g+ = 0.40, k = 18) and vocabulary (g+ = 0.30, k = 11). There was "no significant differences… [versus] sharing traditional print-like stories with an adult". The mechanism is congruence: nonverbal elements help "if… congruent with the story text" (dual coding). In one included study, "when children had the option to play with the games, they hardly spent time listening to the story." [Study, meta-analysis · full page read · Frontiers in Psychology, Dec 2014] — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01366/full)
- **Features that help vs hurt (children).** A follow-up meta-analysis: 43 studies, 2,147 children. There is a small extra benefit of technology for comprehension (g+ = 0.17) and expressive vocabulary (g+ = 0.20). Animated pictures, music and sound effects helped. Interactive features (hotspots, games, dictionaries) were distracting, and for disadvantaged children, multimedia helped while interactive features hurt. [Study, meta-analysis · summary only (PMC returned a captcha) · Review of Educational Research 85(4), 2015] — [PMC4647204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204); [OpenDevEd listing](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/NDJ8IRSS)
- **Illustrated novels and mental pictures (qualitative).** Jen Aggleton's participatory multiple-case study: five readers aged 9–10, three illustrated novels. It argues that illustration and mental picturing "interact in more complicated ways" than theory assumed, and that illustrations "need not undermine mental picturing". In her earlier study (six readers aged 11–14, illustrated vs non-illustrated *A Monster Calls*), some readers felt illustrations gave more detail than their own images and changed meaning, while others felt scaffolded. [Study, qualitative, n = 5 and n = 6 · summary only · Cambridge Journal of Education 53(1), 2022; Children's Literature in Education, 2016–17] — [Open University repository](https://oro.open.ac.uk/83783); [Springer: earlier study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10583-016-9279-1)
- **Pictures and imagery skill.** In a children's study, readers with low mental-imagery skill may benefit from pictures, which help them form a mental model. [Study · summary only · c. 2016] — [PMC5075566](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5075566/)
- **Seductive details.** Irrelevant but interesting images reduce learning, and the effect has reportedly been replicated with narrative as well as science texts, though some non-science studies did not find it. Adults with lower working memory looked longer at seductive illustrations (Jaeger & Wiley, 2015). A 2023 study found positive effects for decorative pictures that are related to the text but don't explain it. [Study · summary only · various] — [Wikipedia: Seductive details](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seductive_details); [Instructional Science 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11251-023-09618-8.pdf)
- **AI-generated supplementary images (adults, small n).** GenAIReading, 24 participants: AI-generated text summaries, images and image summaries "significantly improved learning outcomes, increasing post-reading test scores by 7.50%". The texts were educational, though the abstract mentions "narratives and academic textbooks". [Study, n = 24 · full page read (abstract) · arXiv, 10 Mar 2025] — [arXiv 2503.07463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07463)
- **AI graphic-novel version of a story.** In a study with ESL readers (76–80 participants), the graphic-novel version made no significant difference to comprehension, enjoyment or engagement, and the control group had higher *emotional* engagement. [Study, master's-level · summary only · TU Delft / Utrecht, 2025] — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:2ef58fcd-4119-4584-b42f-bb7f4ce56c69); [Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/48521)
- **Children notice image–text emotional mismatch.** In a qualitative study (13 parent–child groups, ages 4–8), children noticed when AI images clashed with the text's emotional tone. [Study, qualitative · summary only · NC State news, Nov 2025] — [NC State](https://news.ncsu.edu/2025/11/parents-kids-ai-images/)
- **Maps and spatial situation models.** In the Morrow, Greenspan & Bower (1987) paradigm, readers memorise a map of the setting before reading. Spatial information then enters their situation model, and spatial shifts slow reading only for readers who memorised the map or were told to attend to space (Zwaan and others). Readers otherwise under-track space. [Study, lab paradigm · summary only · 1987 onward; Memory & Cognition 2016] — [Springer: Memory & Cognition s13421-016-0635-6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-016-0635-6); [Nyhout & O'Neill 2017 (PDF)](https://uwaterloo.ca/childrens-communication-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/2017_-_angela_nyhout_-_childrensenactmentofcharactersmovements.pdf)
- **Recaps as retrieval (NLP).** "Similar to the 'previously-on' scenes in TV shows, recaps can help book reading by recalling the readers' memory about the important elements in previous texts." The authors propose "Recap Snippet Identification", a benchmark for retrieving earlier passages, and report it "is challenging to PLMs, LLMs" because it "requires a deep understanding of the plot correlation between snippets." [Study, benchmark · full page read (abstract) · arXiv 2402.07271, Feb 2024] — [Li et al.: Previously on the Stories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07271)
- **What players remember.** "Narrative event salience" is defined as the ease of recalling a past event. It was used to predict, and then try to influence, which endings players chose. [Study · summary only · 2020] — [Farrell & Ware (PDF)](https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/farrell2020manipulating.pdf)
- **Spoilers: conflicting results.** Leavitt & Christenfeld (*Psychological Science*, 2011) found spoiled stories slightly *more* enjoyed. Later replications (Levine et al., *Discourse Processes*, 2016; Johnson & Rosenbaum, *Communication Research*, 2015) found spoilers *reduced* enjoyment, possibly depending on the spoiler's form. One replication (N = 412) found no overall effect, but lower suspense and "movingness". Another found negative effects for television under some conditions. [Study · summary only · 2011–2018] — [University of Arkansas news](https://news.uark.edu/articles/38085/fulbright-college-research-team-explores-how-spoilers-affect-reading-enjoyment); [OnFiction research bulletin](https://www.onfiction.ca/2011/08/research-bulletin-do-spoilers-spoil.html?m=1); [PubMed 30115003](https://www.pubmed.gov/30115003/)
- **Earlier-pass audio studies (not re-read).** Vasilev et al. 2018 (lyrics distract more than instrumental music), Rodero 2012 and Rodero & Romero 2022 (sound cues raise imagery in audio drama), and Booktrack (industry-funded) stand as summarised there. PMC and Taylor & Francis could not be opened to re-verify them. [Study · summary only] — see the earlier note §8.

### Inferences
- **Congruence, not quantity, is what the evidence rewards.** The children's meta-analyses favour pictures, music and sound that *match the text*, and penalise interactive distractions. For the serial, that supports images and songs tied to what the page says, placed beside it, and argues against games or hotspots inside the reading column.
- **Illustration can overwrite the reader's own picture.** Aggleton's readers, the Delft finding of lower emotional engagement with a graphic-novel version, and Failbetter's map "dissonance" all point the same way. The safest order is **prose first, picture after**: the image appears once the text has fixed the look, not before.
- **Maps don't automatically help readers follow space.** Readers mostly don't track space unless prompted. A map is a reference and recap tool, not a comprehension guarantee, so tying it to the chapter (tap a place, see where the chapter is) is what makes it useful.
- **Spoiler caution is justified.** The better-powered replications find spoilers reduce enjoyment or suspense, so a reader-position filter (show only what is reached) is supported, not just polite.
- **"Previously on" is best built from pointers to real passages.** The NLP framing (retrieve the earlier snippet) matches a recap that links back to the exact earlier scene, rather than paraphrasing.

### Gaps
- **No controlled study of adult readers of illustrated prose fiction** was found: no experiment comparing comprehension or transportation with and without illustrations.
- **No study of LitRPG or stat screens and reader memory** was found in ACM, ICIDS, FDG or arXiv searches.
- **No controlled study of maps in published fantasy fiction** and comprehension was found.
- PMC, Taylor & Francis and Springer full texts could not be read (captcha, 403 and JavaScript challenge). Effect sizes above from those hosts are as the search summaries gave them.

## 5. Closing the earlier pass's gaps: progress indicators, autoplay, designer statements on audio, the Wandering Inn's art

### Takeaway
- **Progress indicators:** no controlled evidence was found for fiction or long-form reading. Only vendor claims exist. NN/g's landmark and refinding guidance is the nearest solid ground.
- **Autoplay:** browsers block sound without a tap (Chrome since version 66 for media elements and 71 for Web Audio; Safari on iOS longer). Tap-to-play songs are what browsers allow anyway.
- **Designer statements on music:** they come from composers and studio heads, not from IF ambient-audio theory. They value long themes, motifs that recur across a game, and music matched to the art.
- **The Wandering Inn:** in-chapter art on the official site was not confirmed. The fan wiki does attach one image per chapter page from fan art ("No two chapters should have the same image") and files fan art by chapter number. The official webcomic is a separate illustrated retelling.

### Cited Findings
- **Progress indicators: no study found.** Searches turned up only vendor and plugin pages claiming readers finish more often with a progress bar, with no named study. The nearest research (Grinberg, The Web Conference) classifies reading modes and does not test progress bars. [Community/vendor · summary only · undated] — [Nieman Lab on reading modes](https://niemanlab.org/2018/04/the-five-ways-we-read-online-and-what-publishers-can-do-to-encourage-the-good-ones); [Codecanel (unsourced claim)](https://codecanel.com/?p=8169)

"When changes happen without breaking the rhythmic pulse, the result is very satisfying." [Designer/composer · full page read · 15 Jul 2014] — [Failbetter: Dynamic Combat Music in Sunless Sea](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/pitching-potential-dynamic-combat-music-in-sunless-sea)

[Designer · full page read · Game Developer Q&A, 2023] — [Game Developer: Developing to 120 beats-per-minute](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/programming-to-120-beats-per-minute-a-highland-song-q-a)

[Designer · full page read · 14 Oct 2015] — [80 Days postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-inkle-s-i-80-days-i-)

[Community/Docs · full page read · current, 17 Nov 2026] — [TWI Wiki: Chapter Images](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/The_Wandering_Inn_Wiki:Chapter_Images); [New Chapter Guide](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Help:Policies/New_Chapter_Guide)

### Inferences
- **Progress:** without evidence for progress bars, the defensible design is **landmarks and memory**: the chapter's place in its Book on the rail, "Chapter N of the Book", and restoring scroll position. A thin bar is harmless but has no evidence behind it.
- **Audio:** since browsers require a tap anyway, the song player beside the sung lines should start in the tap handler, catch a rejected `play()`, and never pre-start a Web Audio context. That matches the reading-distraction evidence (tap, listen, then read on).

- **Wandering Inn:** what readers maintain for themselves is one image per chapter page (never repeated) and fan art keyed to the chapter where the moment happens. A serial that keys its own images to the chapter where the prose shows the moment is doing officially what this fan wiki does by hand.

## 6. Spoiler-safe reference in practice: The Wandering Inn wiki's policies read in full, and the designer patterns that do better

### Takeaway
The Wandering Inn wiki, the biggest reference for an ongoing web serial, admits it cannot protect readers. "Spoilers on the wiki are unavoidable." Its only two standing measures are a collapsible "Show Spoiler" around each character's alive or dead status and a general warning on the landing page. It also bans Patreon-only knowledge until chapters are public. Its strengths are chapter-level structure: a tl;dr of three to five sentences per chapter, transcluded into each volume's recap table; first-appearance markers; and "Statistics Revealed/Obtained" sections. The designer patterns in §2 go further because they filter by what the player knows. The reference layer is built from those per-chapter records.

### Cited Findings
- **Spoiler policy, verbatim.** "Spoilers on the wiki are unavoidable and readers are cautioned to beware spoilers at their own discretion… we cannot guarantee a reader will not be spoiled". The standards are "Hiding the status of a character at the top of their page within their information box, such as *Alive* or *Deceased*" and "Placing a general spoiler warning at the wiki's main landing page." [Docs/Community · full page read · current, 17 Nov 2026] — [TWI Wiki: Help:Policies](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Help:Policies)
- **How the status is hidden.** `{{status}}` wraps the value in a collapsed block with "Show Spoiler"/"Hide Spoiler" labels. The template's documentation calls it "Hide status spoilers". [Docs · full page read · current] — [Template:Status](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Template:Status)
- **Landing-page warning and scale.** "Due to the major size of the story, this wiki contains **MAJOR SPOILERS and GAPS**." The serial is "on its tenth volume… over 17 million words". [Docs · full page read · current] — [Template:New Welcome](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Template:New_Welcome)
- **Early-access rule.** "Knowledge and information from chapters locked behind pirateaba's Patreon subscription are not to be referenced until they are publicly posted"; "Contributors must wait for Patreon chapters to become public before proceeding." [Docs · full page read · current] — [Help:Policies](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Help:Policies); [New Chapter Guide](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Help:Policies/New_Chapter_Guide)
- **Chapter recap structure (draft guideline).** A synopsis is "at least one paragraph long and at most one page". A tl;dr is "at most 3 to 5 short sentences… tl;dr's are important to create arc overviews later on". The chapter page lists Characters with "( * = First Appearance)", Groups, Locations, Creatures, Items, and "Statistics Revealed / Statistics Obtained". The page is still marked "This is a Draft". [Docs · full page read · current] — [New Chapter Guide](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Help:Policies/New_Chapter_Guide)
- **One home per recap.** Each volume's tl;dr table transcludes each chapter's own tl;dr section ("To edit a specific chapter's tl;dr, go to the chapter page"). [Docs · full page read · current] — [TWI Wiki: Volume 6/tl;dr](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Volume_6/tl;dr)
- **Tense and neutrality.** Articles use "Historical present tense", even for dead characters. "Theories and speculation are not to be included." "Avoid over-linking. Link to a page once per section, at most." [Docs · full page read · current] — [Style Guide](https://wiki.wanderinginn.com/Help:Policies/Style_Guide)
- **Spoiler labelling by developers.** Failbetter titled a 2013 map post "Mapping the Sunless Sea (geography spoilers!)", so map reveals were flagged as spoilers in the title itself. [Designer · full page read · 19 Nov 2013] — [Failbetter](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/mapping-the-sunless-sea-geography-spoilers)
- **Filtering by what the player knows (designer pattern).** Heaven's Vault timeline entries are "tagged with the states in the knowledge model that unlock them"; the Unterzee map "will fill in slowly as they learn about each location"; Overboard! lists only questions about things "perceived but not fully seen". [Designer · full page read · 2018, 2020, 2022] — [Heaven's Vault](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/ideas-and-dynamic-conversation-in-heaven-s-vault); [Unterzee map](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/map-for-the-unterzee); [Overboard!](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-get-away-murder-overboard)
- **Other wikis (earlier pass, not re-read).** Coppermind's timed spoiler tags and "Time Machine", AWOIAF's "read at your own risk", and Kindle's Story So Far (June 2026) stand as summarised in the earlier note. Their hosts were not allowed this session. [Docs/Press · summary only] — see the earlier note §4.

### Inferences
- **A fan wiki cannot do what a single-author site can.** TWI's wiki, official in name and run by volunteers, only warns and hides one field. A site generated from per-chapter records can show every page **as of the reader's chapter**. That is the knowledge-state approach of Heaven's Vault and the Unterzee map, applied to a Codex, a cast page and a map.
- **Copy the wiki's structure, not its spoiler model.** Worth adopting: a 3–5 sentence tl;dr per chapter as the single source that "Previously" and Book recaps are drawn from; first-appearance markers on cast lists; "revealed vs obtained" for the character sheet; historical present tense; no speculation; link once per section.
- **Status fields are the most spoiler-prone line on a cast page.** Even this spoiler-tolerant wiki hides them. On a reader-position site, a status should appear only once the prose has stated it, and never as a dead/alive field on its own.

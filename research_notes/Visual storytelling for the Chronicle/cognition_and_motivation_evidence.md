# Cognition and motivation evidence: pictures, maps, avatars and progress visuals in a text-first serial

*Grading key used on every finding: **[META]** meta-analysis · **[CTRL n=…]** controlled/randomised experiment (n where known) · **[REVIEW]** systematic/narrative/scoping review · **[THEORY]** theory or humanities argument · **[OPINION]** essay, press piece, practitioner claim. **(abs)** = I read only the abstract, a press release or a secondary summary, not the full paper. Access note: page fetches to most academic hosts (Stanford, NCBI/PMC, Taylor & Francis, e-teaching.org) were blocked in this session's network, so almost every entry below rests on abstracts, publisher records, press releases and secondary summaries surfaced by search; exact effect sizes the snippets did not show are listed under Gaps rather than filled in from memory. Most multimedia-learning evidence comes from expository/instructional material and learning tests, not fiction enjoyment; carrying it over to a fantasy serial is an inference, flagged as such.*

## 1. Pictures and narrative comprehension and memory (multimedia principles, dual coding, picture superiority, seductive details, illustrated stories for adults)

### Takeaway
Pictures that *carry* text content (represent, organise, interpret or make it memorable) reliably help memory and understanding; pictures that only *decorate*, or that are interesting but irrelevant ("seductive details"), do not help and on average hurt learning a little, most when they are static, sit beside relevant material, and the reader is a novice. Placing a picture next to the words it belongs to and signalling what matters are among the best-supported design rules. For adult fiction readers specifically, direct comprehension evidence is thin: one adult experiment found pictures raised ease of imagining and engagement; another found no comprehension effect but a preference for pictures.

### Cited Findings
- **Seductive details hurt learning, on average modestly.** Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32(3), 707–734: 58 studies, >7,500 students; learners given interesting-but-irrelevant material scored lower on learning outcomes than those without it. [META] (abs) — [WSU press release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200319103223.htm)
  - Moderators reported: the harm was larger when the seductive detail was placed next to informative, relevant diagrams, and when it was *constant/static* (e.g., a still joke or image on screen); larger on paper than in digital formats; larger in short lessons (<5 min) than longer ones (>10 min, n.s.). Most included studies used 6–12-minute sessions. [META] (abs) — [WSU press release](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [Learning and the Brain blog (secondary)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/)
  - The authors stress the analysis measured *cognitive outcomes only*; emotional/interest benefits were not examined. [META] (abs) — [WSU press release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning)
  - A separate, newer meta-analysis presented at AERA 2026 (50 studies, 177 effect sizes) reports a small overall seductive-details effect of g = −0.16. [META, conference abstract] (abs) — [AERA 2026 paper summary](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/)
- **Mechanism (Harp & Mayer 1998, *J. Educ. Psych.* 90(3)):** seductive details are proposed to prime an inappropriate base of prior knowledge around which the learner organises the material; when the details were moved to the end of the lesson, performance matched the no-detail group. [CTRL] (abs, via encyclopedia summary) — [Wikipedia: Seductive details](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seductive_details)
- **Coherence principle:** Mayer's coherence-principle chapter reports a median effect size of d = 0.97 in favour of concise lessons stripped of extraneous words, pictures and sounds; the test count differs between secondary sources. The principle is said to matter most for learners with low working memory or low domain knowledge. [REVIEW of controlled studies] (abs) — [Cambridge: Multimedia Learning, Coherence principle](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/coherence-principle/4E80B70CB76E2166B76E5653EBDE7D3E); [Growth Engineering blog (secondary)](https://www.growthengineering.co.uk/?p=82511)
- **Spatial contiguity (words next to the picture they describe):** Schroeder & Cenkci (2018), *Educ. Psych. Review*: 58 comparisons, n = 2,426, overall g = 0.63, holding for static and dynamic formats. Earlier, Ginns (2006) reported d = 0.85 (95% CI 0.68–1.02) for split-attention/contiguity. [META] (abs/secondary) — [TUM Clearinghouse summary of Schroeder & Cenkci](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf); [Frontiers in Education 2019 (cites Ginns)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2019.00086/pdf)
- **Signalling/cueing (highlighting what matters):** Schneider et al. (2018), *Educational Research Review*: ~145 studies, ~12,201 participants; retention g = 0.53, transfer g = 0.33 (includes cueing agents, so broader than "signalling"). [META] (secondary slide summary only) — [Charles University lecture slides](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf)
- **Redundancy:** Adesope & Nesbit (2012), *J. Educ. Psych.* 104(1): 48 effect sizes from 32 reports; spoken+written did not differ from written-only, but beat spoken-only; *condensed* on-screen key words added to narration were associated with better learning. [META] (abs) — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842); [Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013 (secondary)](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/YueBjorkBjork2013_redundancy.pdf)
- **Functions of pictures in prose:** Levin, Anglin & Carney (1987) classified text illustrations as decorative, representational, organisational, interpretational and transformational; illustrations *other than decorative ones* were consistently associated with greater text learning. Carney & Levin (2002), *Educ. Psych. Review* 14(1): "carefully constructed text illustrations generally enhance learners' performance" on text-dependent outcomes (evidence mostly from the 1970s–80s, largely school-age learners). [META + REVIEW] (abs/secondary) — [Carney & Levin 2002 DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1023/A:1013176309260); [TIE Online summary (secondary)](https://www.tieonline.com/article/2084/can-decorative-graphics-improve-learning-from-text-); [e-teaching.org record of Levin et al. 1987](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/levin_etal1987)
- **Picture superiority / dual coding:** Standing (1973), "Learning 10,000 pictures": recognition memory for pictures is "almost limitless" under appropriate conditions (98% two-alternative recognition after 612 pictures; ~83% after 10,000; vivid pictures remembered even better). [CTRL] (abs) — [Standing 1973 PDF](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/psych/rust-lab/publications/standing%5F73.pdf); [Konkle et al. 2010 (secondary)](https://bradylab.ucsd.edu/stimuli/all/pdfs/KonkleBradyAlvarezOliva2010.pdf)
  - Paivio's dual-coding theory (pictures get a visual and a verbal code) is the most-cited explanation [THEORY]; it is contested: Stenberg, Radeborg & Hedman (1995, *Memory & Cognition*) argue the data do not support dual encoding as the cause, and the effect has limits and can reverse. [CTRL] (abs) — [CMU memory lab chapter](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/memorylab/publications/10.1.1.646.3474.pdf); [Springer, Memory & Cognition PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03197244.pdf)
- **Adult illustrated-story evidence (direct):**
  - Cohen et al. (2018/2020), *Psychology of Popular Media Culture* 9(2): 212 adult MTurk participants read the same graphic-novel story with or without images. Images increased *imagery fluency* (ease of picturing), which increased narrative engagement and reduced counterarguing; the imagery-fluency path was the largest indirect effect. [CTRL n=212] (abs) — [RTI publication page](https://www.rti.org/publication/seeing-believing)
  - An Easy Language study of 52 adults (with and without intellectual disability) found visual support had no effect on comprehension, though neurotypical readers preferred illustrated versions. [CTRL n=52] (abs, via search summary) — [Cambridge Core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/3100975690CF0E5579299B5CCFA11716/core-reader)
- **Child-only (flag):** Carnegie Mellon (Fisher lab, 2020): first and second graders' gaze was pulled away from text by extraneous illustrations, lowering comprehension; streamlined pages helped. [CTRL, children] (press) — [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2020/september/busy-pictures-hinder-reading.html)

### Inferences
- The defensible rule for the site: an image should **carry something the prose has already put on the page** (a place, a face, a technique as described), not add new, unexplained, attention-grabbing content. Decorative art is not shown to help comprehension; "seductive" art beside important text is the configuration with the strongest harm signal.
- The serial is not a lesson, and seductive-detail studies measure learning, not pleasure; so decoration is a *low-benefit, small-risk* choice for a story page, not a proven harm. The larger risk is placing a striking static image *beside the text at the moment of a key plot beat*, competing for attention.
- Contiguity and signalling support captions and labels *on* the image (a card's name and rank on its frame, a place name on the map) rather than in a separate legend or a separate page.
- Picture superiority suggests portraits, technique cards and place art will make characters and places easier to recognise and recall across a long serial (a memory aid), which matters more as the cast grows. This is inference from list-memory research, not from fiction studies.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect size for Sundararajan & Adesope (2020) could not be verified from accessible text (full paper: DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4); sources conflict on study count (58 vs 68).
- Levin et al. (1987) effect sizes per picture function were not found in accessible sources.
- No controlled study found on illustrated vs unillustrated *long-form adult fiction* measuring comprehension or recall over weeks; nothing on serialised fiction read on phones.
- No study found on whether readers who wrote the text themselves (author-as-reader) respond to illustrations like ordinary readers.

## 2. Situation models: do maps, timelines and relationship diagrams help readers build them?

### Takeaway
Readers build situation models indexed on space, time, causation, protagonist/entities and goals; spatial tracking is real but is driven by following the protagonist. Studying a map before or alongside text improves memory for map-related text facts in classic studies (conjoint retention), though whether the *spatial layout* or simply the recognisable *icons* do the work is contested. There is essentially no controlled research on fantasy maps, timelines or relationship diagrams for fiction readers; the case for them rests on situation-model theory, map-text studies with expository texts, and humanities argument.

### Cited Findings
- **Event-indexing model (Zwaan & Radvansky 1998, *Psych. Bulletin*; Zwaan, Langston & Graesser 1995):** each incoming clause forms an event representation integrated with those in working memory according to overlap on (at least) five dimensions (time, space, entity/protagonist, causation, motivation/goals); a discontinuity on any dimension triggers model updating; supported in several empirical studies. Dimension labels vary across sources. [THEORY + REVIEW of controlled studies] (secondary) — [Erasmus repository paper summarising the model](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/120435/Repub_120435_O-A.pdf); [K-State memory lab paper](https://www.k-state.edu/memory/publications/pdfs/s13421-017-0707-2.pdf)
- **Morrow, Greenspan & Bower (1987) and follow-ups:** participants memorise a building map, then read a story of a character moving through it; accessibility of objects is best in the protagonist's current room and falls with distance (spatial gradient). The gradient appears only when readers track the protagonist (it vanishes when the protagonist's name is absent from probes) and is stronger for continuous, predictable movement; Rinck et al. (1996) generalised it across three experiments; a 2013 *Memory & Cognition* study extended it to interactive virtual environments. [CTRL, multiple] (abs/secondary) — [Memory & Cognition 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0329-2)
- **Conjoint retention hypothesis (Kulhavy, Lee & Caterino 1985):** students recall more text information when they study a map plus text than text alone, because maps are encoded spatially. Schreiber, Verdi et al. (2002): intact map + well-organised ("considerate") text produced significantly more recall than intact map + randomised text. [CTRL] (abs) — [Arizona Regents experts record](https://experts.azregents.edu/en/publications/differing-map-construction-and-text-organization-and-their-effect/)
  - **Contradicting:** Griffin & Robinson (2000) found no advantage for maps over feature lists; their 2005 follow-up argues the *mimetic icons* (recognisable pictures of features), not spatial layout, drive the recall benefit, supporting dual coding over conjoint retention. [CTRL] (abs) — [Georgia Southern record, Griffin & Robinson](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/does-spatial-or-visual-information-in-maps-facilitate-text-recall/); [Typeset summary](https://typeset.io/papers/does-spatial-or-visual-information-in-maps-facilitate-text-46co8i0jix)
- **Fantasy maps (humanities):** Stefan Ekman, *Here Be Dragons: Exploring Fantasy Maps and Settings* (Wesleyan UP, 2013; Mythopoeic Scholarship Award 2016) surveyed 200 fantasy novels/collections and found maps "not the compulsory ingredients they are widely held to be"; argues the map is a tool for the reader (and critic) as well as the writer; describes the default style as "a pseudomedieval aesthetic". [THEORY] (reviews/publisher) — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/22721); [Strange Horizons review](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/non-fiction/reviews/here-be-dragons-exploring-fantasy-maps-and-settings-by-stefan-ekman/)
  - A Tor.com essay argues the fantasy map's job is to orient the *reader* in the secondary world. [OPINION] — [Tor.com](https://tor.com/2019/05/28/fantasy-maps-dont-belong-in-the-hands-of-fantasy-characters)
- **Tversky (1991), spatial mental models:** foundational theory paper on mental models built from spatial descriptions (listed; not read). [THEORY] — [Tversky 1991 PDF](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/1991_Tversky_spatialmentalmodels.pdf)

### Inferences
- A map that shows **where the protagonist is now and the route already travelled** matches the strongest situation-model finding (readers' spatial models are protagonist-centred and updated by movement). A map showing everything at once (unvisited regions, future destinations) adds load without that support and risks revealing what the page has not said.
- Griffin & Robinson's result suggests map *icons* that look like the places as described (a tower, a ford) may carry more memory benefit than precise geography; a stylised, icon-led map is not a compromise on the evidence.
- Timelines and relationship diagrams map onto the time, causation and protagonist/entity dimensions, so they are theoretically plausible aids for a long serial with a growing cast, but this is untested; their value likely grows with gaps between reading sessions (the serial is read in short phone sessions over months).
- Because a map is a reader-orientation tool rather than in-world truth (Ekman, Tor essay), it can be revealed progressively without breaking fiction.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on fantasy-map use by fiction readers, on progressively revealed maps, or on route traces.
- No controlled study found on timelines or character-relationship diagrams improving comprehension or memory of *fiction*.
- Map-text studies use short expository texts and immediate recall tests; long-delay effects across a serial are unknown.

## 3. Reader imagination: does showing a picture constrain or displace mental imagery? Aphantasia and weak imagers

### Takeaway
There is no good adult experimental evidence that illustrations *reduce* readers' imagery; the one adult experiment found images *increased* imagery fluency and engagement. The "pictures crowd out imagination" claim comes mainly from child-focused and humanities sources. Imagery does matter for absorption: people with aphantasia report less absorption and emotional involvement but equal enjoyment, and lean towards visual media. Once a definitive image of a character exists (e.g., from a film), readers report it is hard to un-see, but this is mostly anecdote.

### Cited Findings
- **Adult experiment, images help imagery:** Cohen et al. (n = 212 adults): illustrations increased imagery fluency → narrative engagement → less counterarguing. [CTRL n=212] (abs) — [RTI](https://www.rti.org/publication/seeing-believing)
- **Aphantasia and story reading:** Speed, Eekhof & Mak (2024), *Consciousness and Cognition* 118, 103645: 47 aphantasic readers vs 51 matched controls read a short story. Aphantasic readers reported **less absorption in the story world and less emotional involvement with characters, but similar enjoyment**; similar reading volume and genre preferences; more fiction consumed via TV, film and games; they appreciated plot and language but not scenery description. Limitation: recruited from aphantasia communities. [CTRL, quasi-experimental n=98] (abs/press) — [Radboud University news](https://www.ru.nl/en/research/research-news/lack-of-visual-imagery-does-not-lead-to-less-pleasure-in-reading); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-aphantasia-research-sheds-light-on-the-role-of-visual-imagery-when-reading-stories/); [Tilburg record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/the-role-of-visual-imagery-in-story-reading-evidence-from-aphanta/)
- **Order of exposure and imagery propensity:** Green et al. (2008), "Transportation across media: repeated exposure to print and film", *Media Psychology* 11(4): reading the novel before the film produced the greatest absorption in the film; **imagery propensity predicted transportation for readers (high imagers more transported) but not for film viewers**. [CTRL] (abs via search snippet) — [Taylor & Francis abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213260802492000)
- **Film images "solidify or compete":** drawing on Barker's *Lord of the Rings* audience research, a 2010 paper describes two outcomes: a film may solidify a reader's visualisation by reinforcing similar images, or force the reader to choose between their own image and the film's. [OPINION/case study] — [Dalhousie Journal of Interdisciplinary Management](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/djim/article/view/2010vol6Martin/53)
- **Children, qualitative (flag):** Aggleton (2022): five 9–10-year-olds reading illustrated novels; notes the small prior literature on illustrations and readers' imagery is largely negative in tone, but finds the relationship more complex and not simply harmful. [qualitative study, n=5, children only] (abs) — [Open Research Online](https://oro.open.ac.uk/83783)
- **Humanities theory:**
  - Elaine Scarry, *Dreaming by the Book* (1999): writers use identifiable techniques to instruct readers in "mental composition" (five principal practices); critics fault its clarity and empirical grounding, and note individual variation in imagery vividness challenges its claims. [THEORY] — [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/node/24542); [Harvard Crimson review](https://thecrimson.com/article/1999/12/3/radiant-ignition-scarry-puts-the-psychology)
  - Peter Mendelsund, *What We See When We Read* (2014): many famous characters are barely described physically yet readers picture them in their own way; argues readers' "pictures" are far vaguer than they believe, and more detail doesn't make a more vivid image. Mixed critical reception (NYT B−, WaPo D per aggregator). [THEORY/OPINION] — [Columbia Magazine review](https://magazine.columbia.edu/article/book-review-cover-and-what-we-see-when-we-read); [Complete Review](https://complete-review.com/reviews/reading/mendelsundp.htm)

### Inferences
- The evidence does not support a blanket "no pictures, protect imagination" rule for adults. It supports: **text first, picture after**. Green et al.'s ordering result and the film-casting anecdotes suggest letting the prose establish a place or person before the image appears, so the image confirms rather than pre-empts.
- If the reader is a weak imager (unknown), images may *add* absorption the prose alone gives less of (Speed et al.: low-imagery readers absorb less; Cohen: images raise imagery fluency). If he is a strong imager, a mismatching picture is the main risk. A cheap mitigation is that images are reachable but not forced into the reading flow.
- Mendelsund's argument (readers' images are vague and schematic) cuts both ways: a portrait fills a gap readers did not know they had, which is a gain for recognition and a loss of ambiguity; per-Book portraits should follow what the prose states, not add new physical facts.

### Gaps
- No adult controlled study found that measures whether an illustration *reduces* or *replaces* readers' own imagery in fiction.
- No study found on author-generated or AI-generated images of one's own fiction, or on readers who are also the story's protagonist-analogue.
- Scarry's book: no scholarly empirical test found.

## 4. Narrative transportation and immersion: do images, sound or music increase it?

### Takeaway
Transportation (Green & Brock 2000) is defined by attention, imagery and emotion, and occurs across print and screen. Images can raise engagement via imagery fluency (one adult experiment). Evidence on music or soundtracks during reading is sparse and mixed: background music can impair reading, but music that the reader likes and that fits the story may help memory; no strong evidence that soundtracks raise transportation.

### Cited Findings
- **Transportation construct:** absorption into a story entailing imagery, affect and attentional focus; greater transportation strengthens story-consistent beliefs and favourable evaluations of protagonists; occurs for spoken, written and audiovisual stories. [THEORY + CTRL (Green & Brock 2000)] (secondary) — [Green & Appel 2024 review preprint](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)
- **Visual narrative transportation:** a systematic review proposes the construct "visual narrative transportation" for visual stories (images that narrate, show action, resonate). [REVIEW] (title/abs) — [Hasselt University repository](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/42486/1/Narrate%20%20Act%20%20and%20Resonate%20to%20Tell%20a%20Visual%20Story%20%20A%20Systematic%20Review%20of%20How%20Images%20Transport%20Viewers.pdf)
- **Images → engagement (adult):** Cohen et al. 2018 (above). [CTRL n=212] — [RTI](https://www.rti.org/publication/seeing-believing)
- **Imagery and absorption:** aphantasic readers report less absorption (Speed et al. 2024, above). [CTRL n=98] — [Radboud](https://www.ru.nl/en/research/research-news/lack-of-visual-imagery-does-not-lead-to-less-pleasure-in-reading)
- **Music while reading narratives:** Kayser (2024, UNLV thesis): calm music showed a non-significant trend to better memory than silence; participants who enjoyed the music or felt it matched the story showed a memory benefit; with intense music, those who felt it mismatched benefited from silence; "the effect depends on both the music and the individual". [CTRL, thesis] (abs) — [UNLV Digital Scholarship](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/thesesdissertations/5182)
- **Reader-chosen soundtracks on e-readers:** Holenderski & Hu (Eindhoven) built and tested soundtrack-enriched reading, measuring imagery, transportation and attention; the accessible snippets did not report outcomes. They cite literature that background music or noise can impair reading performance vs silence, and an eye-tracking finding of more re-reading with music; extraverts and high-working-memory readers are less disturbed. [CTRL, small HCI study] (abs) — [Holenderski & Hu 2013 PDF](https://www.drhu.eu/publications/2013-AMT-EnrichingReadingExperience.pdf)

### Inferences
- Sound should not autoplay over reading: the best-supported effect of background audio during reading is distraction; benefits depend on fit and liking. Songs offered as a separate, chosen listen after the text sings them are consistent with the evidence; a forced soundtrack is not.
- Images are most defensible as transportation aids for scenes the prose describes, presented so they don't interrupt the read (see contiguity and seductive-detail moderators in §1).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that adds illustrations to long-form prose fiction and measures transportation (only the graphic-novel study).
- Holenderski & Hu outcomes not verified; no meta-analysis on music during reading for pleasure.

## 5. Faces and identification: portraits, empathy, and appearance change across a serial

### Takeaway
I found no controlled study testing whether showing a fictional character's face increases readers' identification or empathy in prose fiction. Identification research in fiction is driven by text, perspective and narrative craft. Adjacent evidence (avatar similarity, picture-book analysis of direct gaze) is suggestive only. Continuity of appearance across portrait changes is unstudied.

### Cited Findings
- **Identification via prose:** Johnson and colleagues found that evocative literary fiction helps readers identify with characters from other cultures and reduces stereotyping (one study: 110 students read a novel excerpt, a synopsis, or an unrelated text). No images involved. [CTRL n=110] (press) — [APS Observer, "Literary Character"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/literary-character)
- **Direct-gaze images at emotional peaks:** picture-book analysis describes illustrations where the character looks out at the viewer, typically after conflicts or significant events, "to heighten identification or empathy at key moments". [THEORY/descriptive analysis, children's books] — [Teseo Press, Understanding and Creating Bimodal Narratives, ch. 3](https://www.teseopress.com/understandingandcreatingbimodalnarratives/?p=37)
- **Images raise engagement (not identification specifically):** Cohen et al. (above). [CTRL n=212] — [RTI](https://www.rti.org/publication/seeing-believing)
- **Similarity and identification with a depicted self** (avatar literature, §6): realistic self-similar avatars beat idealised ones on exertion; customisation raises identification. [CTRL] — [University of Bath](https://www.bath.ac.uk/announcements/buffed-up-avatars-deter-us-from-exercising-hard/)
- **Fixed faces are hard to re-imagine:** once a character is given a face and voice (e.g., by casting), readers report difficulty imagining them differently. [OPINION] — [The Week, 2026](https://www.theweek.in/news/entertainment/2026/09/10/book-to-film-adaptations-impact.html); [Dalhousie paper](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/djim/article/view/2010vol6Martin/53)

### Inferences
- Portraits likely help *recognition* (picture superiority, §1) more than they demonstrably raise *empathy*; empathy evidence lives in the prose.
- Per-Book portrait changes that track changes the prose has already described (scars, age, armour) fit the "image confirms text" rule; unexplained changes risk a continuity break, since readers anchor on the first fixed face. Keeping recognisable constants (face, palette, silhouette) across versions is design common sense, not tested.

### Gaps
- No experiment found on character portraits in prose fiction and identification, empathy or parasocial attachment.
- No study found on how readers handle a character's depicted appearance changing over a long serial.

## 6. Avatars and self-representation: Proteus effect, virtual self-modelling, customisation, idealised vs similar avatars

### Takeaway
This is the best-evidenced area for the Wardrobe. The Proteus effect (people conform to their avatar's characteristics) is reliable with a small-to-medium effect (r ≈ .24 across 46 experiments), stronger in VR. Seeing a self-resembling avatar exercise increased real voluntary exercise in controlled studies (Fox & Bailenson 2009). Customising one's avatar increases identification, which increases intrinsic motivation and reduces drop-out. But **idealised avatars raise wishful identification while lowering actual effort**; realistic, self-similar avatars outperform them.

### Cited Findings
- **Proteus effect meta-analysis:** Ratan, Beyea, Li & Graciano (2020), *Media Psychology* 23(5), 651–675: 46 quantitative experiments with randomly assigned avatar characteristics; consistent effect r = .22–.26 (≈.24), "small-but-approaching-medium", relatively large compared with other digital media effects. [META k=46] (abs) — [Taylor & Francis record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2019.1623698); [ResearchHub PDF copy](https://storage.prod.researchhub.com/uploads/papers/2022/01/03/15213269.2019.pdf)
  - Updated meta-analysis (Beyea, Ratan et al., listed in press with *Presence*): 56 experiments; VR head-mounted displays produced stronger effects than flat screens. [META k=56] (listing) — [David Beyea publications](https://sites.google.com/view/davidbeyea/publications)
  - A 2024 theoretical review discusses mechanisms (self-perception, priming, identification, embodiment). [REVIEW] (title only; full text blocked) — [PMC11233444](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11233444/)
- **Virtual self-modelling and exercise:** Fox & Bailenson (2009), *Media Psychology* 12, 1–25, three experiments in immersive VR grounded in social cognitive theory: (1) participants whose avatar's body changed with their exercise did more voluntary exercise than those with an unchanging avatar or no avatar; (2) watching one's **own** virtual self (vs a virtual other) increased exercise, whether it showed reward (slimming) or punishment (gaining); (3) watching a virtual self run (vs a virtual other running, or the self loitering) produced the most self-reported exercise in the following 24 hours. Self-exercise viewers reported higher exercise self-efficacy. [CTRL ×3] (abs) — [Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/medical/virtual-self-modeling-effects-vicarious-reinforcement-and-identification); [BPS Psychologist](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/doppelgangers-new-form-self)
- **Customisation → identification → motivation:** Birk, Atkins, Bowey & Mandryk (2016, CHI), "Fostering intrinsic motivation through avatar identification in digital games": customising an avatar (vs premade) increased identification and intrinsic motivation; Birk's thesis reports four studies supporting the causal chain customisation → identification → motivation. [CTRL, multiple] (abs/thesis) — [researchr record](https://researchr.org/publication/BirkABM16); [Birk thesis (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/226136841.pdf)
  - Field experiment: Birk & Mandryk (2019), *JMIR* 21(1): in a three-week online mental-wellbeing training, players who customised their avatar dropped out less than those with a generic avatar. [CTRL, field] (abs) — [JMIR](https://jmir.org/article/export/end/jmir_v21i1e10133)
  - In exergame work, customisation significantly increased identification, intrinsic motivation and physical performance. [CTRL] (secondary summary) — [Nweon summary of "Me vs. Super(wo)man"](https://paper.nweon.com/?p=1291)
- **Idealised vs realistic self-avatar (direct and important):** Koulouris, Jeffery, Best, O'Neill & Lutteroth (CHI 2020), "Me vs. Super(wo)man", VR cycling against one's own customised avatar: **idealised avatars increased wishful identification but decreased performance**; participants produced more power and were ~3% faster against a realistic self-avatar; authors attribute this to "feedforward" (a recognisable self-model showing achievable performance). [CTRL] (press; n not verified) — [University of Bath](https://www.bath.ac.uk/announcements/buffed-up-avatars-deter-us-from-exercising-hard/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200313112123.htm)
- **Systematic review of avatar health interventions:** physically active, fit, similar-looking avatars were effective; two studies used self-discrepancy theory; few studies had no-avatar controls, so avatar-vs-no-avatar superiority is not established. [REVIEW] — [MSU PDF](https://innovationcenter.msu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Enhancing-Healthy-Behaviors-Through-Virtual-Self-A-Systematic-Review-of-Health-Interventions-Using-Avatars.pdf)
- Jin (2012), *Psychological Reports*: Wii avatar experiment (n = 156) crossing actual-self vs ideal-self activation with regulatory focus; outcome was dieting intention, not activity. [CTRL n=156] (abs) — [catalogue record](https://vufind.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c367t-d2e4a6796a84daab514d766c210f19f12423b9d52f8b47b66222bec553b0f7d63)
- Exergames offer limited customisation, especially body type. [REVIEW of 6 games] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.770139/pdf)

### Inferences
- The Wardrobe (self-designed outfits, full-body images) matches the customisation → identification → intrinsic motivation chain, the most directly applicable evidence in this whole topic. The author designing the look himself is the active ingredient, not the image quality.
- Fox & Bailenson's finding was that an avatar **whose body/look changes in response to the person's own real effort** increased exercise. Outfits that open from real progress are a close analogue (contingent visual change of one's self-representation), though theirs was immersive VR with a photo-realistic self, not a fantasy knight on a phone; expect a weaker effect (the meta shows flat screens < VR).
- **The main risk is idealisation.** A heroic, idealised knight may increase wishful identification but, per Koulouris et al., can lower actual effort when the person compares himself to it. Design implication: the depicted knight's *physical* state should not run ahead of the real person's (e.g., the leg shown as recovered before it is); gear and adornment can be aspirational, the body's capability should track reality. This also aligns with the "story never advances past what is cleared" rule.
- The Proteus effect is about behaviour *while embodied*; transfer to real-world behaviour after the session is less established (Fox & Bailenson's 24-hour self-report is the main transfer evidence).

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and statistics for Fox & Bailenson (2009) and Koulouris et al. (2020) could not be verified (full texts blocked).
- No study found on avatars that are fantasy characters (not photo-likenesses) and real-world exercise or rehab adherence.
- No study found on AI-generated self-avatars or on unlockable cosmetic outfits as rewards for health behaviour specifically.

## 7. Progress visualisation and motivation: goal gradient, endowed progress, streaks, SDT, rewards, gamification and health/rehab evidence

### Takeaway
Visible progress toward a goal reliably accelerates effort near the goal (goal gradient) and pre-filled progress raises completion (endowed progress). Intact streaks raise continued engagement but *broken* streaks demotivate, especially when the break is blamed on oneself. Gamification has small-to-medium positive effects on learning and physical activity (g ≈ 0.4) that shrink after the intervention and against active controls; points/levels/leaderboards tend to raise *quantity* without raising intrinsic motivation. Expected, performance-contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation; informational positive feedback enhances it. Self-tracking numbers can raise output while lowering enjoyment and later voluntary engagement.

### Cited Findings
- **Goal gradient:** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006), *J. Marketing Research* 43, 39–58: in a real café "buy 10, get 1 free" programme, purchases accelerated as customers approached the reward (inter-purchase time ~20% shorter from first to last stamp, per summary); motivation "resets" after the reward; same pattern in a song-rating website. [CTRL, field] (abs/secondary) — [Columbia Business School](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/going-goal)
  - Illusory progress: a 12-stamp card with 2 bonus stamps was completed faster than a 10-stamp card (same purchases required). [CTRL, field] (secondary) — [Columbia Business School insights](https://business.columbia.edu/insights/chazen-global-insights/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration)
- **Endowed progress:** Nunes & Drèze (2006), *J. Consumer Research* 32(4), 504–512: ~300 car-wash customers; 10-slot card with 2 pre-stamped vs 8-slot card (same 8 washes needed): completion 34% vs 19% (per secondary summaries). [CTRL, field ~300] (secondary) — [Coglode](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect); [Generally Thinking](https://generallythinking.com/2009/08/20/how-to-make-people-get-with-the-program/)
- **Streaks:** Silverman & Barasch (2023), *J. Consumer Research* 49(6), 1095–1117, seven studies: highlighting an intact (vs broken) streak increases subsequent engagement; the gap is **larger when people attribute the break to themselves** rather than external factors, and **smaller when the streak can be "repaired"**; in an exercise-app study ~66% chose another strength exercise with an intact streak vs ~58% with a broken one; the lead author advises apps not to dwell on broken streaks. [CTRL ×7] (abs/press) — [Univ. of Colorado](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- **Rewards and intrinsic motivation:** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), *Psych. Bulletin* 125(6), 627–668, 128 experiments: expected, tangible and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (performance-contingent d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28 across reported analyses); **positive feedback enhanced free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31)**. [META k=128] (abs) — [UW PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [SDT 2001 follow-up](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
  - Context: this was a rebuttal to Cameron & Pierce (1994), who found the undermining effect minimal; the debate is long-running. [META vs META] — [SDT 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- **Intrinsic motivation and incentives jointly:** Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford (2014), *Psych. Bulletin* 140(4): k = 183, N = 212,468; intrinsic motivation predicts performance (ρ = .21–.45) with or without incentives; it matters less when incentives are *directly* tied to performance; intrinsic motivation better predicts **quality**, incentives better predict **quantity**. [META k=183] (abs) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/)
- **Games and SDT:** Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006), *Motivation and Emotion* 30(4), four studies: perceived in-game competence, autonomy and relatedness independently predict enjoyment and future play; competence and autonomy relate to intuitive controls and presence. [CTRL + survey] (abs) — [ACU research bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8q128/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-approach); [SDT Center, PENS](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/player-experience-of-needs-satisfaction-pens/)
- **Gamification meta-analyses and reviews:**
  - Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educ. Psych. Review* 32, 77–112: small positive effects on cognitive (g ≈ 0.49), motivational (≈ 0.36) and behavioural (≈ 0.25) learning outcomes; cognitive effect stable in high-rigour studies, motivational and behavioural effects less stable; **game fiction** and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes. [META] (abs; g values from secondary summaries) — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [gamification-research.org](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
  - Koivisto & Hamari (2019), *Int. J. Information Management* 45, 191–210: review of 819 studies; results "lean towards positive" but "the amount of mixed results is remarkable"; points, badges and leaderboards dominate; field lacks consistent theory and variables. [REVIEW k=819] (abs) — [Tampere University](https://webpages.tuni.fi/gamification/2019/06/24/the-rise-of-motivational-information-systems-a-review-of-gamification-research/)
  - **Physical activity:** Mazeas et al. (2022), *JMIR* 24(1):e26779: 16 RCTs, n = 2,407; overall g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14–0.69); vs waiting-list g = 0.58; **vs non-gamified active intervention g = 0.23**; at ~14-week follow-up after the intervention ended g = 0.15; no moderation by age, sex, BMI, or healthy vs chronically ill. [META of RCTs] — [JMIR](https://jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)
  - **Rehabilitation:** a 2022 narrative review (*Current Reviews in Musculoskeletal Medicine*) of 17 articles on gamification in musculoskeletal rehab (shoulder surgery, osteoarthritis, low back pain, fractures); a 2020 scoping review identified 27 gamified shoulder-rehab systems; a 2025 protocol notes no review yet covers gamified rehab mHealth apps across physiotherapy conditions. No meta-analysis of rehab adherence found. [REVIEW, narrative/scoping] (abs) — [JMIR Serious Games 2020 scoping review](https://games.jmir.org/2020/3/e19914); [PMC listing for 2022 narrative review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9789284/); [2025 protocol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12070003/)
- **Where visible numbers hurt:**
  - Hanus & Fox (2015), *Computers & Education* 80, 152–161: 16-week course, gamified (leaderboard + badges) vs same course without; gamified students showed **declining intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment** over time and lower final exam scores, mediated by intrinsic motivation; badges were mandatory. [CTRL quasi-experimental, ~71 students] (abs) — [UCLA SMHP PDF](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)
  - Mekler et al. (2017), *Computers in Human Behavior* 71, 525–534: 2×4 online experiment; points, levels and leaderboards **increased quantity** of image tags but **did not change competence or intrinsic motivation**; they acted as extrinsic incentives. [CTRL] (abs) — [Glassman Lab PDF](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)
  - **Measurement's hidden cost:** Etkin (2016), *J. Consumer Research* 42(6), 967–984, six experiments (colouring, reading, walking): measuring output **increased how much people did but reduced enjoyment**, by drawing attention to output so leisure felt like work; in a reading study (n = 310), people who saw their progress read more pages in 8 minutes but were **less likely to keep reading afterwards**; framing the activity around learning rather than fun removed the enjoyment penalty. [CTRL ×6] (abs/press) — [Duke Fuqua](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps); [Wharton-hosted PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf)

### Inferences
- Applying this to the site: **the progress the reader sees should be informational, not controlling.** The SDT-consistent pattern is: positive, competence-affirming feedback (a technique ranking up, a new outfit opening) and no punishment visuals. The project's existing "no shame, no penalties; missed days are plot turns" rule is directly supported by the streak research (broken streaks attributed to oneself demotivate; repairable streaks blunt that). Avoid any "streak broken" display.
- Keeping real numbers off the story pages is supported by Etkin (visible output metrics reduce enjoyment and later voluntary engagement) and Cerasoli (incentives drive quantity; intrinsic motivation drives quality). The fiction can be the "learning/meaning" frame Etkin found removed the penalty.
- Levels, XP bars and attribute numbers on a character sheet are classic points/levels; evidence says they raise quantity without raising intrinsic motivation, and Mazeas shows the edge over a non-gamified programme is small (g ≈ 0.23) and fades after. For rehab, over-prescribed quantity is itself a risk; the engine's "doing more earns nothing" rule neutralises the quantity incentive, which is the right direction.
- Goal gradient: a visible "close to the next rank" bar will accelerate effort near the threshold, then motivation dips after the reward ("reset"). In rehab, that acceleration could push the person to overdo it; keep thresholds tied to doing the plan as written, not to volume.
- Endowed progress: starting a new Book or tier with some progress already shown is an evidence-backed nudge, cheap to apply.
- Game fiction moderated behavioural outcomes in Sailer & Homner: narrative wrapping is one of the few gamification elements with meta-analytic support.

### Gaps
- No RCT or meta-analysis found on gamification or avatars for post-surgical knee (ACL) rehabilitation adherence specifically.
- Exact g values in Sailer & Homner and Koivisto & Hamari's proportions of positive/mixed/null results not verified from full text.
- No study found comparing a story-wrapped progress display to a numeric one for exercise adherence.
- The 1999 undermining meta-analysis mixes children and adults; the children-vs-adults split was not verified.

## 8. Curiosity and hidden information: information gaps, uncertainty, spoilers, and hidden vs visible mechanics

### Takeaway
Curiosity arises from a perceived information gap and peaks at moderate confidence; it activates reward circuitry and improves memory for the answer. Uncertain rewards can motivate *more* than certain, higher-value ones when attention is on the process. Spoiler research is mixed (original finding: spoilers don't spoil short stories; later work: they can reduce enjoyment, especially for TV). I found no controlled study comparing hidden vs visible game mechanics; the case for "discover, don't study" rests on these adjacent findings plus SDT (autonomy, competence through mastery).

### Cited Findings
- **Information-gap theory and memory:** Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang & Camerer (2009), *Psychological Science* 20(8): curiosity is an information gap between what one knows and wants to know; in fMRI (19 participants, 40 trivia questions), curiosity correlated with caudate (reward-related) activity and **enhanced memory for answers, especially after incorrect guesses**; curiosity had an inverted-U relation with confidence (highest at intermediate knowledge). A later replication reproduced the core findings. [CTRL n=19 + replication] (abs) — [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf); [Berkeley neuroeconomics page](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html); [Lancaster replication](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/225462/1/osf_revised.pdf)
- **Motivating-uncertainty effect:** Shen, Fishbach & Hsee (2015), *J. Consumer Research* 41(5): people invested more effort, time and money to earn an uncertain reward (50% chance of $2 or $1) than a certain reward of higher expected value ($2), across four studies with real rewards; holds only when people focus on the *process* of pursuit, attributed to excitement from uncertainty. Example: more people finished drinking a large amount of water in 2 minutes for a coin-flip $1/$2 than for a sure $2. [CTRL ×4] (abs/press) — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/679418); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/chance-as-a-motivator-uncertainty-can-make-people-work-harder/)
- **Spoilers:** Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011), 819 undergraduates read short stories (ironic-twist, mystery, literary) spoiled or unspoiled; spoiled versions were rated as enjoyable or more so; proposed mechanism: processing fluency. [CTRL n=819] (press) — [APS news release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/spoiler-alert-stories-are-not-spoiled-byspoilers.html)
  - **Contradicted/qualified:** later experiments found spoilers *reduce* enjoyment under some conditions, with negative effects for television but not short stories; moderators include personality and processing fluency (Brookes, Rosenbaum & Ellithorpe 2024 review). [CTRL + REVIEW] (abs) — [PubMed 30115003](https://www.pubmed.gov/30115003/); [IJoC 2024](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/19466/4538)
- **Autonomy and competence in games:** SDT's competence and autonomy needs predict game enjoyment and future play (Ryan et al. 2006, above). [CTRL] — [ACU research bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8q128/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-approach)
- **Visible output metrics shift attention to output** (Etkin 2016, above), the mechanism by which exposed mechanics could make play feel like work. [CTRL ×6] — [Duke Fuqua](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)

### Inferences
- "Discover the mechanics rather than study them" is consistent with information-gap theory (a partly known system sustains curiosity better than a fully documented one) and with the motivating-uncertainty effect (not knowing exactly what a deed will unlock can be more motivating than a published exchange rate) — **provided** the reader knows *enough* to feel the gap (inverted U: total opacity yields no curiosity, total transparency yields none either). Greyed-out Wardrobe pieces naming what opens them, and a visible-but-unexplained sheet, sit near that middle.
- Uncertainty helps only when attention is on the process; if a hidden mechanic makes the person feel his real effort went unrecognised, it undermines competence (SDT). Every real deed should visibly *count* even when *how* it counts stays hidden.
- Spoiler evidence is mixed enough that withholding plot and world facts (the `_gm` wall) is a safe default rather than a proven gain; for serialised, screen-like consumption (closer to TV than to a short story), the negative spoiler findings are the more relevant ones.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing hidden vs visible game mechanics, rule transparency, or "discovery" design on motivation or enjoyment; this is a clear evidence gap (game-design writing on it is practitioner opinion).
- No study found on curiosity effects over months-long serials (Kang et al. is single-session trivia).
- Ellithorpe & Brookes (2018) enjoyment results not verified.

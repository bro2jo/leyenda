# BOOK II — THE TEMPERING ROAD

# Chapter 5 — The Thornwild Woman's

*Steps: 1,117, down, every one. Things on the Holloway I had never seen before: 38. Sheep: not to be counted separately, by order of Ser Hollis. Doors opened to us on the drove road: 0. Doors opened to the carter on the drove road: all of them, until we were on her cart.*

— from the ledgers of Wren Ashdown

---

### Scene 1 — The Drove Road

By the second milestone Darrow had got down off the tailboard, and nobody had stopped him.

Maelis had let him. She had looked at the knee, then at the road, flat and pale between its hedge-banks as far as the snow let anyone see, and said, "To the next stone. Then you ride, and you don't argue." So he walked at Hob's head with one hand on the yoke and the stick in the other, and the ox put up with him.

The cold was the clean kind that stopped at the skin. The knee reported every rut, and he found he did not mind the reports; he was warm to the ends of his fingers, and he was walking down a road. A month ago that had been a thing other men did.

Somewhere below, the banner was coming up the same road. He looked for it at every rise. So, he noticed, did Rae.

Wren had been counting since the winch-house: milestones, gates, a dead crow, an empty gibbet, a woman who stopped beating her rug to stare. She had reached sixty-one sheep when Hollis, on the tailboard with his leg out straight, told her sheep were not counted separately.

"How else do you count them?"

"By the field. Write *a field*."

"That isn't a number."

"It is in the Lowmarch."

She wrote something down. Darrow suspected it was sixty-one.

Once, at a bend, she turned round on the tailboard and looked back the way they had come. The mountain stood over the whole valley, white to the cloud, and under the cloud, small as a fingernail, the House: a pale scratch on the rock with the Steps going up to it like a stitch. She had lived her whole life on that scratch. She looked at it for as long as the bend lasted, and her pencil stayed where it was.

Ash ranged ahead and came back twice to report nothing, delighted with it, the splint clacking on the frozen ruts, and the mule walked beside Dun as if it had been hired and meant to be paid. Rae walked at Dun's shoulder, which put both oxen between her and Darrow, and that seemed to suit everyone, the oxen included. He had been working out how to ask for half a mile.

"Did you—"

"I told her," Rae said. "Thistle. In her ear, at the dealer's, the morning I went down."

He had to look at the road a moment.

"And?"

"She put her ears up. Then she went back to his hay." Rae knocked a clod off Dun's flank with the goad. "She's not sentimental. She's a grey."

He laughed, the whole laugh, the one that used to carry down a hall, and the mule shied at it, and Hollis swore at the mule, and the mule, by every sign, swore back.

---

The road came over a rise, and below them, where the Holloway dipped to the tarn and Coldmere's smoke, there it was.

Riders, two and two, the low sun on their helms, the banner dark on a pale field; in the middle of the column the Confessor's cart, small and slow, with the censer's thread of smoke going straight up from it; and at the head a rider who had the better seat, and had always had it. Darrow would have known him at five miles. Wren had her hand flat over her eyes. "Forty-four," she said. "Forty-six. Their cart. His cart."

Rae had the oxen turned before he had properly looked. She put the goad to Hob's shoulder and said one word to him, and the cart came off the Holloway onto a track Darrow had not seen at all, a drove road between two walls, running north round the far side of the water.

Darrow stayed where he was, in the middle of the Holloway.

"Rae."

"It goes round the tarn. We come at Coldmere from the north, when it's empty of them." She did not stop.

"That's Aldric."

"I know who it is."

"He wrote he'd come himself. He's come. I can stand on this road and let him find me, and say what I've got to say to his face, and—"

What he could not say in front of Hollis was the rest of it: he wanted to watch that face while it answered. The west bank in the spray, Aldric's horse already turned and climbing, one heartbeat early. He had carried that up the Steps and down them again, and the one man who could tell him what it meant was a mile off, riding toward him.

"And then what?" Rae stopped the oxen and turned round on the track, and said it all, more words together than he had had from her in a month. "That's a Lance on the march, and a Confessor's cart gone down to meet it. You've four people on that cart who are on a paper, and one who isn't, who drives it. You stand there and say your piece, and he says his, and then forty men ride on up to the winch-house and take the cart and the oxen and the Mender, because a Lance on the march doesn't pass a cart, it takes it. I've seen it done. Not at the Holloway's Elbow. Anywhere else. This is my road, and I'm not driving it into him so you can see his face."

"She's right," Hollis said from the tailboard, "and you know she's right, Captain, and you hate it, and I'd pay money to watch you hate it a while longer, but they've outriders, and outriders have eyes."

"And you've walked to your stone," said Maelis.

He stood on the Holloway and looked at the rider at the head of the column, and knew what he was doing, because he had done it before. He had looked across a river once at a line of spears and seen the thing he wanted to hit, and decided, and the deciding had felt exactly like knowing. He had decided this one on the Steps, when the road came out of the cloud with the banner on it, and walked two miles toward it without once asking anybody.

"I'd decided," he said. "Before any of you said a word. It's a habit." He made himself look away. "It's how I lost the last horse."

Rae had the cart on her road, and she was not moving it. She stood at Dun's head, looking at him, and it took him a moment to see what she was waiting for. Nobody on the Holloway had ever needed his leave for a thing she did; she had told him so with every load she ever brought up the Steps. She was waiting for it now.

"Your road," Darrow said.

"Aye." Something went out of her shoulders. "Get on the tailboard. You've had your stone."

He got on the tailboard.

---

All afternoon the walls kept off the wind and the outriders' eyes. Twice the column's jingle came across the water, and Rae stopped the oxen in the lee of the wall with her hand on Hob's nose until it passed. At dusk they came to a sheepfold on the north shore, a stone hut for the shepherds and nobody in it, and Hollis declared it the finest inn in the Lowmarch and asked where they kept the beer.

Wren lay on her back on the cart with the ledger on her chest. On the mountain the sky had been cloud most nights and a roof the rest.

"How many are there?"

"Nobody's ever counted them," Darrow said.

"Nobody's ever tried." She licked the pencil. "One."

Across the tarn, Coldmere's lights came out one at a time, and on the green by the water a row of fires came out with them that had not been there at noon. The column had stopped in her village.

Rae looked at the fires for a long time. Then she pointed with the goad at the biggest light in Coldmere, square and yellow, down at the water's edge.

"That's my uncle's," she said. "He'll be pouring for them."

### Between — Across the Water

Maelis had built the fire inside the shepherds' hut, because Rae had said a fire on this shore would show on that one, and Maelis had looked across the tarn and agreed without a word of argument, which Hollis said he would remember on his deathbed. What came out of the kettle was hot, and that was all anybody would say for it.

Darrow carried a cup of it out into the dark with the stick under his arm and no lamp. Rae was sitting on the fold wall above the water, where she had been since the fires came out, with her cloak pulled round her knees and the ox-bell closed in her fist to keep it quiet.

"No light," she said, before he reached her.

"No light." He held out the cup. "Maelis made it. I'd call it a drink if I were feeling generous."

She took it in both hands and drank, and made a face, and drank again. He got himself up onto the wall beside her, which took longer than he liked and less than it would have a week ago. Ash came out of the dark, considered the two of them, and lay down across both their feet, which settled whether he was staying.

Across the water the fires on the green had burned down into a row of red. The inn's door opened and shut, and each time it opened a long bar of yellow went out across the tarn toward them and stopped short.

"Tell me about him," Darrow said. "The man pouring."

She answered all of it, in one go, and set it down where it would stay.

"He keeps the only inn in Coldmere, and the best on the Holloway, and he'd tell you the second is because of the first. Everything that goes up or down the road goes through his taproom: carters, drovers, heralds, the Crown's clerks, your lot, their lot. He pours for all of them, and he listens to all of them, and he forgets none of it. That's what he's for." She turned the cup in her hands. "He poured the night they read your list. Stood behind the tap and filled the reader's cup between the names, so the man wouldn't dry up halfway down."

"And you?"

"I went out the back. The House's salt was on the cart and the oxen were still in the yoke. I didn't ask him." She looked at the yellow door across the water. "I've asked him everything since I could reach the tap. That was the first thing I didn't."

He thought about that, and about a girl standing on a box behind a bar, and about what it had cost to walk out through a yard in the dark without asking.

"Will he give us up?"

"That's two," Rae said, and answered it anyway. "I don't know. He'll do what's good for the inn. He always has." The door opened; the bar of light came out over the water and went in again. "Sometimes that's me."

He did not tell her it would be all right. She would have known what it was worth.

"If it isn't good for the inn," he said, "we don't put it to him. We go round Coldmere."

"You'd go round my village."

"I'd go round anything you asked me to. I've been practising all day."

That got her: one short breath through the nose, which on Rae was a laugh, and her shoulder against his for the length of it, and there it stayed.

"You've had two," she said. "I'll have mine." She was looking across the water, not at him. "On the road today. Standing there. What were you going to say to him?"

### Between — The Better Seat

He took a while over it. Across the water the inn's door opened and let its bar of light out over the tarn and took it back. Behind them in the hut Hollis had started singing to the kettle, low, one of the Sixth's, with a verse in it about a Confessor and a goat that no kettle should have had to hear.

"I was going to say I'd missed him," Darrow said, and watched how she took it.

She took it with the cup in both hands, and weighed it, and he felt it being weighed.

"That's light," Rae said. "For what you were carrying on that road."

"It's true, though." It was. "We held other men's horses together when we were too small to hold them. We were knighted in the same chapel on the same morning, and I watched his face when the warmth went into him, like a man hearing music he'd only been told about. He had the better seat and the better manners and the second-fastest feet in the Lowmarch, and he'd have told you they were the fastest." Something went through him that was nearly a laugh. "Nobody's called me an idiot in that particular voice since midsummer. Hollis does it, but Hollis does it about everything."

"And the rest of it?"

He looked back at the hut. The door was shut, and Hollis had reached the goat.

"The morning of the ford he told me to ride wide," Darrow said, lower. "Then wider. He had his mouth open on something else, and the chaplain's bell went, and he shut it. Halfway across the river the warmth went out of me, and I looked back at the west bank." He had to stop and start again. "His horse was already turned. Already climbing out of the shallows. One heartbeat before the rest of us went down, he was going the other way."

Rae had stopped turning the cup.

"Somebody knew it was coming," he said. "Sixty men don't lose the same thing in the same breath by chance, and you don't turn a horse for nothing. If somebody knew, he knew. I've known it since the water. And I don't want it to be true. I've wanted that every day since midsummer, and every day it's been true anyway."

"So you wanted his face."

"I wanted to stand in front of him and say, *Did you know?* and watch it while he answered. He could lie to anybody in the Lowmarch. He never once managed it to me, not to my face."

Rae looked across the water at the yellow door for a long breath before she answered, and when she did, it was plain.

"I keep faces," she said. "You know I do. Every man I've brought to the foot of those stairs, and every one I took down again. A face tells you what a man's afraid of. It's no good at all for what he did." She tipped the cup at the far shore. "My uncle's got a good face. Everybody says so."

Darrow turned that over, and liked it less the longer he held it, which was usually how he knew a thing was true.

"Does Hollis know?" she said.

From the hut came the end of the goat verse, and Hollis laughing at his own rhyme.

"Does that sound like a man who knows anything?" Then, because she was still waiting: "No. He carried the order up the bank, and he's never looked at the seal on it. When he does, I'll be there. It's his to come to, not mine to hand him."

"Then it's carried." She put the empty cup into his hands. "I don't open what I carry."

Across the water the inn's door shut for the night, and the tarn went dark from shore to shore. In the hut, Hollis finished the goat and started on the Confessor's sister.

### Scene 2 — Above Every Other

If Rae remembered the fold wall, she showed it only by walking on his side of the oxen.

The bread had run out at breakfast, by a route Hollis would not discuss, and by noon the drove road had brought them down off the moor's shoulder to the first houses since the Holloway: six of them round a well, a byre, a pound for strays, and a bell-post where the tracks crossed, with one small bell under a roof of its own. Every door was shut. Every shutter was barred, at noon, in a hamlet where the only thing worth stealing was the bell.

"Six chimneys, four smoking," Wren said. "Somebody's home, and somebody would rather not be."

A paper was nailed to the bell-post, the nails still bright and the paper still white under its blob of blue wax. Darrow could read the first line from the cart. He got down and read it anyway, because a man ought to read his own name standing up.

Wren read it aloud behind him, fast, as she read everything. "By the Knight-Confessor's seal, these to be held and given up, on pain of the Crown's displeasure, as Faithless who would not kneel." She stopped. "You're first."

"I'm usually first."

"Not like this." She put her finger on it. His name sat alone at the top, in letters a size bigger than the rest, as if the clerk had wanted it read from the road: SER DARROW OF EDGEMOOR, KNIGHT-CAPTAIN OF THE NINTH LANCE. Then a space. Then, smaller: *the Mender Vorne, of Saint Ysolde's, who harbours him.* Then the Ninth and the Sixth, man by man.

"Where am I?" Hollis demanded from the tailboard.

"Third."

"Third." Hollis took it like a wound. "I was second at the bars. A man goes down one mountain and loses his place to a surgeon."

Maelis had come round the cart and was reading her own name with the pipe clamped in her teeth. She read it twice. Darrow watched her do it and could not have said what was in her face, which was the purpose of her face.

"Well?" he said.

"Good," said Maelis. "Now nobody has to pretend I'm the nurse." She took the pipe out. "It means he wants me as much as he wants you, and that I'm the easier one to carry off. Don't go standing in front of me about it. You'll only fall over."

Rae had gone to the reeve's door.

Darrow knew it for the reeve's by the bench outside it and the iron on the shutters. Rae knocked, and said something low through the grille, and a man's voice answered her by name, and they talked a while in the voices of people who had done business for years. Then the voice said, loud enough for the well to hear: "Not off that cart, Rae. Not with that on the post. I'm sorry for it."

Her hand went to her purse. The grille shut.

She walked back to the cart unhurried and took up the goad, and her face was a carter's face that had just been told the road was out.

"How long have you sold to him?" Darrow said.

"Since I could lift a sack." She was looking at the oxen. "His father before him."

There it was, then, what he cost her. Not coin, and not the bread: twenty years of a door opening when she knocked on it.

"Put me off at the next wall," he said. "Take the cart through without me and pick me up a mile on. They'll sell to you."

"They'd sell to me." She set the goad to Hob's shoulder. "And then I'd be a carter who put her load down because a reeve looked at it. On this road that's worse than anything they can nail to a post." The cart moved. "Get up or walk. I'm not stopping."

He walked.

---

They made camp past the hamlet in the lee of a field wall with a barred gate in it. The rain came over the hills at dusk in a hurry and left the same way, and after it the drove road smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke. Nobody said anything about supper, there being nothing to say it about.

The man came over the wall a little after the rain stopped, at the corner farthest from the hamlet, with a cloth bundle held against his chest. He was young and broad in the face, and Darrow knew the face before he could place it, until the man stood in the firelight and he could: it was a lay brother's face, younger, and without the hood.

"My brother had the foot of your litter," the man said. "Going up the Steps, the summer. He sent word down with the salt, after." He held the bundle out, not to Darrow but to Rae. "He says the captain stood up at the gate and wouldn't kneel. There's a few of us here heard it. We don't put it on any post." He looked at Darrow once, quickly, as a boy looks at a man on a horse. "It's bread. It's only bread."

Rae took it. "Tell your mother I'll be by," she said, "the next time I'm by."

The man went back over the wall. Hollis already had the cloth open.

The lantern came a while after: down the track from the hamlet, swinging, with three men behind it and one of them carrying a fork. They stopped at the barred gate and held the lantern up to the bars, and the man holding it was the voice from the grille, grey and heavy and frightened.

"Rae," he said. He sounded sorry about that too. "I saw that boy go over the wall. I've a writ on my post and three children in my house and a Confessor's rider through here twice a week, and if they ask me, I want to have asked." He lifted the lantern past her, to the fire and the faces round it, and stopped on Darrow's. "So I'm asking. Who rides on the Thorne cart?"

### Between — A Load Named at a Gate

Every face at the fire went to Darrow. He felt them go, Wren's first, then Hollis's, then the lantern's yellow edge on his cheek, and the reeve's eyes behind it, waiting to see whether the face fitted the name on the post.

He looked at Rae.

It was a carter's road and a carter's gate and a reeve who had bought her salt since she could lift a sack. She knew every man behind that lantern and which of them would remember what was said, and he knew none of them. She saw him look, and understood it, and got up from the fire.

She went to the gate and stood at the bars with her hands on the top rail, close enough that the lantern lit her and not the people behind her.

"You've asked," she said. "That's done, and you can say it. You came down with a lantern and three men and asked Rae Thorne who rides on her cart."

"And?"

"And she wouldn't tell you. You know why, because your father told me why, at your own door, when I was nine and tried to sell him a sack I hadn't paid for." She did not raise her voice. "A carter names her load to the one who paid for it. Nobody else, and never at a gate. You didn't pay for this one."

The reeve's lantern came down a hand's breadth.

"If a grey cloak asks you," Rae said, "tell him that. Tell him Rae Thorne stood at your gate and wouldn't say. It's true. You can swear to it on your post with your hand on the nails."

"And the boy?"

"The boy brought bread to a carter he's known since he could walk. That's what he did. If anybody asks you that, it's true as well."

The reeve looked at her for a long moment, then past her once more, at the fire, and Darrow saw him decide what he had seen. It was the face of a man setting down a heavy thing in the place he had meant to set it all along.

"That'll do," the reeve said. "That'll do, Rae." He turned the lantern away. "Home."

Two of the men went with him. The third, the young one with the fork, stayed at the bars a breath longer than his father had, and he was not looking at Rae. He was looking at Darrow, and from Darrow to the bell-post up the track, as if he were reading the one off the other. Then the reeve said something short over his shoulder, and the boy went, and the lantern went jogging up the track and in at the reeve's door, and the door shut on it.

Rae came back to the fire and sat down in her place.

"Men at the gate: three," Wren said into the quiet. "Men who went home believing her: two." She had the ledger open on her knee and the pencil moving. "The one with the fork can read. I watched his mouth go along the post this afternoon."

"He's the reeve's eldest," said Rae. "He reads. He's wanted that bell-post since he was six."

"Then we're gone before their bell," said Maelis, who had not looked up from the fire the whole time and had heard all of it. "Grey light. Not dawn. The leg's had two days of road; it can have the third in the cart until noon."

Hollis was tearing the bread into equal parts with the care of a man dividing an inheritance. He handed Rae hers first. "That," he said, "was the best lie I ever heard that wasn't one."

"It wasn't a lie."

"I said that. I'm praising you, carter. Don't argue with it, it doesn't happen often."

Darrow had said nothing since the lantern came. He found he had nothing he needed to say, and one thing he wanted to.

"You answered for me," he said to her, across the fire.

"You looked at me." She tore her bread in half and put the larger half on his knee without looking at it. "It's my cart. You were right to."

He ate it. It was only bread, as the boy had said, and it was very good.

The fire burned down. Ash went and lay with her nose to the gap under the gate, where she could hear the track. Wren wrote one more line in the ledger and shut it without showing anyone, which meant it was about one of them.

When the others had gone under their blankets, Rae got up to see to the oxen. On the way across the dark, the bell at her belt rang once, and she let it.

### Between — From the Belly

Darrow lay under his blanket after the reeve's lantern had gone, long enough for the cold to get into the knee, as it always did now when he lay still. Getting up off the ground took the wall, the stick and a word to the knee that his mother would not have liked. He went over to the oxen anyway.

Rae had them on a short tie in the lee of the wall, out of the wind, and she was going over Hob by feel in the dark: the shoulders where the yoke sat, the dewlap, down each leg to the foot. She heard him coming the whole way and kept her back to him.

"Want a hand?"

"Can you hold a head?"

"I walked at a horse's head before I could argue."

"An ox isn't a horse."

"Nothing is." He took Hob's head-rope where she pointed, close up under the jaw, and the ox turned one great wet eye on him and decided he would do. "Evening, Hob. You did well today. You were patient with a reeve. Few are."

"He doesn't like being talked to like a horse."

"I'm talking to him like a sergeant."

"He likes that less." But she had bent to Hob's near forefoot and lifted it with her shoulder against the leg, and was working at the split of the hoof with the point of her knife. Her back had gone unsteady. She was trying not to laugh at a man telling an ox it had been patient.

A stone came out of the cleft and clicked off the wall. She set the foot down. Hob shifted, all of him at once, as oxen do, and leaned toward the warm of the ashes, and Rae said a word.

It was short, with a catch in the middle that Darrow's mouth had no shape for, and she did not say it so much as set it down from somewhere under her ribs. Hob stopped dead. So did Dun, who had not been going anywhere.

"What was that?"

"Stand." She wiped the knife on her knee. "My grandmother's word. It's Thornwild. She had a few dozen, all for oxen. This is the one that works."

"Say it again."

She said it again. He tried it. What came out of him was a Lowmarch noise with a hiccup in it, and Hob turned his whole head round to look at him, slowly, the way a sergeant looks at a recruit who has said something about his mother.

Rae laughed. Not the breath through the nose; out loud, once, into the dark, and put her hand over her mouth after it as if it had got out without leave.

"From the belly," she said, when she could. "Not the mouth. The mouth's for talking. This isn't talking."

He tried it from the belly. It came out deeper and no more right, and Dun, who was already standing, stood harder.

"Wrong ox," said Rae.

"He took it as meant for him. Dun's a generous animal."

"Dun's a fool. He'd stand for a cough." She took the head-rope back from him and tied Hob short again, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the ox's neck. "Keep it. It's not much use to anybody but oxen."

"I'll keep it."

He went back to his blanket saying it under his breath, a word for stand, and got it wrong all the way there. Behind him, into Hob's neck, where nobody was meant to hear it, Rae laughed again.

### Scene 3 — East

They left the field wall at grey light, before the hamlet's bell had done more than clear its throat, and Darrow rode the cart until noon because Maelis had said so. She had made the tailboard her bench and Hollis's boots her first patients, and spent the morning putting a new sole on one of them with a sail-needle and open contempt.

The cold had got into Darrow in the night and stayed. It sat in his hands on the side-board and would not be rubbed out, and the sky had gone the hard pale blue that makes a cold day colder. Nobody sang to the oxen. Even Ash walked close.

At noon the drove road came down off the moor and ran along the top of a turnip field, a long brown slope of it, the leaves gone to rags with the frost. Maelis looked at the sun and said, "Noon," and Darrow got down.

Wren saw him first. "There's somebody in the field."

A man was coming up the rows from the bottom of the slope. A knight: he had the shoulders for it, and mail under a Lance's surcoat with the colours faded past reading, and no helm, and no sword. He was walking east. Not toward them; across the slope at a slant, at the pace of a man following a plough, stepping over each row as it came. He did not look at the cart or the walls or the dog. The frost on the turnip leaves broke under his boots with a small, dry sound, and it was the only sound in the field.

"Bells take it," Hollis said, very quietly, and then nothing.

Darrow went down into the field. The furrows were frozen hard and uneven and the knee took them one at a time, complaining, but it took them, and he came across the knight's line ten paces ahead of him and stood in it.

"Ser."

The knight came on. Darrow had stood in front of men who were charging and not stepped aside; this one was not charging. He walked into Darrow's chest, gently, and stopped there because something was in the way, and stood. He was breathing slow and even. Darrow knew that breath. He had lain listening to it every night of the autumn from the far end of the hall.

The eyes were open. Whatever was behind them was a long way back.

"Ser. What Lance?"

Nothing.

"Who was your captain, lad?" Hollis, from the cart, in the voice that had carried over the Wend at the ford. It went down the field like a thrown stone. The knight's head did not move.

"Turn round," Darrow said.

The knight turned round and stood facing west, toward the cart and the moor and the way they had come.

"Walk."

He walked: three steps up the slope, four. On the fifth his feet began to slant, the way water slants on a tilted board, and by the seventh he was going east again across the rows, at the same pace, as if he had never been asked anything.

"Stand," Darrow said, and the knight stood.

Darrow went round to the front of him. He was younger than Darrow had thought: thirty, perhaps. His boots were worn through at the toes and his feet were bound in rags inside them. There was a healed nick on his jaw where a razor had slipped, not long ago.

Wren came down the field with the water-skin. Nobody had asked her. She reached up, put the mouth of the skin to his lips with one hand and the other flat under his jaw, and said, "Drink," in a voice Darrow had never heard her use, flat and kind and without a number anywhere in it.

# Prologue — The Three Heartbeats

*Count the lances on the west bank, child,*
*count them, one through nine.*
*Count them going, count them coming home,*
*and never get the same sum twice.*

— counting rhyme of the Lowmarch

---

## I. Harrow Ford

The Wend was running high for midsummer, brown with three days of rain off the hills, and the noise of it filled the valley so that a man had to lean out of the saddle to be heard. Darrow liked that. It meant the Ninth couldn't hear him yawn.

He sat his grey mare at the head of the Ninth Lance on the west bank of Harrow Ford and watched the mist tear itself apart over the water. Sixty knights behind him. Sixty squires behind them. The banners hung wet on their poles like washing nobody wanted. A hundred yards downstream, Hollis Garrow's Sixth was dressing its line, and Hollis himself was audible even over the river, explaining to some unlucky boy what the boy's mother had done to deserve him.

At Darrow's left hand, close enough to touch, sat Aldric Vane.

They had ridden knee to knee since they were squires holding other men's horses. They had been knighted on the same morning in the same cold chapel, kneeling shoulder to shoulder with their palms on the Oathstone while it warmed under their hands, and Darrow still remembered Aldric's face when the Grace went into him the first time. He had looked like a man hearing music he had only ever been told about. Aldric had the Second Lance now. He had the better seat, the better manners and the better luck with the kind of women whose fathers mattered. Darrow had the fastest feet in the Lowmarch and had never once cared whose father mattered.

"You look like a man at his own wedding," Darrow said.

Aldric didn't smile. He was watching the far bank. "Ride wide today."

"I always ride wide."

"Wider." He turned his head, and for a moment his mouth was open on some other word. Then the chaplain's bell began down the line and Aldric shut his mouth and looked away.

The field-stones came up on their carts, one to a lance: slabs of black Oathstone the size of a tavern door, set in iron frames and hauled by oxen that hated them. Each lance dismounted by rank. Darrow swung down, walked to the Ninth's stone in the wet grass, and knelt on his left knee as he always did, without thinking about it, because there was nothing to think about. He laid his palm on the stone.

It was cold, and then it wasn't. The warmth came up through his hand and into his arm and settled in his chest. Old knights called it *the Crown's hand on your back*. Every ache he owned went quiet, and the morning got sharper at the edges. He could hear the river separately from the wind now, and the wind from the horses, and under all of it his own heart, slow and enormous.

He got up. Sixty knights got up behind him, and the Ninth was full of Grace.

"Captain." Hollis had walked his horse up the bank to them, red-faced, a sealed writ in his fist. "From command. The Sixth and the Ninth to clear the ford. Second in reserve." He broke the wax with his thumb without looking at it and held the paper out as if Darrow might want to read it. Darrow didn't. He was looking across the water.

"Hollis," he said. "Look at their wagons."

The clans of the Thornwild were drawn up on the far bank among the standing stones, and they were drawn up wrong. The fighters stood in a ragged screen at the water's edge with long knives and boar spears, but behind them, where reserves should have been, there were carts. Ox-carts piled with bedding. Old people sitting on the bedding. A goat. Children, a lot of children, being shushed. And the warriors at the front kept turning their heads to look behind them, into the trees, as if what frightened them was at their backs.

"So they're stupid," Hollis said.

"Or they're not coming at us. They're coming *through* us."

"Is that in the writ?" Hollis rattled the paper. "No. The writ says clear the ford." He spat in the river. "Your feet are the fastest thing in the Lowmarch, Darrow, but your head's not. Mount up."

At the front of the clan line, a woman stood knee-deep in the shallows with her hands painted red to the wrist. She was looking straight at Darrow. Not at the banners, and not at the lances. At him. She called something across the water in her own tongue, and the river took it.

Darrow mounted.

---

He would be honest about this later, because Maelis would make him: he loved it. He loved the moment when sixty horses came off the bank together and hit the water, the white explosion of it, spray going up higher than the lances, the cold slapping his face and failing to reach him through the warmth. He loved the speed. With the Grace in him, the river was a road and the stones underfoot were stairs he'd known all his life. He loved being at the point of the thing, with nobody in front of him, his men a weight of noise behind him and everything ahead of him getting larger, faster, simpler.

He was laughing. Thistle had her ears flat and was laughing too, in her way.

Halfway across, the warmth went out of him.

It went the way heat goes out of a room when someone opens the door onto winter. One moment it was in his chest, and the next there was a hole where it had been, and the hole was cold, and the cold was everywhere. Frost bloomed on the rings of his mail, white feathers of it, in midsummer. The noise of the river came back all at once and was enormous.

That was the first heartbeat.

On the second, everything happened at once. Thistle stumbled. Her legs had been running on his Grace as much as her own, and now they were only a horse's legs in a flooded river, and she went down at the shoulder. All around him the Ninth sagged in their saddles like men whose strings had been cut. Lances dipped. A knight to his right simply slid sideways into the water, and his horse kept going without him. To Darrow's left, Tobin Marsh, nineteen years old and the youngest of the Ninth, who sang badly and constantly, went under his own horse in the current.

Darrow kicked his feet out of the stirrups and came off Thistle as she fell. He landed thigh-deep on the riverbed stones, and every instinct he owned said *turn*: get to Tobin, cover the left. He planted his left foot on a stone and turned on it, the way he had turned ten thousand times, fast and low.

And the knee went.

There was no blade. Nothing touched him. Something inside the joint simply let go, the way a rope lets go under too much weight: a soft, wet give that he felt in his teeth. Then his leg was a thing that belonged to someone else. It folded sideways under him like a door off its hinge, and the river took the rest.

The third heartbeat he spent underwater.

Brown water, roaring. Stones. A horse's belly going over him like a cloud. He had been told all his life that a drowning man sees his whole life, but Darrow saw only one thing, and it would stay with him longer than the cold: as he went down, through the spray, he saw Aldric Vane's horse on the west bank. Already turned. Already climbing out. Aldric bent low over its neck.

Already. One heartbeat early.

---

The Grace came back, if you could call it that. A thread of warmth crept into his chest like a candle lit in a barn, too thin to do anything but show him how cold he was. He came up gasping, grabbed a stone, lost it, grabbed another. When he tried to stand, the leg slid out from under him. When he tried again it did the same, sickly and loose. It wasn't pain yet. It was wrongness, and that was worse.

All around him, the river was full of the Ninth.

A hand closed in the collar of his gambeson and hauled.

He was dragged backward through the shallows, not toward his own bank but away from it, east, and dumped on the gravel among the standing stones of the far shore like a sack of meal. He rolled over. The red-handed woman was standing over him, waist-deep in the water, breathing hard. Up close she was older than he'd thought, forty or more, with a scar through one eyebrow and the patient, furious face of a woman who had been right about something for a long time.

She grabbed his jaw and turned his head toward the river.

"Look at it," she said in rough Vaelish. "Look. Remember it."

He looked. The Wend was carrying knights downstream like driftwood. On the west bank, the Second Lance sat its horses in perfect order, untouched, watching. And behind the clan carts, in the dark under the trees of the Thornwild, stood a line of grey figures he had not seen before. They were very still. They were wearing armor. They didn't move at all, not even when the river screamed.

When he turned back, the woman was gone.

He did not see her again for a long time. He didn't see much of anything for a while.

## II. The Steps

Five days on a cart, then a barge, then another cart. Nineteen of the Ninth Lance came back from Harrow Ford. He made himself count them. He knew the names of the other forty-one and didn't say them.

Hollis Garrow rode the next cart over, missing his right leg above the knee, and cursed for five days without repeating himself once: the surgeon, the river, the Crown, the clans, the oxen, the weather, the carter, Darrow, and finally himself, which was when he went quiet. In every village they passed, the Crown's heralds were already reading out the proclamation from the steps of the bell-house. The Grace had departed the knights of Harrow Ford for the faithlessness of their oaths. The Faithless would be received with mercy at the Oathspire and permitted to kneel anew.

"Kneel to that," Hollis said, and nobody on either cart laughed.

The surgeons in the Lowmarch looked at Darrow's knee, pressed it, watched it slide, and sent him north. *Saint Ysolde's*, they said, the way you would say *the sea*. The House of Menders took the ones the lowland surgeons couldn't fix and didn't want to watch.

They carried him up the Thousand Steps on a litter. Four lay brothers, two at each end, switching every hundred steps, while the cliff fell away on one side into cloud and the wind came round the shoulder of the mountain with a knife in it. Beside the litter walked a girl of about twenty with ink on her fingers and a pencil behind her ear, and she was counting the steps out loud.

"Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and twelve."

"Do you have to?" Darrow said, around the fever.

"Technically, yes. Someone has to." She glanced down at him. "There's eleven hundred and seventeen. Everyone says a thousand. Everyone's wrong. Four hundred and fifteen."

"You missed one."

"I did not miss one. Four hundred and sixteen."

He didn't have the strength to argue, and she didn't stop until the top, where bells were ringing and the air smelled of snow and sulphur and boiled linen, and someone very small and grey-braided was standing in the gate with her arms folded, looking at his leg as if it had said something rude.

## III. The Binding

"It isn't broken," said Maelis Vorne. "It's undone."

She had her two first fingers on the joint and her eyes shut. She had been like that for a long time. The infirmary hall was dark except for her candle and the long double row of cots running away into shadow, and from the far end, where they put the ones who didn't speak anymore, came the slow, even breathing of people who no longer needed to be told when to sleep.

"The cord that holds the thighbone to the shin," she said. "The one in the middle, that stops it sliding. It's gone. Snapped." She opened her eyes. "What hit you?"

"Nothing."

"Good. That's the right answer. Most of them say a mace." She took her fingers away. "You planted and turned and the leg couldn't hold what you put through it. It wasn't a blow."

"It held fine for thirty-two years."

"It had help for twelve of them." She said it without any particular weight, but she watched his face while she said it.

He waited for her to tell him what she meant. She didn't. She told him instead that she would not touch the knee for six weeks.

"You don't bind a river in flood," she said. "The swelling comes down first. The leg straightens first. The thigh wakes up first. Strength before the Binding, because a Binding takes the measure of whatever you tie it to. Bring me a weak leg and I'll give you a weak knee."

So for six weeks he lay on his back with his heel on a rolled blanket and let the weight of the leg pull it straight, which hurt in a slow, stupid way. He clenched the muscle of his thigh until it shook and watched the kneecap twitch, which was all it could do. He learned to walk the length of the hall with a stick, then without one. Hollis watched from the next cot, still mostly drunk, and offered commentary.

On the last night, Maelis sat down on the end of his cot with a wooden box on her knees and didn't open it.

"There are two ways off this mountain," she said. "The first: you go down the Steps on a crutch, you go to Calden, you kneel at the Oathspire like the heralds say. The Grace fills you up again. The knee stays loose, but you won't feel it for years, because the Grace will carry it. Then one day it won't, and you'll fall down a stair and not get up. That's the Crown's way, and to be fair to the Crown, it's fast."

"And the second."

She opened the box. Inside, on black cloth, lay a curved needle and a spool of something that wasn't thread. It glowed faintly, the deep orange of a coal when you blow on it, and the warmth came off it even from where he lay.

"I take a strand from the sinew below your kneecap," she said. "Your own. I put it where the cord was, and I bind it in with this. It's an old art. The Crown burned the people who knew it three hundred years ago, and I'll thank you not to mention to anyone that I do."

"And then?"

"And then it's yours. Made of you. It'll be exactly as strong as you make it, and not one hair stronger. It'll be soft for a long time, softest just when it starts to feel good, and if you're stupid in that season you'll tear it and I won't do this twice. It will take most of a year. Maybe more. Every day of that year you'll do dull things I tell you to do, and some days you'll do them and nothing will happen, and you'll do them again the next day anyway." She shut the box. "No Grace in it. Nothing given. Nothing anyone can take back."

Darrow looked up at the dark roof of the hall. He thought about the field-stone warming under his palm. He thought about Aldric's horse on the west bank, already turned.

"Can I kneel?" he said. "After."

"No. Not for a long time. Perhaps not ever."

It was a strange thing to grieve for, and he was surprised to find he did. Then it passed, and in its place was something he didn't have a name for yet. It felt like a door left open.

"Well," he said. "I was never much good at it anyway."

Maelis took that as a yes.

---

The emberthread went in like warmth poured into a cold cup. He remembered that much, and the smell of her bitterleaf pipe, unlit, clamped in her teeth while she worked, and her voice saying, very quietly, as if to someone who wasn't there: *Time makes it possible. Proof makes it permitted.*

Outside, the bells rang the hour. Somewhere in the House, Wren was counting them.

# Chapter 6 — The Weir

*Roads out of Coldmere: 2. Riders behind us: 1, which is worse than forty. Carts on the road ahead of us, by their tracks: 2. Carts on the road: 1. I was in it.*

— from the ledgers of Wren Ashdown

---

### Scene 1 — Three Fingers and a Thumb

The mill tracks went up into the hills between hedges higher than the cart, two ruts and a crown of frozen grass, so that the company climbed all morning in a green dark with a strip of sky on top. When the sun found the hedge-tops the frost came off them in drops, and the drops found Hollis.

"Bells take it," said Hollis, for the fourth time, and moved along the tailboard, and the next drop found him there.

Rae drove. She had the lines in both hands and her eyes on Hob's ears, and the bell at her hip still had its rag in it from Coldmere, so that when a wheel dropped into a rut the cart made the noise of a cart and that was all. The only sound off the box was the leather of the lines.

Darrow walked at Hob's head with the stick and left her the quiet. He had chosen it for her at the fork, with his own mouth, and a man who did that owed her the hearing of it. Behind them the river road had people on it. He looked at the hedge.

Wren counted, and there was little enough to count. Gates, four, all shut. A fox, one, who stood in the track and considered Ash, and Ash, in the cart among the sacks, considered him back and let him live. Hedges she gave up on. "How far do they go?" she asked the box. The box drove. She asked the tailboard instead.

"To the mill," said Hollis.

"Which mill?"

"The one at the end. That's how you know a mill track. You follow it till it stops, and there's a mill, and you say, ah."

"You've never been up here."

"I've been up a hundred of them. They're all the same track." A drop found him. "Bells take it."

Darrow laughed, and Hob's ears went back and came forward, and the box stayed as it was: two hands on the lines and a hood turned to the road.

The knee had kept its own counsel since the fork and went on keeping it, and he had stopped waiting for it to speak. It was the hands he noticed this morning. The right one on the stick had a thing in it he had carried off the mountain and only put a name to in the night, lying in the hut on the tarn with the cold in his fingers; and he had kept the name to himself, because Maelis walked behind the cart with her roll on her shoulder and would put it to him when she chose. The choosing was hers. He had learned that much on a mountain.

---

The tracks came down to a stream at noon: a brook off the hills over brown stones, with a slab laid across for feet and a ford beside it for wheels, and on the near side a bank where the sun had got in and the grass was wet and green instead of white. Rae stopped the oxen at the water with the word from her belly, the first of her voice Darrow had had all day, and it was for Hob and Dun and nobody else.

"Sit," said Maelis.

He sat on the tailboard with the leg out along the boards, as he had sat on a cot for a season, and she put the pipe in her teeth and her two fingers either side of the knee and shut her eyes, and the company went away a little, as it did, and pretended.

Wren took the ledger down to the water and counted stones aloud, which was pretending badly. Hollis got down and told the mule what he thought of its mother. Rae stood at Hob's head with her back to the cart and her hand on the yoke. Ash found the one piece of bank the sun had reached and lay down on it as if she had ordered it.

The Reading took as long as it took. A week ago she had done it at the top of the Steps and said one word, and he had not known where to put it. Today she opened her eyes, took her fingers off, and said, "It's walking. Walk it. Till I say."

That was the knee. He had learned on the mountain to ask it for nothing more.

"The other one," said Maelis, and held out her hand.

He gave her the right.

She turned it palm up on her knee, as she turned a knee, and pressed the two last fingers flat to the palm with her thumb, and let them go. They came back up. They came up late, the two of them, behind the others, like men late to a muster who have heard the horn and are coming at a walk.

"Close it."

He closed it. The thumb and the first two shut as a fist shuts. The two last came after, and stopped short, a hair off the palm, and stayed there, and he looked at them as a man looks at a gate that will not quite swing to.

"Feel that?" She pinched the little finger at the last joint, hard.

"Late."

"Late." She let go. "That's what it is, then. The frost went into those two at the grate under the House, and what frost takes that deep it keeps. They've come back as far as they'll come. Slow, and half there. It'll be a different grip on a sword from now, and on anything else you mean to hold." She took the pipe out of her teeth. "I'll not dress it. A Mender who lies to a patient buries him."

Down at the water Wren's count had stopped. Hollis had stopped on the mule's mother. At Hob's head Rae's hand lay still on the yoke.

Darrow looked at his hand open on the Mender's knee, the two fingers lying a little crooked of the others.

"I'd have taken that about the knee," he said.

"I know you would. You've been waiting for it since the river."

"Yes."

"The knee I bound with your own sinew and a thread it cost somebody their life to learn. There's no thread for a finger." She said it as plain as weather. "It's not the hand you had. You've a season to learn the one you've got."

He got down off the tailboard.

He took the stick in the hand he had always taken it in, and it turned. Not far: a quarter turn in the slack of the two fingers, enough that the ferrule went where it liked on the frozen ground. He stood and looked at that. Then he moved his hand up the stick and took it higher and closer, in the fork of the thumb and the two good fingers, with the slack pair laid along the wood for the look of the thing, and leaned on it, and the stick stayed where he had put it.

"There," said Maelis.

"That's how my grandmother held a spoon."

"Did she drop it?"

"Never once."

"Then hold the spoon."

He turned the stick in the new grip, once, and set it down again, and said what he had meant to say when he got down.

"Give me the sword."

She looked at him a moment. Then she reached into the cart behind him and brought the oilcloth bundle out from under the salt, and unwrapped the end of it across her knee: the hilt, and a hand's breadth of blade, dull as a Mender's knife, with the one word cut near the hilt that he had laughed at on the mountain.

He took it high on the hilt, three fingers and a thumb. The two others lay along the leather with nothing to do. It was a strange hand to have a sword in. The sword sat farther back in it, and lighter, and when he turned his wrist the point went where the wrist went and stopped there, where his old hand would have sent it, out of long habit, a span past the mark. He cut the air once over the stream, slowly, and stopped the cut where he meant to.

"Well?" said Maelis.

He gave her the honest answer first, because she had.

"It's a smaller sword than it was," he said. "And it's quicker in the stop." He turned it, and watched the two idle fingers ride along the hilt. "I'd sooner have the fingers."

"So would I."

"There's a grey in Coldmere I'll want a rein for in the spring."

"A rein's two fingers' work. Ask me about a saddle in the spring, and not before." She held her hand out for the sword. He kept it a moment longer. "But I'll take the stop."

"About time," said Hollis.

He had come up on the beech leg with a hand on the mule's neck for a rail, and stood looking at the hand on the hilt as a farrier looks at a foot.

"Every man with ten fingers holds a sword like he's strangling a goose, Captain. You did. I watched you do it in the cloister and let you, because you'd have argued." He flicked the two slack fingers with a nail. "The bind's in the first two and the thumb. It always was. Those two are for the pommel, and for showing the ladies, and they're what puts a man's cut a span past where he wanted it and his guard open behind it. The Seated Blade never had a use for your little finger. Now you've none for it either. That's not a loss. That's an argument finished."

"You've been waiting to say that since the river."

"I've been waiting to say that since I was thirty. I never had a man with the right hand for it." Hollis took the sword out of his grip, turned it, and put it back a finger's breadth higher up the hilt. "There. Fight from where you're put, I told you. That goes for the hand and all."

"Fingers that work: eight," said Wren, from the water, writing. "Captain's. Out of ten." She looked up. "That's a better count than Ser Hollis's legs."

"Leave my legs out of your book."

"They're in it under *furniture*."

Darrow laughed, the whole of it, over the stream, and the sound went up between the hedges and a bird came out of the far one in a hurry; and when he had done laughing he wrapped the sword as she had wrapped it and gave it back, and Maelis put it under the salt without a word about any of it.

Rae had turned round at Hob's head. She had heard all of it, as the oxen had. She looked once at the hand, and then at Hob, and said the word that moved them, from the belly, and the cart came off the bank and down into the ford with the water loud on the wheels.

Darrow walked at Hob's head with the stick high in the new grip and the two fingers along the wood for show, and for the first hundred paces up the far bank he watched the hand and not the road. Behind the cart Maelis walked with her roll on the north shoulder and watched him take them.

"Good," she said then.

He had had that word from her twice in a season now, and it weighed what it had weighed the first time, and he carried it up the track into the hills.

### Between — Not the Carter

"Are you still angry about the river road?"

He had got himself up onto the box to ask it, which took the rail, the new grip and longer than he liked, and he asked it before he had his breath back before he could think better of it. Rae moved along the board to give him the side away from the wheel. Behind them Wren had gone to sleep in the sacks with the ledger under her cheek, and Maelis walked a dozen yards back, where the hedges kept the talk. Hollis sat on the tailboard with his eyes shut, which proved nothing.

"Aye," said Rae.

She let it sit. Hob found a rut, and the cart rolled through it and out.

He had sat out a reeve's lantern beside her. He could sit out this.

"Some," she said, at the next gate. "Not all of me."

"Which part?"

"The part that knows that road." She tipped her head east, where there was only hedge. "I've carted salt to the farms down it since I could hold the lines. Their dogs know my bell. There's a boy on that road rides my tailboard from the mill to the bend every harvest and won't sit still on it, and that shoe in the ditch was his size." She looked at the oxen. "And I took my cart past the end of it with the bell stopped. A carter with no bell is a thief till she proves otherwise. I went by them like one."

Darrow had his answer ready, and he gave it, because it was true.

"There was a herald's man on that road with my name by heart, and the Mender's, and Hollis's. Your cart's the one a grey cloak crossed the green to ask you about. Every farm on that road would have had us in its yard by dark. They're safer for the cart that didn't come."

"That's true," said Rae.

He waited for the rest of it.

"It's still a road I didn't drive." She shifted the lines in her hands. "That's not the part, though."

"What's the part?"

The cart went a long way up the track before she said it, and she said it with her eyes on the road.

"I was glad," she said. "At the fork. When you said north." The bell at her hip swung on its rag and made no sound. "I've carried for everybody on this road since I could hold the lines. Everybody or nobody, that's the trade. You don't pick. And you said north, for your own, and I was glad of it, and I turned the oxen glad." She looked at him then. "That's what I've been chewing on since the fork. Not you."

He had climbed up to take her anger off her. Anger would have been easy to carry. This was heavier, and it was hers, and he sat with it while the hedge went by.

"One true thing," said Rae. "That was mine."

He gave her his.

"I chose north for the herald's man. Not only for him." He turned the stick over on his knees. "I watched that grey cloak walk across the green to you and say his piece, and watched you answer him, and I couldn't hear a word of it from the wall. I'd not have you asked again. So I picked the road with nobody on it to ask."

"You chose it for me."

"Some of it."

"And said not a word of that at the fork."

"You gave me the road."

"I gave you the road." Rae shook the lines, and Hob leaned into the yoke. "I never gave you the carter."

It went in where she had aimed it. She had told him in her uncle's yard, in other words, that on her road he would be first at nothing, and he had agreed and meant it. Then at the fork he had decided for her in his head and called it a choice of roads.

"No," he said. "You didn't."

"Next fork, then." Her eyes were back on the road. "You say why first. Out loud. Then I'll say aye, or I won't."

"Next fork. Why first."

"Aye."

From the tailboard, with his eyes still shut, Hollis said, "Is that the end of it? Only there's been a drop of hedge working down my collar since the ford, and I've waited all day for somebody on this cart to be speaking so I could complain to them."

"Complain to the mule," said Rae.

"I did," said Hollis. "The mule agrees with me. Where's the joy in that?"

# Chapter 3 — The Undercroft

*Doors into the hall: 3. Keys on the Prior's ring: 9. Keys on the Prior: 10. Bells between the night bell and the dawn bell: 8. Oaths said at them: 3. Steps down to the undercroft: not yet counted.*

— from the ledgers of Wren Ashdown

---

### Scene 1 — The Flour

At the dawn bell Darrow swung both legs off the cot together, out of old habit, and was halfway down the row before he noticed the left had come along without being asked.

He had a question for the Mender. The flour came first.

The hall was still grey. Most of it was asleep or pretending, and the fires were down to their coals. At the dark end of the east wall a lamp was already burning low on the flags, and behind it, on her knees with her arms spread like a woman guarding a hen from a fox, was the kitchen novice.

"Nobody step in my flour," she said, before she saw who it was. Then, a little less fiercely: "Kinsman. Captain. Nobody has. I've been here since the lamps."

"Good. Don't let them."

Sister Pell sat on the stool beside the linen press with both shawls up to her nose and her eyes shut. The stool was the one the lay brother had set behind the Mender. Her hands were in her lap, and they were red across the palms in two straight lines.

"I'm not asleep," she said, without opening her eyes. "Up the tower. Ring. Down the stair. Sit. Up again. Eight times." The eyes opened, pale and very much awake. "Nothing came through that door, Captain, and nothing went in. I'd have heard a mouse. I've heard mice for fifty years."

He looked at her hands and did not tell her she should have sent a novice. She would only have told him the bell was hers.

He could not get down to the flour, and the kitchen was not going to watch him try.

"Hold the lamp low," he said. "Lower. There."

The kitchen novice put the lamp almost on the flags, and the light ran flat across the floor the way it had run along the limewash two dawns before, and showed him what was there.

The flour lay as she had laid it, white and smooth from the iron to the foot of the linen press, and there was not a mark in it. No boot, no bare foot, no mouse. But in a half-moon out from the gap under the door the white had gone grey and stiff, glazed over like breath on a window, and when the novice moved the lamp a hand's width every grain of that glaze caught the light the same way, all of them leaning, as if the floor had been combed toward the door in the night.

"Something breathed on it," the novice whispered.

"Breathed in," Darrow said.

Wren came down the row at a run with the ledger under her arm and her pencil in her teeth, and stopped dead a yard from the flour, because the kitchen novice put out an arm like a gate. She looked over it for a long moment. Then she took the pencil out.

"Prints in," she said. "None. Prints out. None. Frost." She wrote. "The wall sweats along that seam every dawn. I've looked." She looked up. "It's not new. It's just the first night anyone sat up with it."

"Every night," said Sister Pell, from her shawls, not at all surprised.

Wren turned the ledger round so he could see the top of a fresh page. *Times the Captain has asked the Mender: 1.* There was a great deal of room under it.

The House arrived the way the House did everything now, all at once and with opinions. Lay brothers in their aprons, novices with their hair down, the cook with a cleaver she had forgotten to put down, and in front of them the lay brother who banked the fires, who had nodded at the door in the night and had evidently been nodding at it in his sleep.

He looked at the flour. He looked at the frost. He took it in faster than Darrow had expected, and his face went from frightened to useful, which on a frightened man is worse.

"Then it's down there now," he said. "Right now. Captain, we'll have that door off its pins by the noon bell. Mother Ione's at the chapel; she needn't be troubled. Two bars and the big mallet from the—"

"No."

"You found it, Captain. You'll want to be first down."

That landed somewhere under the ribs. Every head in the hall knew it, because every head in the hall went, very briefly, to the knee.

"I will be," Darrow said. "On the Mender's count, with the Prior's key. I'll just be the last to arrive."

Somebody laughed. The lay brother didn't. Behind him two of the younger ones had already gone for the mallet; Darrow could see the backs of them at the kitchen door.

He did not raise his voice. He let it go down to the bottom, where it carried.

"Listen to me. There's a gap under that door you could slide a letter through, and something on the other side has been drawing on this hall through it every night. Through a letter's width." He let them look at the glaze. "You want to take the whole door off and see what it does with a doorway."

Nobody answered. At the kitchen door the two young ones had stopped with the mallet between them.

"Nobody touches this door without the Prior. She has the key, and the Mender has my word." He looked at the lay brother from the fire, and made it a request, because a man who has been useful in the night wants to be useful in the morning. "Sister Pell has been up the bell tower eight times since the night bell. There's no wood up there and her hands are raw. If you want something to carry, carry that."

The lay brother looked at Pell's palms. His face did something complicated, and then he went, and the two with the mallet put it down by the kitchen door and went after him. The rest of the House stood a while longer looking at the flour, and then went back up the row to its fires.

Sister Pell got up off the stool, which took her some time, and would not be helped.

As she went past the Grey Cot, Hollis lifted his head from the lad's hand.

"Go and get some sleep, sister," he said. "The door'll keep."

He heard himself say it: the same words he had given the lad, the last night the lad could hear them. Darrow saw it happen, the words out and then the old man's hand closing on the lad's, slowly, as if he could take them back that way.

Sister Pell looked at him, and at the lad, and at his hand.

"After the noon bell," she said, more gently than Darrow had ever heard her say anything, and went on to her tower.

---

Maelis came at the hour, as she always came, and stood at the foot of his cot until he put the knee where she wanted it. She read it the way she always read it: two fingers, then the flat of her hand, then her eyes shut.

"What did it do this morning?"

"Came off the cot with the other one," he said. "Didn't ask me first."

She opened her eyes. She did not say *good*, because she never said good.

"You told me to ask you again," he said. "It's tomorrow."

"So it is." She wiped her two fingers on her coat. "Then I'll ask you something first. What will you do at the bottom?"

It was the Prior's question. Last night he had not had an answer to it that would bear weight.

"Come back up."

"On what?"

"On this." He put his hand on the knee, the way you put a hand on a horse you are about to ask something of.

Maelis looked at the hand, and at the knee under it, for a long moment.

"That's the right answer," she said. "It isn't true yet. Ask me tomorrow."

She went down the row and past the Grey Cot and into the dark at the far end, where the far cots were, without a lamp, as she always went.

When he looked round, Wren was writing. She did not show him the page. She did not need to.

At the Grey Cot, Hollis had not let go of the lad's hand.

"Well?"

"Not yet."

Hollis looked at the lad's mouth, where the frost still was.

"Then ask her faster, Captain," he said. "He's not getting any warmer."

### Between — Even Money

After the night bell there was light under the kitchen door again, and Darrow went toward it, because the hall had nothing else for him tonight.

The cook was asleep in the chimney corner with her cleaver across her knees. The kitchen novice had the book open on the bread table. On the bench across from her, Rae Thorne sat splicing a halter, and did not look up.

"Captain." The novice turned the book to him before he could ask, the way you show a man his own horse has won. "Look."

The column of cots had not changed. The foot of it had. Under NOBODY and *the Captain, the ovens* and *Thorne, a load*, the stakes went on down the page in a dozen hands and over onto the next: wicks, a knife, a month of scouring pots, a lay brother's good boots. Someone had written *the cleaver*, and someone else had crossed it out.

"Since last night," said the novice. "You said nobody's. In front of everyone. So it's even money on nobody now, Ser, and they're still coming." She had the pencil ready over the page. "Do I rub them out? The prices. Like you said."

He looked at the page for a while.

"No."

Her face fell a little. She held on to the pencil.

"The bet was no other cot goes grey before the thaw," he said. "Not who did it. The thaw's a long way off, and the cold comes up that stair every night. Nobody's won anything yet." He put his finger on NOBODY. "But you can cross off the nights. Every dawn there's no new grey blanket, put a line through one."

"A number," said the novice, as if he had handed her something. She wrote at the top of a clean margin, very small: *Nights without.* Under it, a single stroke.

Rae had stopped splicing. She was looking at the book the way she looked at a load on a cart: how it sat, and what was under it.

"Heavy book," she said. "Whole House is under it now."

"It was already. Now it's written down."

"Aye." She pulled the splice tight and tested it with both hands. Then she looked at him, which she did not often do. "I'll have my aye."

He had lost it to her on the gate wall, over a load he had called wrong. She had not mentioned it since, and he had known she would not forget.

"Ask."

"When you've been down that stair, you'll tell me what the cold does on the stone. Plain. Not how you'd tell the House."

He looked at her. She had had that cold in her cart, she had told him once, and would not say when.

"Aye," he said.

The novice wrote that down too, because the kitchen wrote everything down, and Rae went back to her halter as if nothing had been owed in the first place.

### Between — The Even Bells

When he came back up the hall from the kitchen the lamps were down to the far one and the brazier, and the only man awake in the row was Hollis, on the edge of his cot beside the Grey Cot, with the lad's hand in his.

Darrow sat down on the end of his own cot, across the brazier from them, and did not lie down.

"You'll be wanting your sleep," Hollis said, without looking up. "Captain."

"The bell's not far off."

"No."

The lad lay as he had lain for a week: on his back, breathing, eyes open on the roof, the frost on his mouth catching what light there was. He had said his oath at every bell since the ford. The night he was taken he had said it at three and not at the fourth, and the whole House knew that now, and nobody had said it to the two men who had been in the river with him.

"What is it you say to him?" Darrow asked. "Every night. It never carries."

Hollis was quiet for a while. His thumb moved over the lad's knuckles.

"I tell him what I didn't." He said it to the lad, not to Darrow. "That nobody was letting anybody kneel. That I said it so he'd sleep. He said his oath at every bell for me, the whole way up the road, because I told him it would keep him. So I owe him one bell at a time." He looked up at last. "It's not much of a trade. The truth, for a lie, to a lad who can't hear either."

"He heard the lie."

"Aye. Well." Hollis looked at the roof. "That's the part I'm paying off."

Down the hall, under the far lamp, the night novice turned on her stool and was still. Then the bell came, up in the tower, slow, the way it came now that the hand on the rope was sitting down.

The lad's lips did not move. They had not moved since the fourth bell.

Darrow had given him the shapes of the words twice, in the nights before, and no sound with them; his throat had shut on the first word both times, like a door he had not known was there. He found the first word again now and set his breath against it.

"Stone and Crown," he said, aloud, and the door was not there. "Knee and hand. What I am given I hold. What I hold I give."

They were not loud. They did not need to be. They went across the brazier and settled on the cot, and the lad went on breathing, and the frost on his mouth did not change.

"It wasn't for the Crown."

"No. He'd not have known the difference." Hollis looked down at the hand in his. "I do."

The bell had stopped. Up the row somebody turned over and slept on.

"There's seven more before dawn," Hollis said. "He never missed one till the fourth. I'll not have him missing them now." He set the lad's hand down on the grey blanket, carefully, and took it up again. "You take the odd ones, Captain. I'll take the even."

Darrow looked at him. Forty years of barracks had taught Hollis Garrow to be asleep when a bell came.

"You'll say it?"

"I'll say it." His voice did not change at all. "And the fourth's mine."

### Scene 2 — Blue Wax

The fog rose from the springs at dawn and did not lift, and at the hour bell the Confessor walked up out of it to the bars as if the fog had made him.

He had brought no censer this time and no grey cloak to carry one. He stood alone on the far side of the gate with his hands folded, and when the gate-ward went for the Prior he called after him, very civil, that it was not the Mother he had climbed for this morning.

"It's you he wants, Captain," the gate-ward said, at the foot of Darrow's cot. He was turning his cap again. "He asked for you by your rank."

"He always does."

Wren was already on her feet with the ledger. Darrow told her to stay, and she came anyway, three paces behind him across the court, which was her idea of staying.

The court was white and close. The fog had taken the far wall and the bell tower and left only the gate, black bars standing in nothing, and Marrant behind them. He smiled when he saw who was coming, the way a host smiles at the first guest.

"Captain. I'm glad. I'd have hated to leave these with a stranger." He brought two letters out of his sleeve, one tucked inside the other, and held them through the bars. Blue wax, and a hand on the outside he would have known in the dark. "They came up the Holloway last night with a rider of Ser Aldric's. I said I'd carry them. It seemed the least I could do, being already on the stair."

Darrow did not take them at once.

He knew what Marrant wanted. Not the letters; whatever was in them, he had not climbed for that. He wanted the Captain's face when he saw the hand, and he wanted the court to see him want it.

So Darrow looked at the bars, and the wax, and at nothing in particular, the way he had looked at a herald's writ across a table, with lords watching to see what it cost him. Then he took them.

"That's kind," he said.

"It's nothing." Marrant bowed a little. "I'll thank you for your candour another day. You've been sparing with it this morning." He looked past Darrow at the House, the novices in the kitchen door, the lay brothers along the wall, Wren with her pencil. If he saw anything new in them he did not say so. "Good day, Captain. Ser Aldric is anxious for an answer. I'd not keep him waiting long."

He went back down into the fog, and it closed behind him before he had gone a flight.

---

Darrow read them on the end of his cot, with the brazier between him and the Grey Cot, the way he had read the first.

*Brother,*

*I have written once already and do not know whether it found you. The man who carries this will not tell me, so I will write as if it did.*

*They are saying in Calden that the Ninth broke faith at the Wend: that its captain turned out of the line to go back for one of his own, and that the Grace left the Ninth in the moment he did. I have said in three rooms that it is a lie. I will say it in a fourth, before the King, if you will give me the words. I know you. So I know what I am asking.*

*Did you turn? Tell me you did not turn toward the boy.*

*A.*

He read it again, the way he would have read a writ, for what it wanted and who stood behind the man who wrote it. It had been written for two readers. Aldric had known whose sleeve it would ride up the Steps in, and there was nothing in it a Confessor could use but the last line, which he had not been able to keep out.

He had turned. He had planted his left foot on a stone and turned on it, fast and low, the way he had turned ten thousand times, and the knee had gone. But the warmth had gone out of him first, halfway across, before any of it. Then the mare. Then the boy, Tobin Marsh, nineteen, going under his own horse at Darrow's left. Then the turn. He had been there for the order.

Aldric had been on the west bank by then, his horse already climbing out. He could not have seen which way Darrow turned. Somebody had told him, and told it backwards.

The second letter had been folded small inside the first and sealed on its own, in a faster hand and a different ink, as if Aldric had come back to it after the candle was out.

*Answer me, or I will come myself.*

At the head of the first, where Aldric dated everything like a clerk because he had been taught to and had never once forgotten, it said: *Calden, the third day before the snow.*

Darrow sat with that.

Rae had heard the list read at the inn in Coldmere the night the first snow came. Three days before that, in a warm room in Calden, Aldric had known the House and the mountain, and written to the Captain of the Ninth there as if he had never needed to ask.

Across the brazier Hollis had looked up from the lad's hand. He was looking at the blue wax on the blanket. He looked at it for exactly as long as he had looked at the Confessor's writ at the bars, and then he looked away, at the wall, and did not say anything at all.

Wren had come in behind him and was standing at the foot of the cot with the ledger open, not writing.

"Letters to the Captain of the Ninth," she said at last, very quietly. "Three."

"I can count, Wren."

"I know." She shut the ledger. "I just didn't want to be the only one who'd said it."

He turned the smaller sheet over in his fingers. *Or I will come myself.* Aldric had never once said a thing like that and not done it.

### Between — What I Hold

Darrow did not write that night, not at once. He sat on the end of his cot with Aldric's letters on his knee and nothing yet to say to them.

*Somebody had told him, and told it backwards.* Somebody had also sent the Ninth into the river.

Across the brazier Hollis sat at the Grey Cot with the lad's hand in his. The night bell had gone. The first hour bell was not far off.

"Hollis. The writ from the ford."

Hollis did not look up. "What of it?"

"You brought it up the bank. You broke the wax and held it out to me, and I didn't read it." He had been looking at the water, and at the clan line, and at the carts behind the spears; at the man holding the paper, not at all. "What became of it?"

For a while Hollis did not answer, and his thumb went on moving over the lad's knuckles. Darrow had seen him go quiet three times now over blue wax. This was not that quiet. This was a man deciding whether to lie, and finding he could not be bothered.

"I've got it," Hollis said.

"Still?"

"Came down the river road on a cart with me, and up the Steps on a litter. It's not gone anywhere."

"Have you looked at it since?"

"At the order? Don't need to. The Sixth and the Ninth to clear the ford. Second in reserve." He said it the way the lad said his oath, by heart. "I could say it in my sleep. I have."

"At the seal."

Hollis's thumb stopped.

"No."

"Why not?"

"A seal's command. The rest is for clerks." It came out worn smooth, like something he had said to himself a great many times. Then, lower: "And if I look, I'll know whose. And then I'll have to be a man who knows."

Darrow thought of the blue wax against the bars, and the blue wax curling black in the brazier, and Hollis walking out of the hall on one leg rather than watch it. He did not ask to see it. Hollis was waiting for him to, and was ready to refuse, and Darrow would not make him.

"When you look," he said instead, "I'd like to be there."

Hollis looked at him then, a long, measuring look, the kind he gave a man before deciding where to put him in a line.

"Aye," he said. "Maybe."

The bell came, up in the tower, slow. It was an odd bell, and Darrow's, and he said the words across the brazier to the lad, aloud, as he had the night before.

When the next one came, an hour later, it was Hollis who said them, gruff and low, without missing one. Lying awake, Darrow heard him stop for half a breath before the last line, and then go on.

*What I hold I give.*

### Scene 3 — Nine Down

Marrant came up to the bars at the dawn bell to ask whether there was an answer, and there was. Darrow gave it through the bars folded once and not sealed. He had no wax, and would not have used it if he had.

The Confessor did not pocket it. He unfolded it there at the gate, in the grey before the fog rose off the springs, and read it in no hurry, and Darrow stood on the other side of the bars and let him. He had it by heart. He had written it twice over at a brazier that went out before he did, and he read it again now off the Confessor's face, line by line, as the eyes went down the page.

*Aldric,*

*Yes. I turned. I planted on a stone and turned toward Tobin, and the knee went, and I would turn again.*

*But tell them the order, since you are telling them things. The warmth went out of me first, halfway across, before Thistle went down, before the boy, before I turned. I was there. Whoever told you was not.*

*I will not kneel. Not for pride in the leg. Because I would be kneeling to that order told backwards.*

*I burned your first. I nearly burned this one too, so that you would come. I want to see you that much. I want to understand what I saw from the water, and I cannot, and you are the only man who can tell me.*

*D.*

Marrant's eyes stopped once, near the middle, and went on.

"There's the candour," he said, and folded it along its crease. "I said I'd thank you for it another day. Thank you." The letter went into his sleeve. "It'll find him. Letters do."

He went back down to his terrace. Darrow went up to the wall-walk, where Wren had Sister Pell's glass on its strap and had been at the parapet since before the bell.

"I'm counting it down," she said, without lowering it. "Your letter. Somebody should. He's handed it to one of his at the fire, and that one's taking it down." The glass crept. "The Long Reach. They don't hurry, do they. The Elbow." The cloud took the runner there, the way it took everyone, and the glass went on without him, down to where the Steps came out of it at the Shins, past the winch-house, out along the Holloway where the road ran pale through the snow, and stopped.

"Riders," she said. "Far down. Twenty, about. I'll say twenty and be wrong by two." She turned the glass a hair. "There's a banner."

"Grey?"

"No." She lowered the glass and held it out to him. "Not grey. That's all I can tell you, and I'm the one who's been holding it."

He found the road, then the dark knot on it, then the scrap above the knot that might have been colour and might have been the wind in his eye. Too far. They were not hurrying either. His letter was not yet at the Shins. Whoever they were, they had set out before it.

He gave her back the glass. "Write it down. Twenty. Not grey."

"Twenty, about." She wrote. "Technically, *about*'s the important word."

---

Maelis came at the hour, as she always came, and for once she did not read the knee. She looked at it, and then at his hands, which still had the night's ink on them.

"Up," she said. "Warming room."

He heard it as a reprimand, and was too tired to argue with one, and went.

The warming room was nine steps down off the cloister, with no rail. He had been down them before and up again, a hand to the wall, on days when nobody was counting. Somebody was counting now. Maelis stood on the top step. Two novices at the fire below turned round to look and did not turn back, and a lay brother with an armful of wood for Pell's tower stopped in the cloister behind her and stayed there with it.

"Hand on the wall," Maelis said. "You go on my count. Not before it."

"I've done these."

"Not on my count. One."

He went down onto the first step. Down was the part the knee hated. Going up it only had to push; going down it had to hold him and let him go at once, and on every step it thought about it. He could feel it thinking. The stone was rough and cold under his palm.

"Two."

She did not hurry him. Each number came the moment the step was finished and not before, as if she could see the knee settle from where she stood, and somewhere about five he stopped listening to the knee and listened to her instead, which was easier. At nine his foot was on the warming-room floor and the novices at the fire had stopped pretending to talk.

Maelis came down after him, put two fingers either side of the kneecap there at the foot of the stair with the novices watching, and shut her eyes.

"Up," she said.

He went up on her count. At five, with the top in sight and the lay brother still standing there with his wood, he took his hand off the wall. He could do the last four without it. He was nearly sure he could, and he was tired, and tired of being counted like a child on a ladder, and a man who could take four steps without a wall could—

Maelis, a step below him, reached up past his hip, took his wrist, and put his hand back flat on the stone. She held it there under hers while she said "Six," and then let go.

Nobody at the fire laughed. It would have been easier if they had.

"Seven. Eight. Nine."

He stood on the top step with his hand still on the wall, because she had not told him to take it off. She came up beside him and looked at the knee, and then at his face, the way she had looked at the knee.

"That's a stair," she said.

It took him a moment. *It straightens. It walks. Neither of those is a stair.* She had said it in her cell with the pipe clamped in her teeth, the morning Wren first saw grey cloaks on the Holloway, and he had hated her for it for a week.

"Tell the Prior I'll take you down," Maelis said. "My count. Wren's shoulder. Dusk, when the House is at its supper. It's as dark down there at noon as at midnight, and I'd rather fewer people watched you do it."

He heard that short too, an order to be got on with, and was halfway along the cloister before it came to him that he had not asked her today. He had meant to. He had been too tired to want the answer again, and she had given it anyway.

*As dark at noon as at midnight.* She had said it the way she said what a knee would do. Not as a guess.

---

Mother Ione was in the chapel with the lamps not yet lit. She heard him come in, and did not turn round until he had got all the way up the aisle.

"You told me to come when the Mender said yes," he said. "She has."

"Has she?" The Prior folded her hands. "And at the bottom?"

"I look. Then I come back up it. On this leg, at her count."

"Is it true, Captain? Or only the right thing to say?"

"It's true today."

She looked at him the way she looked into a full room, for whoever was missing from it. Then she nodded, once.

"Would you like it at dusk, then, when the House is at supper?"

"The Mender said the same."

"Did she," said the Prior, and for once it was not a question.

---

At dusk the Prior asked the House whether it would like its supper at the kitchen end of the hall tonight, and it would. The fog had gone back down the mountain in the afternoon, and the last of the light came in white off the Steps and lay along the row. Up the row Hollis had turned at the Grey Cot to watch, the lad's hand still in his. He did not call anything down the hall. He watched.

At the iron-banded door there were four of them: the Prior, the Mender, Wren with a lamp, and Darrow. The evening's flour lay across the gap under the door, smooth and unmarked.

Mother Ione lifted the cord over her head.

"Do you know what the masons found when they cut the House's footings?" she said, to the key as much as to them. "This. They built on it, not through it. The Prior before me gave me this with the ring and told me to wear it, as she'd been told. She never turned it. Nor did hers."

The key went in. It did not want to turn. She put both hands to it, and it went over with a sound like a knuckle.

"Keys turned," Wren said under her breath. "One."

The door swung out toward them, and its foot swept the flour into a white fan across the flags. Then the cold came up the stair. It did not blow. It rose, the way water comes up into a boot-print on a riverbank, and stood in the doorway at the height of his knee, and the lamp flame leaned away from it.

The stair went down into the dark. Cut stone, older than the House.

Wren went first with the lamp, because that was the arrangement, and because she was on the second step before anyone could say otherwise. He put his hand on her shoulder. It was narrower than he had thought. Behind him Maelis had no lamp.

The cold stood round his knee and did not move off. Whatever it came from was down there, and was in no hurry either.

"One," said Maelis.

He put his foot down into it.

### Between — Room Under It

On the first step down, both feet on it now and the cold standing round his knee, Darrow kept his hand on Wren's shoulder and did not take the second.

He could feel the bone of her shoulder through the wool. She had been at his side since the litter. Up the Steps beside him, counting them aloud while he burned. A hundred and twelve paces to the tower door, run, so the bell would ring before anyone found him. Five round a fire and one at the winch-house. On her knees at a cold wall because he could not get down to it. And now first into the dark under the House, with the only lamp.

He was counting it, the way she would. He stopped.

"Wren."

"You've stopped," she said, without turning round. "Mender's on one, Captain. Two comes after you move. That's how counting works."

"Turn round a moment."

She turned, and the lamp came round with her and lit her from under the chin, so that she looked about twelve and very cross. "If this is where you send me back up—"

"It isn't." He had a joke ready for the face she was making, and let it go. "You've been good to me. Since the Steps, every day of it. And I've had you counting for me like a ledger. You're not one. I wanted to say so before we went down, not after."

"Technically I'm the House's ledger." It came out fast. "Sister Pell says so. Eleven hundred and seventeen Steps, whatever anybody tells you, nine keys on the ring and ten on the Prior, three doors into the hall, which I had at two and won't hear the end of, and—"

"Wren."

She stopped. Her lips, which moved along every row of lamps she passed, were still. He had seen her without a number twice before, both times on the day the grey cloaks came.

The lamp flame stood up straight between them, then leaned away from the dark below.

"You don't get to do that," she said at last, quieter. "Say a thing like that on a stair, with the Prior up there listening. Where I can't put it anywhere."

"Where would you put it?"

"In the ledger. Under something." She looked at his hand on her shoulder and not at his face. "Everybody in this House wants to know what my counts mean, Captain. You want the count. That's not me being good to you. That's me liking an audience."

He let her have that.

"Come back up, then," Wren said. "I'm not having you in the book going down and not up. Spoils the page."

"Down and up. You can count both."

"You'll say that." She turned back to the dark and held the lamp out over the next step down. "Times the Captain has said a decent thing on a stair: one. There's room under it."

Behind him Maelis had not said a word. He did not have to turn round to know she had been watching the knee the whole time, and not them.

"Still one," she said.

### Scene 4 — What Is Given

Wren's new count still stood at one, with room under it, when Maelis said "Two," and Darrow took the second step down.

His right hand was on Wren's shoulder and his left was flat on the wall, because those were the terms. The steps were cut out of the rock and worn hollow in the middle, each one a shallow dish, as if a great many feet had come this way once and then, for a very long time, none. At every one the knee stopped to think about it. He let it. Maelis gave it the next number when it had finished, and not before.

"Three," she said. A step below him Wren said "Three" as well, under her breath, half a beat behind, the way her lips went along a row of lamps. Two counts. One was for the knee. The other was for the book.

The cold stood round his knee and got no higher. It could not get into his hands. They were warm tonight to the ends of the fingers, and he could feel the warmth of Wren's shoulder through the wool under one and the cold of the stone under the other, and he went down between the two.

"Go on, you coward," he told the knee, under his breath, and it went.

At seven there was a noise in the hall above them: boots scraping, two men grunting under a weight, and a voice he would have known in any dark there was, telling them what their mothers had been.

"Gently, you pair of— there. Down. Not on the leg. On the arse. I've only the one of those as well."

He could not turn round on a stair, and did not try.

"Captain." Hollis's voice came down the shaft, filling it. "Look at the wall. Not the floor. The floor's coming whether you watch it or not. Look at the bloody wall."

He looked at the wall.

His own hand was on it, pale in the lamplight, and under the hand the stone was not smooth. He had felt it for four steps and put it down to the masons. It was cut. A line of fine strokes and dots ran down beside the stair at the height of his shoulder, angular and deep as a thumbnail, going down ahead of the lamp the way the stair went.

He knew the hand. He had seen it every few days since the Steps, coming up pale over his own palms when it pleased. Nobody had read it in three hundred years, and when it came for him he could read it anyway. Here on the stone it did nothing of the kind. It lay still under the lamp and waited, like letters on a slate for a boy who has not learned them.

"Eight," said Maelis. "You've stopped."

He went on, and tried to do both. The knee would not have it. Whenever he gave the wall his eyes the knee took them back, and whenever he gave the knee its due the line had gone on without him. He got one shape, a long stroke with two dots above it like a man with his hands up, three times, and could make nothing of it. Then the stair had the rest of him.

Wren counted. At twenty, Hollis's voice came down to them smaller. At thirty it was only a noise, and then not even that.

"Thirty-nine," Wren said, and the lamp came level. "Down."

---

The floor at the foot of the stair was flagged and dry, and colder than the stair had been. A short passage ran ahead to an inner door. No door was left in it, only the frame, a square of black, and over the black a lintel cut deep in the same hand: one clean line, the strokes as sharp as if the mason had gone up to his supper an hour ago.

Wren held the lamp up to it.

The first shape was the man with his hands up. He had it. He nearly had what came after, it was there the way a name is there at the back of the tongue—

"It says—" Maelis began, behind him.

"*What is given can be taken*," said Wren.

She read it straight off, her lips going along it in the right order. Then her lips stopped with the shape of the next word on them, and nothing came after it. The lamp stood very still.

"*What is built is yours*," said Maelis. She said it flat, to the lintel, as if she were putting it into plain speech for a slow pupil and nobody had spoken before her. "That's what it says. You've heard it now. You'll not hear it in the hall."

That last was for Wren. Wren did not answer. Under his hand her shoulder had gone as hard as the wall.

He had heard the second half before, in a cell, through pipe smoke: *It can't be poured. It can't be taken.* Two people under this House could read a hand nobody had read in three hundred years, and he was neither of them. He had a joke for that, and let it go. He closed his hand once on Wren's shoulder and left it there, and said nothing, and after a moment she breathed out and the lamp moved again.

They went through.

The hall beyond was older than the House. It was low and vaulted, the pillars cut out of the living rock, and the lamp reached the first two pairs of them; the rest went off into the dark in rows. The walls were painted. People stood along them larger than life, faded to the colours of old harness, men and women in a long rank with their hands held open at the waist, and over every pair of open hands, laid on in a pale paint the lamp turned gold, hung a small cloud of strokes and dots.

He looked down at his own palms before he could stop himself. They were only palms.

"Captain," said Wren. It was her first word since the lintel. She had taken the lamp to the far end of the rank.

One of them knelt. A knight in a knight's harness, on both knees before a stone the painter had made black, with his head bowed and both hands flat on the stone. There was no cloud over his hands. The pale paint came out of him instead, out of the chest, in a single fine line, and ran up. Up the wall, up past the heads of the standing figures, a thread drawn tight into the vault where the lamp could not follow.

The others held theirs. His went up.

There were strokes beside the knight's head, faint, the paint half flaked away. He put his face a hand's breadth from the wall and tried them. The man with his hands up again; something after it that would not come. He got nothing, and Wren held the lamp for him the whole time and did not read it. He noticed that she did not.

Maelis was no longer behind them. He found her by her voice first, and then by her shape against the far pillars, out in the dark past the lamp, where he could not have gone a yard without a hand out in front of him. She was walking. She went round a fallen block of stone without looking down at it, and stopped at a pillar the way a woman stops at her own door.

The flour had lain smooth under the iron-banded door. The Prior's key had not been turned by the Prior before her, nor by hers.

"Mender," he said.

"Look at the floor," said Maelis, from the dark. "Now. Ser Hollis isn't here to stop you."

He looked. Out beyond the pillars, past where she stood, the flags were white. Rime, furred thick, and on the far wall it climbed. It grew up the stone in feathers toward the vault, the way frost comes up a window from the sill, except that nothing up there was cold. Up there, as near as he could work it, was the infirmary hall. The row. Every cot on it.

"What's at the end of it?"

"Tonight? The stair." She came back into the lamplight. "You said you'd look, then come up. You've looked."

He thought about walking on into the white. He thought about it with his whole body, the way he had thought about the last four steps of the warming-room stair, and Maelis watched him think it, and waited, and did not take his wrist.

"Up," he said.

"One," said Maelis.

---

They came back up the same way, with the cold on their backs. Up was easier. Going up the knee only had to push, and it pushed, sullen, a step at a time, at her count. Halfway, Hollis's voice found them again.

"Wall, Captain."

"I'm looking at it."

He was. The strokes went by under his palm the other way, and he had the man with his hands up four more times and nothing else. Wren counted. At thirty the light from the hall came down the shaft to meet them, the supper lamps at the kitchen end, and then Hollis's face at the top, red and upside down to him, because Hollis was sitting on the floor with his back to the doorframe and his one foot braced against the linen press. Mother Ione stood behind him with her hands folded.

"Thirty-nine," said Wren at the top step. "Up." She had the ledger out of her scarf before the lamp was down, and wrote two numbers side by side. "Down and up, both columns, same number. Technically that's a clean page."

"Did you come up on it, Captain?" said the Prior.

"I did."

"Then you've kept your word to me." She looked at him a moment longer, the way she looked into a full room. "You'll tell me what you saw when you know what it was."

Hollis squinted up at him from the floor. "Well? You look like a man who's been shown his own grave and didn't think much of the masonry."

"It's good masonry," said Darrow. "That's the trouble."

Wren's pencil had stopped under the two columns, where a line would go for whatever was over the door. She looked at the place for a moment. Then she shut the ledger on it, tucked it back into her scarf, and went to give the kitchen back its lamp.

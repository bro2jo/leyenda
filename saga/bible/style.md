# Style Guide — how the Chronicle is written

The test for every page: **a stranger who has never heard of ACL rehab, calories or this repo should read it and want the next chapter.** If a sentence only makes sense to someone who knows the real logs, cut it.

---

## 1. The wall between the Ledger and the Chronicle

The real world drives the story's **outcomes**; it never appears in the story's **content**.

**Never in the prose:** foods, meals-as-metric, calories, protein, macros, supplements, weigh-ins, body weight, exercise names (squat, bridge, step-down, bike, bench), sets, reps, loads, RPE, PT, physio, ACL, graft, surgery, rehab, phases, dates or numbers from the real logs, anything that winks at the reader ("Darrow felt like he'd skipped breakfast").

**Characters can still eat, sleep and train** like people in any novel. A camp meal, a feast, a soldier's drill all belong to the world. What's forbidden is mirroring: no scene exists *because* of a food log, and no in-world meal or drill corresponds to a real one.

**How real effort shows up instead:**

| Real side (Ledger) | Story side (Chronicle): show it like this |
|---|---|
| Fuel / Ember (nutrition) | warmth vs. cold in Darrow's body; color in his face; how fast cuts close; whether he lasts through a long scene; how far the cold gets in when Ember is low |
| Floor minimum, morning checks | quiet discipline: the dawn vigil, the Mender's forms, Warden's Eye; Darrow catching his own lies |
| Knee sessions | Maelis working the Binding: "the forms," slow and exacting, never named as exercises; the leg answering, or not |
| PT visit | the Reading of the Knots (Maelis's weekly examination) |
| Upper-body work | the Seated Blade; grip, shoulders, a sword that feels lighter |
| Conditioning | the Long Breath; the Steps; wind on the ridge; endurance in a fight |
| Sport-skill work | the Arts tied to them (see `mechanics.md`) |
| Time together on purpose (the Tether) | how far the second thread has been drawn, in the five words the Reckoning gives it (a name, a hand, a road, a door, a home): one planned scene with Rae Thorne per stage, written when the stage is reached, wherever the road has them. Rae is on the page by her own road and the chapter's plan, warmer or more careful by approval. **Never** a scene because an evening was logged, and a logged evening is never Rae on the page. |
| A strong week | the chapter's climax goes his way; allies arrive; the plan works |
| A thin week | the climax is costly; the enemy advances; a plan fails. **This is a plot turn, never a punishment and never a lecture.** |
| A red-light/rest day (plan says stop) | a **cutaway** scene: the world moves without him, in another POV (Wren, Hollis, the House, an enemy) or with Darrow made to rest by others. Never a setback frame; resting is never failure. |
| A missed day (nothing logged at all) | No scene; the world moves without him. A day with only food logged is still a day, coloured by what was logged. The next scene after a missed day opens with **one off-page world move**, planned in advance: the grey cloaks a day closer, a companion did something alone, the weather closed a road. No guilt, no lesson. |

**Tone, never the slot's content.** What happens in a scene is fixed when the chapter is planned; the day's real deeds set only its colour, and the colour is Darrow's alone: warmth or cold in him, how long he lasts, how closely he reads a room, how he hears what is said to him. On a cold day a plain remark lands as short; it is the same remark that person would have made on any day, and the page keeps what was said apart from what he made of it. Everyone else acts from who they are, what they want in that moment, what they have seen, and how they stand with him, which only his choices move; nobody is warmer or shorter with him because of a day they could not know about. The **outcome** of the week is set only at the climax, by the tier. A warm day never buys a victory and a cold day never spends one.

Numbers that are *game* numbers (Level, Might 11, Ember "Steady", DC 13, a d20) may appear, but only inside Reckoning boxes and dice lines, never in narration.

## 2. Voice and craft: aims, not recipes

This section says what a good block does and the tools that usually get it there. It is not a checklist to satisfy: when one of these would make a scene worse, the scene wins, and the record's `gm.note` says why. What does **not** bend lives elsewhere and stays hard: the wall (§1), the formats the build parses (§3), dice only from the engine, the knee only as far as the real plan has cleared it, nothing the page has not earned (a name, a truth, an epithet), and the records.

**Point of view:** close third person on Darrow, past tense. At most one short **interlude** per chapter from another POV (Wren, Hollis, Maelis, an enemy, a distant hall), under its own heading `### Interlude — Title` (§3); it is not tied to a day.

### What every block is for
1. **A stranger wants the next one.** Every other aim serves this one.
2. **Something turns, because someone did something.** By the end a person can, must, believes, risks or chooses what they could not at the start (the slot's `turn`; if the plan has none, name one before writing). Darrow attempts something in every block, from wherever he is: he persuades, bargains, leads, tests a guess, hides a thing, jokes someone out of a temper, comforts, delegates, commits, refuses. A block that only explains is not done: let the fact arrive later, where it costs something.
3. **The people are alive.** Everyone who walks in is the hero of their own day and wants something that is not Darrow's problem. They notice different things first, go after what they want differently, and laugh at different jokes.
4. **It is clear.** A reader may open any block alone, on a phone, after a day away: who is there, where and when arrive in the first lines after a break (and in the first line of an interlude that steps back over a day already shown), and a name goes where a pronoun could point two ways.
5. **It sounds right read aloud** (the sound test, below).
6. **Its length is the writing's.** A block is as long as it needs to be to land its turn well, and the test is never a word count: does every paragraph earn its place, and is anything the scene needs missing? A Book's first scene, a death, an arrival or a council may run long; a quiet turn may be short. Book I's trouble was never length itself but beats that repeated and openings that waited; cut those, and add whatever makes the scene land.
7. **Its register is the moment's** (below).

### Register and tone
- **The baseline:** grounded and specific; concrete nouns, working verbs; earned emotion; people who are funny the way soldiers, carters and nurses are funny. Think *The First Law* dialogue, *Chalion* interiority, *Baldur's Gate 3* companions.
- **The register follows the Book and the moment.** Each Book sets its own (the arc's `bookN.tone`, printed in every brief), and each planned slot names a `tone`: comic, rowdy, warm, tender, wonder, eerie, tense, action, dread, grief, triumph. What happens decides it. A chapter moves through three registers or more, neighbouring scenes are rarely alike, and the climax has its own. A comic scene still turns, and a grave one may have a joke in it.
- **Let joy be joy.** Laughter on the page is out loud, at jokes that are actually funny. Triumph can be loud, a reunion warm in words, wonder unembarrassed. Darrow's humour is quick and his laugh carries (`cast.md`): let him use both. His jokes stop dead when something matters, which only lands if they run the rest of the time.
- **Silence is earned.** A held silence, a refused answer, a line said to the fire: rare, and placed where it costs something. One in a chapter lands; one in every scene is a mannerism.
- **The day's colour is not the register.** The colour (§1) is warmth or cold in Darrow's body and how he hears a room; the register is the scene's. A cold day in a comic scene is a comic scene in which he is cold.

### Dialogue
- **Dialogue does the heavy lifting**, each person in their own voice (`cast.md`). A voice line is a tendency, not a quota: write the person's tactic and the tic follows by itself.
- **The default is an answer.** Most questions get the whole answer, or most of it, in the speaker's own words; a practical question gets a practical answer; what the Annals mark `common` is told in full by anyone who would know it. Deflection, a question for a question, a wrong answer and no answer are seasoning, and no answer is the rarest, kept for what the arc keeps. People are also often simply wrong, and say so with confidence.
- **People do more than withhold.** They argue at length and out loud, tell a story, interrupt with good news, ask him for things, change their minds, gossip, apologise, sing badly, get excited, get it wrong. When two people's wants clash, let them have the argument, each with a point.
- **Under pressure and at ease.** The GM notes give each person a tactic under pressure and a register at ease (`_gm/characters.md`; the brief prints both). Most hours of most days are not pressure.
- **Every line does something to the person it is said to.** A line that only informs is cut or given a reason to be said now. The last line belongs to whoever's turn it is, and some blocks end on an action instead.

### Darrow
- **He drives.** Whatever his legs can do, his will is never a passenger: low capability, high initiative.
- **He can be wrong.** His reads are good and not perfect. Some of his mistakes come from who he is (moving too fast, deciding alone, wanting a thing to be true) and cost something he then mends in the open; the dice are not the only way he fails. A misread repaired is worth more than another flawless read.
- **He is changing.** Each Book's Darrow sounds a little different from the last.
- **The body is real.** Darrow's knee is a presence: stiffness at dawn, the hot ache after the forms, the terror of a stair going down, the moment it holds. Write it the way a wounded soldier would feel it, never the way a clinic would describe it.
- **The eye is his.** Close third means the page sees what Darrow would notice, in his order: in a fight the weapons, not the cloaks; in a sickroom the hands; in a room he knows, almost nothing. An entrance earns a detail or two, and the rest arrives in action across later scenes, or never. Suspect every *thought, knew, realised, wanted, felt, remembered*: show the thing he noticed and let the reader think it.

### Shape and pace
- **Enter late, leave early.** Open inside the moment and stop once it has turned. If the first paragraph could go without loss, it goes.
- **Crowd and leap.** Spend detail where the scene turns and cross the rest in summary. Telling is allowed: a sentence for three days of road, a page for the hour that changes something.
- **After a blow, a decision.** A quiet block that follows a turn (the reaction, the dilemma) still ends on the decision, so the next day opens with a want.
- **Outcomes with a *but* or an *and*.** Success with a cost, failure that opens another road, never a dead end (§3, Dice).
- **Vary the shape.** A block's shape differs from the last of its kind: after a question refused, a thing given freely, a game, a want that has nothing to do with him, a set piece, a comic disaster, a road in summary. Rotate the kinds of scene too: dialogue, action, investigation, quiet, travel, set piece.
- **Openings and endings.** Time and place can arrive many ways: a bell, weather, a voice, a thing in someone's hand, the middle of an action. End on movement (a decision, a door, a line, a new problem, a laugh), not always on a quiet gesture, and never on a summary.
- **When in doubt,** do to him not the worst thing but the most revealing one.

### Threads, lore and the Bearing
- **Plant and pay off.** The brief lists the open threads with their plant counts (`saga.py threads` for all, `--id N` for one). Every chapter should touch at least one open thread and plant at most one new one. A truth is revealed only after its plants have reached the page, twice, in blocks the thread's `on_page` list can name. Open small things and close them inside the scene or the chapter, so a reader has somewhere to rest whatever the calendar does to a chapter's length; the Book's questions stay open. **Pay before you plant:** a chapter that plants something new also pays or closes something old, and the threads are ranked, so a week advances one or two of them properly rather than all of them a little.
- **Lore is seasoning.** The Annals (`saga/bible/_gm/lore.md`) hold the realm's history, customs and songs, and the doctrine at their top is the rule. The scene's want, obstacle and turn come first, and lore never stands in for them. **No fixed count:** use as many touches as the scene's meaning needs (often one; more when the reader needs the world to follow what is happening, such as a word met for the first time). The usual shape is small: a name and a half-line, a custom done without comment, two lines of a song, a few lines in Darrow's head, through a person who would know it; prefer to keep a climax's action beats clear, and prefer that to a paragraph of history in the narrator's voice. A touch earns its place if it also characterises, raises the stakes, sets the place or makes the scene clear. Never a truth ahead of the arc. In doubt, cut it: the world is deep whether or not this scene says so.
- **The Bearing in prose.** Who Darrow's choices are making him (`saga/state/bearing.json`) is never a number or an axis name in narration. It shows as how people address him, which options he is offered, and the epithet in writs and talk. **A new epithet is given on the page at a climax or a Choice block only** (the brief says `EPITHET PENDING` while one waits): in a writ, from an enemy's mouth, in a companion's jibe; the record promotes it when that block carries the name, and the site shows it from then. A title of the lower rung is a thing people say of him before it is a name, and a daily scene never gives the name itself.

### Choices
- Each option is right for someone and costly for someone; none is the obvious hero's answer, and the cost is visible before he picks. At least one option tempts who he used to be. What he chose comes back on the page later where the reader can see it: the ledger keeps the books, the prose shows the echo.

### Habits to watch
Book I found its voice and then leaned on it. These are its tells. `python3 engine/saga.py reread` counts them in a block; the numbers are hints, not limits, and a scene that needs one keeps it.

| Habit | Try instead |
|---|---|
| "the way X does Y" comparisons stacked through a scene | keep the best one; let the others be plain, or become an action |
| negation beats: *did not answer*, *said nothing*, a "not a yes, not a no" pair | what did they do? let someone answer whole |
| lines said *to the roof*, *to the cup*, *to the valley* | people look at each other, or at the work in their hands |
| catchphrases in every scene (*Technically*, *Aye*, a signature line, a saying) | rare is louder: once a chapter unless the scene is about it |
| every scene opening on a bell, every scene closing on a small gesture | open mid-action or on a voice; close on a line, a laugh, a problem |
| Darrow reading every room "like a ford" or "a line before a charge" | let him read it wrong sometimes, or in someone else's terms |
| a beat that repeats, an opening that waits | the cut: the first paragraph, the last exchange, the beat said twice |

### When stuck (tools; use any or none)
- What does the *other* person want right now, however small, and why now? The plan gives a scene its turn and never the other person's want, and that is the line most worth finding.
- Who wants what, what happens if they don't get it, and why now?
- What is given whole and what in part, and whose last line is it?
- What would make Hollis laugh here, Wren count, or Rae say a second sentence?
- What is the most revealing thing that could happen to him in this room?

**The sound test.** Read the scene back as if aloud, before the bookkeeping (§4). Where it hesitates, where it drags, where it rushes a moment that should cost, the rhythm is telling you what the sentences hide. Only short sentences go monotonous, and so do only long ones; the one duty of a sentence is to lead to the next. Then find the clusters: three similes in a paragraph, two maxims in a row, a silence doing the work a line should do. Keep the best one and let the sentences around it be plain.

*Where these come from, for the curious: McKee's and Story Grid's scene that turns, Swain's scene and sequel, Mamet's three questions, Bujold's most revealing thing, Abercrombie's eye, Le Guin's ear and her crowding and leaping, Kowal's nested questions, Sanderson's promises and proactive heroes, Failbetter's consequences that echo, and the tabletop habit of failing forward. The aims above are the House's own.*

**Banned (or once a Book at most):** "a testament to", "little did he know", "in that moment", "a dance of", "the weight of the world", "he let out a breath he didn't know he was holding", "something shifted", "steeled himself", eyes that "sparkle", any sentence that tells the reader what to feel. No moralizing narrator. No "lesson" paragraphs.

## 3. Formats

*The brief (`saga.py brief …`) prints the exact form of the block being written; this section is the reference the build parses.*

### A daily scene (written when a day is closed)
- **Length follows the scene.** One scene: someone wants something, something stops them, something turns (§2), and it is as long as good writing needs to land that: no number pads a quiet morning or makes a council break in one line. Heading: `### Scene N — Title`. Appended to the current chapter file.
- **The opening never reads as a list of debts.** What is owed from before (a missed day's world move, the answer to the open choice, a Between's promise) lands where it fits, the most important early and in a clause where a clause will do; what does not fit this scene can land in the next (the engine keeps it due). When the plan sets a scene in two places, it moves once, cleanly, with the time between them said.
- **Story time.** A chapter covers roughly its own calendar week: a scene may open hours or days after the last, and says so in its first lines. Story time never runs ahead of the real calendar; the season and the feasts follow it.
- Every daily scene is exactly one **kind**, fixed when the chapter is planned: **spine** (advances the chapter's core beat, and is the default: every day moves the story; at least two per chapter, and Saturday's slot is always spine; when a gate comes early, a spine scene may carry a folded core beat's own choice, a **beat choice**, below), **quest** (one stage of an active side quest, run when the story is ahead of the Knot, or a Tether stage; at most two stages of one quest per chapter), **interlude** (another POV, at most one per chapter, heading `### Interlude — Title`, not tied to a day), or **cutaway** (a red-light day: the world moves without him; never a setback frame).
- Tone is coloured by that day's deeds (see the table above); the content is the planned slot's, and the outcome is set only at the climax. The plot moves forward every day regardless.
- A missed day gets no scene. The next scene opens with one off-page world move from the chapter's planned list, then carries on.
- Reckoning notifications, if the engine reported any, go at the end of the scene in a box.

### The Reckoning box (LitRPG notifications)
Rare in prose, always at a scene's end, never more than 6 lines:
```
> ⟦ THE RECKONING ⟧
> Art learned: **Iron Grip** I. *No one takes your blade from your hand.*
> Might 11 → 12
> Level 5 → 6 · Kindled
```
The first Reckoning (Chapter 1, Scene 1) is the only time it's described in prose: pale, angular script of light that only Darrow sees, which reads the same with his eyes closed. **A rank crossing is the one exception.** When the sheet's rank changes (Kindled at Level 5, Tempered at 10, then Warden-Errant, Emberknight, Unbowed, Warden of the Ember), the next scene written after it carries one short prose beat in which Darrow notices that the script has changed: the word under his name, how it sits on his palms, warmer or steadier than it was. Never a number, never an explanation, and nobody else sees it unless the page already lets them. The beat serves the scene's turn and never stops it, never in a climax's action beats, and that scene's box names the rank (`> Rank: **Kindled**.`). `saga.py now` prints RANK OWED until it is written; then `saga.py plan set rank_on_page=<rank>`. A level without a new rank stays one line in the box, and shows the change (`> Level 5 → 6 · Kindled`), so a level-up reads as one. The Tether's line, when a stage is paid, reads `> The Tether: **a hand**.`; the first of them is the only place the page says what it is.

### Dice (Baldur's Gate style)
Show the check on its own line, before the outcome is narrated:
```
`[MIGHT · DC 13]` d20 **14** +2 = **16** — *Success*
```
- Rolls come only from `python3 engine/darrow.py roll` and are written exactly as returned. **No invented rolls. No rerolls** except on Inspiration: a climax check that fails outright is cast again on his Inspiration, and again, until it succeeds or he has none left (`rules.json → inspiration.auto_reroll_on_fail`); `--reroll` is the same spend by hand. `roll` prints the casts with an Inspiration line between them, and they go on the page **together, in order, as one block with nothing between the lines** (the site shows them as one check), then the prose tells what the dice decided; the Reckoning box shows the spend (`> Inspiration 4 → 2`):
```
`[VIGOR · DC 8]` d20 **2** +0 vigor +1 chapter 3 Hard-won = **3** — *Failure*
`[INSPIRATION]` 4 → 3 · cast again
`[VIGOR · DC 8]` d20 **19** +0 vigor +1 chapter 3 Hard-won = **20** — *Success*
```
- Use rolls for moments with real stakes: 1–2 per daily scene at most (often zero), 2–4 in a chapter climax.
- A failure is never a dead end. It's a complication, a cost, a different road.
- DCs: **the brief sets them.** At a climax, `saga.py brief --climax` prints, for each kind of check, his modifiers under the frozen tier and the DC at which a full success has the tier's chance (`rules.json → week_score.tiers[].target_odds`: a Triumph's checks are very likely, a Setback's are even); roll at that DC. The tier's roll modifier stays in the dice line; the DC is what answers it, so a strong week shows in the odds and the die can still fail. In a daily scene or a Between, `python3 engine/darrow.py odds --stat … [--prof --art …] --dc N` says what a DC means for him now; easy is about three in four, hard about one in two.

### The weekly climax (written at the Sunday checkpoint)
- **As long as the decision and its cost need** (the space goes to the decision and what it costs, never to everything the chapter touched): the chapter's battle, confrontation or revelation. A climax carries its own beat; whatever else is owed and does not fit the moment waits for the next chapter's first scenes. Close the chapter with the week's tier (Triumph / Hard-won / Costly / Setback) setting the shape of the outcome, and the dice deciding the details.
- End with **a choice for Darrow** (2–3 options), in this format:
```
**What does Darrow do?**
1. **Give the courier his answer.** *(Maelis approves.)*
2. **Keep him talking and learn who sent him.** *[Resolve 10]* *(Wren approves; risky.)*
3. **Say nothing — yet.**
```
  Gated options show the requirement in brackets, a stat (`*[Resolve 10]*`) or a Bearing value (`*[Guile 1]*` for a first step, `*[Guile 4]*` for a lean); if Darrow doesn't meet it, show it struck through: ~~[Finesse 14] Leap the gap~~. **An option that costs Inspiration** shows `*[Inspiration]*` the same way: a bold way, or the reroll of a check that failed in the climax; it is open while he holds one (the brief says), struck through at none, and never the default. `saga.py choose` spends the point when he picks it; a reroll is then cast in the `### Choice` block with `python3 engine/darrow.py roll <the climax's label> --reroll --paid`, its line pasted as printed. A climax choice may move the Bearing by two or three; only a betrayal or a sacrifice moves it by four, and the epithet it earns must be spoken on the page.
- A climax choice carries across the first two scenes of the next chapter; if still open at the second close it resolves to the climax's planned default, recorded `by: bearing`, and its `### Choice` block is written then. **Except a Book's last climax:** its choice does not carry. The crossing (its `### Choice` block) must be on the page before the next Book's first scene, so if he has not answered by the first close of the new chapter, the default answers it there (`by: bearing`) and the crossing is written first, then that day's scene.

### A small choice (micro-choice, at the end of a daily scene)
One stage of a quest or a beat may end on a choice smaller than the climax's. Format, the heading `**What does Darrow do?**` or `**What does Darrow say?**`, each option bold:
```
**What does Darrow say?**
1. **"…"**
2. **"…"**
3. **Say nothing.**
```
- At most one open at a time, at most one per scene, never in two scenes running. Each names exactly one Bearing axis, ±1 per option; gates as above.
- It carries across one scene. If it is still open at the following close, Darrow answers for himself with the planned default (recorded through `/choose`, so its Bearing moves), and the Ledger says so in one line.
- Its consequence is folded into the opening of the next scene. No separate block, no `### Choice` heading.
- **A beat choice** is the same format carrying a folded core beat's own choice (the arc's options, each moving its own pole by its climax weight, and the flags it sets): a gate came early and the beat landed in a daily scene instead of a climax. It opens and closes like a small choice; what the tier decides about it lands at that chapter's climax.

### A Between (role-play; between two scenes)
- Darrow's own action in the story's present, played out: `### Between — Title`, as long as the exchange needs, close third, past tense, appended to the chapter after the last block. Every Between changes an understanding, a relationship or a small obligation: it may reveal an immediate motive or establish a commitment, and the disagreement gets the room that movement, game mechanics and closing silences give up. It never does the plan's work: nothing from the next slot, no scheduled revelation ahead of its time, no new name or place, nothing with the knee the plan has not cleared.
- People answer in voice and may refuse, deflect or lie. Zero or one die, only with stakes or a game, never with the chapter's tier. A failure costs a copper, a laugh or a line.
- At most two between two scenes; a third is two lines and the moment passes. It ends on a line that hands the moment back, **never on a choice list**. No Reckoning box. The next scene opens on it in a clause, then does its own slot. Consequences, when there are any, go through the ledger (`design.md` §9).

### A tale's piece (the past, paid for by a work)
- `### Piece K — Title`, appended to its tale's file (`saga/chronicle/tales/NN-slug.md`: `# Tale N — Title`, the italic line of when it is set, `---`, then the pieces in order), as long as the small story needs, past tense, close third on the tale's own person (Darrow's past, or Rae's). A whole small story each time: a want, a turn, a last image. No dice, no choice list, no Reckoning box; the present never intrudes except as an echo the reader can feel, and the prose never points at it. The last piece ends on the keepsake (an object, a name, a song or a saying) in the page's words. All of §1 applies; the work that paid for it never reaches the page.

### Devices on the page (a challenge, an invitation, the project)
- **A challenge** is a scene whose want has a few ways in. He tries them one at a time, in prose, each with its dice line where the roll happens; nothing is counted aloud, and the scene ends when the thing is done or the cost is paid, never on a tally. A lost one turns the scene; it does not stop it.
- **An invitation** is the last line of a close-day reply, in-world, one sentence: *Wren is at the door with her ledger and a question.* Never a list. If he answers, the Between opens on that person's first line, not his, and ends like any Between.
- **The project** leaves a trace: when a stage is written, the place's codex entry and the Now page carry one line of what changed, in the page's words, and every later scene set there has the thing in it without remark.

### A glimpse (after a log; not a scene)
One or two italic sentences, about 40 words at most, closing a log reply: the company in the story's present moment, wherever it is, between the last scene and the next. Rotate the kind: a moment where the company is, a line overheard, a tally from Wren's ledgers, a saying or verse of the realm, the body, the weather. Tone comes from how the day has gone, the sheet and the choices already made; content is texture only. A glimpse carries no plot, plants nothing, reveals nothing, names nothing the page has not named, binds no later scene, and never mirrors the log it follows. All of §1 applies. It never goes in a chapter file; it lives in `saga/state/glimpses.md`, which the site does not read. Full rules: `.claude/skills/log/reply.md`, "The glimpse".

### Chapter epigraphs
Each chapter opens with 1–3 lines from an in-world document, in italics, with a source line: *Wren's ledgers*, a Mender's maxim, a Confessor's writ, a lancers' marching song, a letter from an old friend, a children's counting rhyme. Original text only.

### File layout
- `saga/chronicle/NN-slug.md`, one file per chapter. Front matter line: `# Chapter N — Title`, then the epigraph (italic lines and a `— source` line), then a `---` line to close it, then scenes in order (`### Scene N — Title`, with at most one `### Interlude — Title` among them, and any `### Between — Title` pieces where they were played), then `## Climax — Title`, then the choice (`**What does Darrow do?**` and a numbered list). A small choice sits at the end of its scene under `**What does Darrow do?**` or `**What does Darrow say?**`. A consequence written by `/choose` for a climax choice goes under `### Choice — Title`; a small choice's consequence is folded into the next scene. The site build parses exactly these forms.
- Book openings get a title page line: `# BOOK II — <TITLE>`.
- `saga/chronicle/tales/NN-slug.md`, one file per tale, started by `saga.py tale bind`: `# Tale N — Title`, the italic line of when it is set, `---`, then `### Piece K — Title` in order. Nothing else goes in it.

## 4. The reread (before anything goes on the page)

Every scene, climax, Between and Choice block gets this once it is drafted and before its bookkeeping. The build checks structure; this checks meaning, and it takes two minutes. Nothing here is written down except what it changes.

1. **Canon.** What did this block make true that was not true before, and which sentence does it? Each such sentence is something seen, something someone reported, or something someone believes; the wording says which, and the records (`known_facts`, `codex.md`, `now`) say the same. A paraphrased secret is a spoiler the build cannot catch: if a sentence tells the reader a thing the page has not shown, it goes.
2. **Who knows.** For each new fact: the reader, Darrow, which others? The thread's *who knows* (`saga.py threads --id N`) agrees, or the record's `gm.threads` updates it. Nobody acts on what they could not know.
3. **Continuity.** Time of day and weather since the last block; where everyone is standing; what the knee has been allowed; the objects in hand. The Between before this scene, and the day's glimpses.
4. **Debts.** The DUE NOW items the digest printed, the open choice, a promise made in a Between: honoured here, or still owed on purpose.
5. **The turn.** The slot's `turn` happened on the page, and Darrow attempted something. If the block only explained, it is not done.
6. **The sound.** §2's test: the clusters, the closing line, the thought verbs, and the habits to watch. `python3 engine/saga.py reread` prints the block's length (information, never a target), the habits it counts, and its opening and closing lines.
7. **The cut.** Would the first paragraph go without loss? Then it goes, and the block opens inside the exchange. Does any line say what the block means? It goes, or it goes to the one person who would say it wrong.
8. **The tactic.** Every line of dialogue does something to the person it is said to. A line that only informs is cut, or given a reason to be said now.
9. **The register.** Did the block land in the tone it was planned for, or the one the moment wanted? Either can be right; when it changed, the record's `gm.note` says so, so the week's balance can be kept.

Then the record: `python3 engine/saga.py record '<the brief's skeleton, filled>'` (it writes the event and the note, moves the plan, rebuilds the Now page, the character files, the world file and the Codex, and runs `check`), then `darrow.py ship`.

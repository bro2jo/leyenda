# Let code referee while Claude narrates

Claude runs a long role-playing campaign well when it does only what language models are measurably good at (reading intent, voicing people, dramatising an outcome it did not choose) while code owns what they are measurably bad at: dice, arithmetic, running state, the calendar, secrets and refusal. That division is the one point on which Anthropic's context-engineering guidance, the academic work on LLM game masters, at least eight independent open-source Claude Code game-master projects and the commercial AI-GM vendors agree. A long arc holds when it is stored as bottlenecks, pressures and written-down debts that a script puts in front of the model at the moment they fall due, not as an outline the model is trusted to remember. Rules hold when outcomes come in a few bands with a mandated cost and the stakes are fixed before the roll; scenes hold when a short compiled brief, placed last in context, says who wants what and what must be different by the end. This repository already runs that design, and on prose-evidenced records, witness-gated relationships and its publish-time leak check it is stricter than anything the research found. Its gaps are narrower and mostly cheap to close: nothing independent ever reads a finished scene, several documents contradict the engine on mechanics, a handful of rules that research says models are weakest at still rest on the model's own judgement, and the standing instructions are heavier than Anthropic now recommends for the Claude 5 family. Most of the non-Anthropic evidence below was read only as abstracts or search summaries, so every figure is labelled by how well it was checked.

## Four grades of evidence, most of it second-hand

This report separates four kinds of support. **Vendor-documented** means Anthropic's own documentation and engineering posts, read in full on 2026-11-10 and covering the current Claude 5 family (Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5). **Measured** means a study reported a result; the researchers could not open arXiv, most blogs, Reddit, vendor documentation or tabletop sites in this session, so almost every measured figure comes from an abstract or a search summary and is marked "abstract-level" or "snippet-level". **Doctrine** means what tabletop designers and fiction-craft writers teach, also read through summaries. **Anecdote** means READMEs, blog posts and forum reports from people running Claude as a game master, a hobbyist practice whose largest project has 173 GitHub stars ([claude-dnd-skill](https://github.com/Bobby-Gray/claude-dnd-skill)).

Two cautions apply throughout. Anthropic publishes no guidance on game-mastering or fiction, so every use of its guidance here is a transfer from agent engineering. And no source of any grade describes a campaign of several months run by an LLM with a planned arc and then evaluates whether the arc held ([arc notes](../research_notes/Running%20RPG%20campaigns%20with%20Claude/campaign_and_arc_structure.md)). The report contains no story content: this repository's story-side parts are named only by their function, and its hidden numbers are left out. The six notes files it synthesises sit in `research_notes/Running RPG campaigns with Claude/`; five are safe to read, and the sixth, the survey of this repository, prints mechanic values.

## A long arc survives as bottlenecks, pressures and debts held in data

The failure that ends long campaigns is specific and has been measured. A January 2026 study finds that LLMs "frequently fail to bridge these long-range narrative dependencies, often leaving 'Chekhov's guns' unfired even when the necessary context is present", and that writing each setup down as an explicit foreshadow, trigger and payoff obligation substantially improves payoff (measured, abstract-level) ([arXiv 2601.07033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07033)). A 2026 benchmark of six narrator models, none of them Claude, reports **the best model keeping only 42% of runs free of factual conflict after 20 turns**; adding memory reduced violations without raising the number of fully successful runs (measured, abstract-level) ([arXiv 2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)). Anthropic reached the same conclusion from agent work: continuity across context windows came from a structured list of what remains, a progress log and a fixed start-of-session routine, because compaction alone "isn't sufficient" (vendor-documented) ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Having the past in context is not enough. The obligation has to be stated and put in front of the model when it falls due, and four structures from tabletop and interactive-fiction design do that. Each is a data shape a script can check.

The first is the bottleneck. Sam Kabo Ashwell's survey of choice-based games names the shape that fits an overall arc that still answers to choices: branch and bottleneck, where paths diverge, rejoin at fixed points and differ afterwards because of tracked state (doctrine, via summary) ([IFWiki](https://www.ifwiki.org/Standard_Patterns_in_Choice-Based_Games)). Chris Crawford's objection is that every fold-back "renders some decision meaningless" ([Crawford](https://erasmatazz.com/library/a-solution-to-interactive/a-solution-to-interactive.html)); practitioners answer that the rejoined story must read variables the branch set ([Blooloop](https://blooloop.com/theme-park/opinion/multi-branching-narrative/)). The working rule for a planner follows: fix what becomes true at a bottleneck, never how, and require that something the chosen road left behind shows up in the bottleneck scene. Justin Alexander's "don't prep plots" makes the same cut from the tabletop side: prepare situations and what must become known, and leave the order to play ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots)).

The second is pressure that advances by default. Dungeon World's fronts give each danger an impending doom and a short ordered list of visible "grim portents" (one to three for an adventure, three to five for a campaign), plus one to three open stakes questions ([Dungeon World, Fronts](https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts)). Apocalypse World's countdown clocks let the GM "put down a bid on future events without scripting or mandating those future events" ([The Daily Apocalypse](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/85-threat-countdown-clocks)), and Blades in the Dark ticks faction clocks forward in downtime ([Blades, Progress clocks](https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Rules:Progress%20clocks)) (all doctrine, snippet-level). Only the antagonists' future is written; the story that happens is the difference between that default and what the player did. This matters more for an LLM than for a human GM, because narrators left alone under-deliver setbacks: one study found over 51% of GPT-4 stories followed a single fall-and-rise arc against 30.7% of human stories (measured, older model) ([arXiv 2407.13248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13248)), and a major AI-fiction vendor trained a dedicated model because players "felt that modern AI models were too nice and never let them fail or die" (anecdote, vendor) ([Wayfarer model card](https://huggingface.co/LatitudeGames/Wayfarer-12B)). A clock the engine ticks does not depend on the model choosing to be harsh.

The third is the debt ledger. Brandon Sanderson teaches plot as promise, progress and payoff ([Odyssey Workshop](https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=19170)), and the foreshadowing study above shows why an LLM needs the promise written down with its trigger. The solo-RPG tradition supplies mechanics. Mythic keeps a Threads list and a Characters list from which its random events draw, a forced selection that would keep dormant threads alive against a model's pull toward whatever is recent ([Word Mill adventure sheet](https://www.wordmillgames.com/assets/mythicadventuresheet.pdf)). Ironsworn finishes a quest by a roll against its progress track, so an arc cannot simply be declared complete ([Starforged SRD](https://rsek.github.io/starforged-srd/moves/quest)). Mike Shea's version is ten one-sentence secrets per session, written "independent of where they might find them", about half of which get used ([Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/sharing_secrets.html); [Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/throwing_away_secrets.html)). The shared discipline is that every planted thing is a debt, so a planner needs a cap on new plants and a closing audit before an arc ends: each open promise paid, carried with a reason, or released on the page.

The fourth is a pacing vocabulary. Robin Laws' beat analysis maps each beat's movement toward hope or fear, which makes a long run in one direction visible ([Gameplaywright](https://gameplaywright.net/books/hamlets-hit-points/)); Mary Robinette Kowal's "yes, but / no, and" gives every attempt a consequence ([Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/16-41-middles-and-conflicts/)); Emily Short's storylets, content with prerequisites and effects chosen by state or salience, are the modular form for everything off the spine ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/11/29/storylets-you-want-them/)). The 2025 Drama Llama prototype let an LLM judge storylet triggers written in natural language, and a third-party summary reports authors struggling to keep those triggers consistent, which argues for recording arc-critical conditions as explicit flags ([arXiv 2501.09099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09099); [third-party review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/drama-llama-an-llm-powered-storylets-framework-for-authorable-responsiveness-in-interactive-narrative)).

How much of this is proven with an LLM at the table is limited. State kept in files, clocks, open-thread lists and storylets are attested in projects or papers, and foreshadow tracking has one paper; fronts with ordered portents, clue-redundancy lists, beat tagging and relationship thresholds were not found in any LLM game-master project ([arc notes](../research_notes/Running%20RPG%20campaigns%20with%20Claude/campaign_and_arc_structure.md)). Where projects do hold an arc it is light: one Claude Code skill keeps a three-act, six-beat arc that is revised when the player redirects it ("The arc bends, never breaks") ([claude-dnd-skill](https://github.com/Bobby-Gray/claude-dnd-skill)). The arc techniques above are designer doctrine applied by inference, not tested practice.

## Rules hold when code rolls, counts and refuses

Four weaknesses are measured. **Models are biased dice**: in the largest study found, 10 of 11 models passed none of 15 statistical distributions when each sample was a separate request, which is what a roll in play is (abstract-level) ([arXiv 2601.05414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05414)). **Running state degrades with length**: given an explicit state machine to step through, the largest model tested reached about 50% whole-task accuracy, and many branches per state were much harder than many states ([arXiv 2511.14777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14777v1)); in tool-grounded D&D combat every model's hallucinations rose with scenario length, in a study whose Claude entrant was the older 3.5 Haiku ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=3Op7kJOvaD)). **Adjudication yields to rhetoric**: across 20 adjudicator models applying Call of Cthulhu rules, neither model scale nor explicit reasoning gave reliable resistance to players arguing in narrative form ([arXiv 2607.02802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02802)), and in an LLM-only detective game 10 of 33 players talked the suspect into a full confession ([arXiv 2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.23043)). **Narration leans positive**, as above. None of the four has been measured on a Claude 5-family model. The nearest current evidence is a detailed October 2026 play log on Sonnet 5.5 whose author's verdict was "The story, roleplay, and planning went well. The friction was in combat procedure.", with many slips late in a large context (anecdote, read in full) ([issue #6](https://github.com/abaskett3/dungeon-master-claude/issues/6)).

The fix on which studies and builders converge is to narrow what the model can do to validated operations. Giving a game-master model a dice function and state functions improved both narrative quality and state consistency, and without the dice function the model stalled waiting for a roll it could not produce ([arXiv 2409.06949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06949)). A narrator paired with a separate archivist that records and retrieves state was preferred by 12 players over one large prompt ([arXiv 2502.19519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19519v2)). More agents is not the lesson: a 2025 study that added reviewer agents got better scenario progress but more rule violations and more spoilers ([Wordplay workshop paper](https://wordplay-workshop.github.io/pdfs/20.pdf)). Practitioner projects state the boundary bluntly: "LLMs propose intentions. The engine validates and executes. LLMs never directly mutate world state" ([mnehmos.rpg.mcp](https://github.com/Mnehmos/mnehmos.rpg.mcp)); "These files are the truth; your context window is only a cache of them" ([dungeon-master-claude rules](https://github.com/abaskett3/dungeon-master-claude/blob/main/kit/dm-rules.md)). Anthropic's enforcement advice matches: instructions in CLAUDE.md are context, not configuration, and "if something has to happen every time, make it a hook, a deny rule, or a managed setting" (vendor-documented) ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). The October play log shows it in miniature: a one-line instruction to show every roll did not stop rolls being dropped in long replies, and labelled script output with a hook as backstop did ([issue #6](https://github.com/abaskett3/dungeon-master-claude/issues/6)).

Which rules to run is less settled, because no study compares rule families under an LLM referee. The indirect evidence favours systems with few outcome bands and a consequence on every band. Powered by the Apocalypse games resolve everything in three tiers where "on a miss, it's never nothing, it's always something worse" ([The Daily Apocalypse](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/6-moves-and-dice)); Fate has four outcomes including success at a cost ([Fate SRD](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/four-outcomes)); Blades in the Dark fixes position and effect before the dice are rolled ([Blades play aid](https://community.bladesinthedark.com/t/notecard-play-aid-getting-a-handle-on-position-effect/1169)). Few bands are the easy case in the state-machine study, every band hands a positivity-biased narrator something it must write, and state shrinks to a few tracks and tags. Crunchy systems fail in documented ways, such as invented spell damage or a miss re-narrated as a hit, unless a complete rules engine does the work ([Xebia](https://xebia.com/blog/building-a-dnd-dungeon-master-out-of-claude-part-i/); [DEV Community](https://dev.to/austin_amento_860aebb9f55/prompt-architecture-for-a-reliable-ai-dungeon-master-d99)). Procedural doctrine lines up with the same weaknesses: roll only when success and failure are both possible and both change something ([The Angry GM](https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system)); let one roll settle the question ([EN World on Burning Wheel](https://www.enworld.org/threads/how-does-burning-wheel-play.226603/page-5)); never alter a result afterwards, which Justin Alexander calls "a betrayal of trust" ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/43708/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-9-fudging)). One Claude Code builder adds a step that makes "no softening" structural: compute every outcome tier before the die lands (anecdote, snippet-level) ([Testing in Production](https://www.testinginproduction.co/blog/playing-dnd-with-claude-as-dm)).

Agency needs three separate rules because LLM narrators fail it in three directions. They write the player's character for him, which published prompts forbid outright ([Feathered Fiction](https://featheredfiction.com/2026/01/05/solo-rpg-ai-gm-guide/)); they ignore or misread input, a named failure category in the 2026 benchmark ([arXiv 2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)); and they agree with whatever the player asserts. A student study found players preferred being redirected through the world, by an NPC's influence or an in-world consequence, over having an action refused ([Aalborg University](https://projekter.aau.dk/projekter/files/784680954/Narrative_Adherence_in_LLM_driven_Games.pdf)). No study compares open prompts with enumerated options or tests how much one turn should resolve. Chris McDowall's test is the usable one: a choice is real only with enough information, real options and an impact that arrives ([Bastionland](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09/the-ici-doctrine-information-choice.html)). Anthropic's policies permit dark fantasy and fictional violence within fixed limits, and Claude will always break character to avoid harm, so a design should give the model a sanctioned out-of-story channel instead of forbidding it to leave the fiction ([Claude's constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)).

Two findings bear on a campaign whose mechanics are hidden and tied to real behaviour. Design writing supports "discoverable systems", rule sets that explanation would spoil ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/developers-wont-hold-hand/)), with the counterweight that players need enough information to choose. The reconciliation is to hide the exchange rates and show the consequences, and it works only because a deterministic engine makes the patterns a player notices real. Gamified activity programmes, for their part, show a small-to-medium effect that fades: **pooled g = 0.42 across 16 randomised trials, falling to 0.15 at follow-up** ([JMIR 2022](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)). What backfires is punishment mechanics ([IJHCS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581918305135)) and displaying a broken streak, which lowers later engagement even when the break was outside the person's control ([Silverman and Barasch](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)). Story and avatars support relatedness, and no game element was found to raise felt autonomy ([Sailer et al.](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109059/109059.pdf)), so felt choice has to come from decisions inside the fiction.

## Context works as a thin standing layer, state on disk and a brief placed last

Anthropic's position since September 2025 is that context is an "attention budget": every token draws on it, recall degrades as the window fills, and the aim is "the smallest set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of your desired outcome" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). For long work it recommends a small always-loaded instruction layer, state persisted outside the window ("Use structured formats for state data", free text for notes, git for checkpoints), retrieval just in time, and a fresh window in preference to compaction because current models are "extremely effective at discovering state from the local filesystem" ([Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). In Claude Code terms: keep CLAUDE.md to facts needed in every session, under 200 lines, because "longer files consume more context and reduce adherence"; put procedures in skills, which cost about 100 tokens each until used; put rules with no exceptions in hooks ([Claude Code memory docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory); [Agent Skills overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/overview); [Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

The case against loading the story so far is about quality before cost. The current Claude models all have a 1M-token window by default and cache reads cost as little as 2.5% of input, yet the page that documents the window says "more context isn't automatically better" ([Context windows docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows); [Prompt caching docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). A 2025 study of 18 models, Claude 4 models among them, found all degraded as input grew; on a conversational-memory test **a focused prompt of about 300 tokens outscored the full history of about 113,000 tokens that contained the same answer** (measured, snippet-level, previous model generation) ([Chroma Research](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)). A campaign archive is the worst case that study describes: the same people in dozens of scenes with superseded moods, places and knowledge, each a plausible wrong answer to how one character regards another now. The large window earns its place for occasional whole-archive passes run in a separate context that hands back a short result, such as a continuity audit ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)).

The AI-fiction tools reached the same layout independently, starting on context windows of a few thousand tokens. SillyTavern, NovelAI and AI Dungeon all keep a tiny always-on memory, lore entries triggered under an explicit token budget, a steering note injected close to the newest text, a rolling summary plus retrieval, and recent history verbatim (vendor docs, snippet-level) ([AI Dungeon help](https://help.aidungeon.com/faq/what-goes-into-the-context-sent-to-the-ai); [SillyTavern docs](https://docs.sillytavern.app/usage/core-concepts/worldinfo/); [NovelAI docs](https://docs.novelai.net/en/text/editor/storysettings)). Four lessons transfer. What should shape the next output goes last, which matches Anthropic's finding that a query placed after long material improves quality by up to 30% in its multi-document tests ([Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Always-on memory stays tiny. Budgets must be explicit and overflow loud, because the recurring user complaint is entries that matched and were silently dropped, and rolling summaries that compound their own errors (anecdote) ([r/AIDungeon](https://axebps-redlib.hf.space/r/AIDungeon/comments/1ivhg9n/dungeon_ais_memory_system_is_bad)). And keyword triggering is brittle: a game master who has planned a scene already knows its cast and place and can select by rule. The layout that follows from vendor guidance and tool practice together is below.

| Layer | What it holds | When it loads |
|---|---|---|
| Always loaded | The GM's role, the few invariant rules, a routing table from message kinds to procedures, pointers | Every session; survives compaction |
| Path-scoped rules | Prose style rules, rules for public output | When a story file is touched; summarised away by compaction |
| Skills | One procedure each (close a scene, resolve a choice, plan a chapter), critical steps first | When the turn calls for one |
| Reference by section | World bible, cast, arc plan, GM-only truths, past chapters | Only the section needed, through a tool |
| The brief | Where things stand, the cast with current relationships, what is due, the recent tail verbatim, scene-relevant secrets with handling directions, what the scene must do | Immediately before writing, last in context |
| The record | A structured statement of what the scene made true | Written after the scene, never loaded wholesale |

For canonical memory, research systems and practitioner projects point one way, though none of it is a head-to-head measurement: an append-only log of what happened, structured records of people, relationships and open threads as the source of truth, and a brief derived from them, with prose summaries as a convenience layer ([Generative Agents](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.03442v2) and [MemGPT](https://ar5iv.org/html/2310.08560), both 2023; [DOME](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.13575)). One engineering write-up reports Claude misreporting the game world until it was made to keep a ledger separating what the source book says from what had happened at the table ([Xebia](https://xebia.com/blog/building-a-dnd-dungeon-master-out-of-claude-part-i/)).

Secrets follow the same logic. A "do not reveal" instruction is a backstop: a 2025 study extracted secrets from LLM game characters despite system-level prohibitions (snippet-level, with inconsistent figures) ([arXiv 2508.19288](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19288)). In a solo campaign the realistic leak is accidental, because to the model everything in context is equally known. The dependable techniques are structural: store unrevealed material apart from what the page has established; pass each scene only the truths that bear on it, each with a handling direction; record every reveal as an event that moves a fact into canon; and scan every reader-facing output mechanically. One developer's rule is that a consequence "will never be added to any context prompt unless" its trigger has happened ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/how-i-built-an-llm-based-game-from-scratch-86ac55ec7a10/)). Anthropic adds that a reviewer in a fresh context "isn't swayed by the reasoning that produced" the work ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

Advice written for older models has expired in specific ways. Prefilling the assistant turn, the role-play community's main steering trick, returns an error on Claude 4.6 and later; the replacement is a reminder in the user turn or a tool result ([Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Capitalised "CRITICAL / MUST" wording now causes over-application. Fable 5.2 "follows style rules closely", so hard numeric limits make its prose choppy, and "skills developed for prior models are often too prescriptive for Claude Fable 5 and can degrade output quality"; the remedy is to state each rule's scope and reason and delete cautions whose reason is gone ([Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2); [Prompting Claude Fable 5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5)). Prompts that ask the model to print its reasoning can be refused on Fable-class models, so planning belongs in thinking or in a separate call. After compaction Claude Code re-injects the root CLAUDE.md, summarises away path-scoped rules and tool output, and re-injects each invoked skill only up to 5,000 tokens from the top ([Claude Code context-window docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/context-window)). The "lost in the middle" curve and the persona-drift results come from 2023 models and are not evidence about current behaviour.

## Strong scenes need a turn, filled slots and a reader who did not write them

What LLM fiction gets wrong is measured at the level of words and shape. A 2026 study of 20,000 stories from four current models found **11 words, among them stock names, settings and professions, in 88.3% of the stories**, and traced them to post-training preference data, not to literature (snippet-level) ([arXiv 2605.26492](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492)). The "not X, but Y" construction is common enough to make up a quarter of one public slop metric ([slop-score](https://github.com/sam-paech/slop-score)). LLM stories are "homogeneously positive and lack tension" (2024 models, including Claude 3 Opus) ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.978)), and professional writers editing paragraphs from three model families, Claude 3.5 Sonnet among them, found none better than the others ([arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)). For the current generation the only statement is Anthropic's: Fable 5.1 has "few stock phrases" but a residual tendency to "mannered prose" ([Prompting Claude Fable 5.1](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)). Em-dash overuse, therapist-voiced dialogue and reflective closers are widely reported by practitioners and counted by no study found.

The mitigations with evidence are structural. Planning before drafting improved human-judged coherence in the pipelines that tested it, on older models ([Re3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06774v2); [DOC](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.10077v3)). Supplying turning points and arcs explicitly improved diversity, suspense and arousal by over 40% ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.978)). Selecting among candidate drafts with a trained quality model won 66% expert preference ([arXiv 2504.07532](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07532v2)). Editing by an LLM helps less than editing by a writer, because "LLMs don't inherently know what aspects of the writing needs improvement" ([arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)). Banned-phrase lists are the weakest tool available through the Claude API. Suppression works at the sampler, where a pattern is detected and regenerated ([arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)), and an API user can only imitate that with a script that scans the finished draft. In the prompt, reports are mixed, and Anthropic's own finding on a persistent default is that a generic "don't" tends "to shift the model to a different fixed palette rather than producing variety", while naming a concrete alternative or asking for several options first works ([Prompting Claude Opus 4.8](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8)). Examples are copied closely, so three to five varied ones beat one, and the stock-name result implies the cheapest fix of all: never leave a name, a job or a place for the model to fill.

The craft sources agree on one checkable requirement. Robert McKee holds that if the value at stake "stays unchanged from one end of a scene to the other, nothing meaningful happens" ([McKee](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)); Dwight Swain's scene is goal, conflict, disaster ([Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel)); Matt Bird's is "a compelling collision of competing agendas" ([Kill Zone](https://killzoneblog.com/2023/09/the-ultimate-story-checklist.html)). A brief that states who wants what, what stands in the way with a want of its own, and what is different at the end encodes all three. The commercial drafting tools add two practical points: name every character present, which both pulls in the right reference and stops the model inventing people, and keep one instruction to one moment, because a whole scene packed into one beat gets rushed ([NovelCrafter](https://docs.novelcrafter.com/en/articles/8675715-crafting-beats)).

For recurring characters Anthropic's advice is a detailed description plus "a list of common scenarios and expected responses" ([Increase output consistency](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/increase-consistency)). In fiction those become lines of the form "when contradicted, she does this", which give a default-agreeable model licence for a character to refuse. Anthropic's 2026 interpretability work found that "therapy-like and philosophical discussions consistently caused models to drift" from their default persona ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/assistant-axis)); companion scenes resemble those discussions, so each present character's current state and want is worth restating in every brief.

On review, Anthropic names draft, review against criteria, refine as its most common chain, and says that "separate, fresh-context verifier subagents tend to outperform self-critique" (vendor-documented, about agentic work) ([Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [Prompting Claude Fable 5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5)). The limits are documented too. Zero-shot LLM judges did not track expert judgement of fiction on 2023 models ([arXiv 2309.14556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556v2)); a widely used LLM judge is acknowledged to carry a bias toward overused tropes ([EQ-Bench](https://github.com/EQ-bench/creative-writing-bench)); and Fable 5.2 obeys whatever conditions a reviewer attaches ([Prompting Claude Fable 5.2](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2)). The design that follows is a reader that reports and does not rewrite, checks concrete things (did the scene turn, does anything contradict the record, which phrases repeat), and whose notes are not carried forward as standing rules. No game-master project surveyed runs a critic on prose at all, and no measured account of critics flattening Claude's prose was found.

## This repository already runs the pattern, and exceeds surveyed practice in four places

Everything said here about the repository comes from a read-only survey of its reader-safe files and engine code, made without opening any GM-side file ([repository survey](../research_notes/Running%20RPG%20campaigns%20with%20Claude/this_repo_current_system.md)). That survey prints mechanic values this report leaves out, so skip it if you want to keep discovering them. Nothing below describes story content.

The system is the narrator-and-archivist split with code as the archivist. Every block of prose is bracketed by two engine calls. `saga.py brief` assembles a bounded, sectioned packet: what the block must carry, continuity from the records, the people present with their current regard and behaviour lines, open threads each with a handling state, the arc section, tone, format and a pre-filled record skeleton. `saga.py record` takes the filled skeleton back, validates it against the page, appends one event, rebuilds every derived view and runs a consistency check. A typical scene brief is about 5,000 tokens and a climax brief about 9,000, built from fixed-size tails so that "a long story costs the same per block as a short one" ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)). After about 93,000 words and 94 recorded blocks, the design is doing what the research says such a design should.

| What the evidence supports | What the repository does | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| Code owns dice, arithmetic and state | Engine-only dice, every cast logged and final; every total computed; commits blocked while checks fail | Matches |
| State on disk, rebuilt and checked | One structured record per block; all current-state views rebuilt from records and verified fresh | Ahead of surveyed practice |
| A compiled brief, last in context, at constant cost | Sectioned brief from capped tails, delivered as tool output just before writing | Matches |
| Selection by rule, not keyword luck | Cast chosen from the slot's plan text, lore by explicit id, callbacks by fit | Matches, with a blind spot for people the plan does not name |
| Debts written down and surfaced when due | Consequence ledger, undated callbacks, standing rules, threads with plant counts, all printed in the brief | Matches; no closing audit of threads found |
| Few outcome bands, stakes fixed before the roll | The week's real-world adherence fixes the climax's outcome shape and odds before any die; partial success exists | Matches |
| A bounded relationship variable against agreeableness | Regard moves only through recorded entries, and only for characters who witnessed or learned of the event | Ahead of surveyed practice |
| Secrets separated structurally, outputs scanned | GM-only directories, a write-time gate on unspoken names, a publish-time build that fails on GM sentences | Ahead; paraphrase and tool output remain open |
| Thin standing layer, procedures on demand | Skills, a path-scoped rule file, a session-start hook; but a dense always-loaded file and a long style guide read for every scene | Partly |
| An independent check of the prose | The writer's own reread plus a small regex linter | Diverges |
| Hide the rates, show the consequences | Mechanics never explained unless asked; a choice's cost visible before he picks; the sheet on request | Matches |
| No loss mechanics, no shame | Missed days cost nothing; overshooting earns nothing; a planned rest is excused | Matches, with one item to audit |
| Pressure that advances by default | Planned world moves each chapter; faction standing; a countdown with shown steps not found in the engine | Unknown (the arc file was not read) |

Four things go beyond anything the research found. State is event-sourced in the strict sense: prose is the truth, one record per block indexes it, and every current-state view is rebuilt and checked for freshness, a pattern the rules research did not find documented in any LLM game-master project. Records are evidence-gated: a fact about what someone thought or knew, and every claim that a debt was paid, must quote the block verbatim or the record is refused. Relationship values move only for characters recorded as having witnessed or learned of an event, which encodes who-knows-what into the one number most exposed to a model's agreeableness; no surveyed project ties a relationship score to recorded witnesses. And the secrecy boundary is enforced three times, by directory, by a write-time gate that refuses any name the GM files know and the page has not spoken, and by a publish-time build that fails on any GM sentence or unrevealed name, the most thorough leak check the research encountered.

The system also makes the real world the clock without making it a punishment. The day's real effort sets only tone; the week's sets the shape and odds of the climax before any die is cast; a real-world gate recorded with evidence is the only thing that ends a Book; a computed pace line tells the planner when to stretch and when to compress. That is the tiered-outcome, stakes-before-the-roll, no-loss design that the tabletop doctrine and the gamification studies describe, and the planning procedure also carries book-level structural requirements that the planner must place whatever the model's inclinations.

### Where it diverges

**Nothing independent reads a scene.** Quality judgement is the writing model's own nine-point reread plus a linter of seven regex habits; hard checks cover structure, canon, debts and leaks, not craft. Nothing verifies that the planned turn happened, that the protagonist attempted something, or that a quoted piece of evidence actually discharges the debt it is attached to, since the check proves only that the words are on the page. The style guide itself concedes that a paraphrased secret is a spoiler the build cannot catch. This is the gap vendor guidance speaks to most directly.

**The standing instructions are heavy, and in places they contradict the engine.** Before the first word of a scene the model holds roughly 4,000 tokens of CLAUDE.md, 2,500 of path-scoped rules, 2,500 of the day-close skill and 8,900 of style guide against a 5,000-token brief, so the scene-specific part is about a fifth of what it reads. The guide is already written in the idiom Anthropic recommends (aims with reasons, no word counts), so the issue is volume, in three specifics: a banned-phrase list that sits in the prompt and is absent from the linter; skill steps written as dense single paragraphs with many exceptions; and one 150-word paragraph duplicated verbatim in four files. Separately, the climax odds are described differently in the rules file, the reader-facing mechanics page, the style guide and the weekly-climax skill; how a failed climax check interacts with the reroll currency is described one way by the code and reference pages and another way by the engine's help text and that same skill; and three help or README passages describe behaviour the code no longer has. Anthropic notes that contradictions between instruction files are resolved arbitrarily ([Claude Code memory docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)), and one of the disagreeing pages is a reader-safe reference the player may open. The engine's end-to-end self-test exists and nothing runs it.

**A few rules rest on judgement exactly where models are weakest.** The dice command accepts any difficulty for a climax check, though the brief has already computed the right one. A thread can be recorded as paid off however rarely it was planted, though the brief shows its planting state. The size of a relationship change from a choice is unchecked, where free-play changes are trimmed by the engine. The price of a write-in answer and the number of callbacks paid per block are stated in skills and validated nowhere. These are the adjudication-under-persuasion and many-branch cases from the research.

**Memory has four soft edges.** The GM-side character notes are the one store that is neither a record nor a rebuilt view: hand-edited after each block, pruned by hand each chapter, checked only for size and a few stale phrases. People enter the brief by name-matching against the slot's plan text, so someone the plan does not name gets no entry. Two fields the record collects at every block, what the next block must know and what was kept off the page, are printed by no brief. And no procedure consults prose older than the last block or two, so a long-range callback depends on what the record captured at the time.

**Two design choices cut against tabletop doctrine on purpose.** Scene content is fixed a week ahead, which Alexander would call prepping plot, and the model writes the protagonist, which every AI-GM prompt forbids. Both follow from the format: an asynchronous serial with one scene a day has no live table to improvise with, and planned turning points are the best-supported quality lever in the generation literature. Agency is concentrated in choices, write-in answers and free-play scenes, and the free-play rule that the world declines in voice when an action would do the plan's work is the redirect-through-the-fiction approach players preferred in the one study. Two soft spots remain. That rule has no worked example and no check. And the advisory budget for free-play scenes has been exceeded in most chapters while their average length has more than tripled against a skill that calls them short; a soft limit that practice ignores is a line taxing every turn for nothing.

**Three leak surfaces remain.** One context holds the secrets and writes the reader's prose, so paraphrase is possible. The brief and the session digest print plan material into the session as tool output, and whether the player sees tool output depends on his client. The publish-time sentence check covers GM Markdown, notes and threads but not the strings inside the plan and the consequence ledger.

## What a verification pass would need

Three limits bound this report. First, sources: a verification pass needs the full text of every paper behind a figure here (the narrative-consistency, dice, rule-adjudication, function-calling, foreshadowing, stock-name and expert-edit studies, and the Chroma report), the primary tabletop texts (several procedures in the notes are tagged as recalled, not retrieved; none of those is relied on above), the SillyTavern and NovelAI documentation, the community threads where stylistic complaints about Claude would appear, and two practitioner series seen only as summaries, Xebia's and Jason Craft's three months of co-writing with Claude ([Craft](https://www.jasoncraft.org/workbench/posts/01-context-management/)). Second, currency: no measurement of context degradation, agreeableness, dice bias or prose tics was found for a named Claude 5-family model, so those claims rest on vendor statements and on data from earlier generations. Third, the repository survey read no GM-side file, did not assess the prose itself, did not measure whether the checks have caught real errors, and could not say how often the override flags are used. Whether the arc already holds a visible countdown, and how large the GM-side stores are, can only be answered in a session whose output the player does not read.

## Recommendations in priority order

The table orders twelve changes by how well supported and how cheap they are; the paragraphs after it give the reasoning. Items 1 to 4 are fixes. Items 5 to 8 are tests to run for two or three weeks and keep only if they win. Items 9 to 12 are design experiments and checks.

| # | Change | Evidence behind it | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reconcile every document with the engine on climax odds and reroll behaviour; delete stale help text; give the duplicated paragraph one home | Vendor-documented (contradictory instructions resolve arbitrarily; rules applied as written) | Small |
| 2 | Let the engine own the numbers it still takes on trust | Measured (adjudication yields to rhetoric; many-branch lookups fail) | Small to medium |
| 3 | Close three mechanical gaps: run the self-test in `ship`; re-run the digest after compaction; scan plan and ledger strings at publish | Vendor-documented; anecdote (a hook held where a prompt line did not) | Small |
| 4 | Print the two unprinted record fields in the next brief, or stop collecting them | Vendor-documented (continuity from prescribed re-reading) | Small |
| 5 | Add a report-only reader in a fresh context that sees only page canon | Vendor-documented for agentic work; mixed measured results on critics | Medium; test in shadow mode |
| 6 | Move phrase bans from the style guide to the linter; restructure the densest skill steps; A/B a pruned guide | Vendor-documented; measured at sampler level only | Medium |
| 7 | Complete the brief: a one-line stub for everyone present, a line naming what was cut, the original evidence for long-range callbacks | Tool practice (name every character; overflow must be loud) | Small |
| 8 | Make growing the GM notes a recorded step; run a whole-archive continuity audit in a subagent each Book | Measured (errors accumulate with length); vendor-documented | Medium |
| 9 | Test one default-advancing pressure clock, ticking on story time only | Doctrine; not attested with LLMs | Medium |
| 10 | Add a book-close audit of open threads; optionally tag each record with a hope-or-fear arrow | Measured (unfired setups); doctrine | Small |
| 11 | Decide the free-play budget one way or the other; add worked examples of an in-voice redirect | Vendor-documented; one student study | Small |
| 12 | Check two things only the owner can: whether his client shows tool output, and whether any reward shrinks with real-world lateness | Measured (streak and punishment effects); the repository's own rules | Small |

**Fix first (1 to 4).** The document drift is the cheapest high-value item because Fable 5.2 applies what it reads literally and because a reader-safe mechanics page currently describes something the engine does not do; pick the engine's behaviour as truth, correct the mechanics page, the style guide, the weekly-climax skill and the engine's help text to match, and keep the "grow the notes" paragraph in one file that the others point to. For item 2, make the climax roll look up its difficulty and its permitted number of checks from the engine instead of accepting them as arguments; have `saga.py record` refuse a payoff on a thread below the style guide's planting minimum unless a reason is given; validate a choice entry's relationship changes against the range the procedure states; and have the engine compare a write-in answer's recorded cost with the option it is priced as. Each is a rule a persuasive paragraph could currently bend. For item 3, wire `engine/selftest.py` into `ship` so the whole flow is exercised before every push; add the `compact` source to the session-start hook so the digest is regenerated instead of recalled after compaction, and have the writing skills re-run the brief if one was summarised away; extend the publish-time sentence check to the strings in the plan and the consequence ledger. Item 4 is a decision: either the next brief's continuity section prints the previous block's "must know" line, or the record stops asking for it.

**Test next (5 to 8).** The reader is the largest quality bet and the one with real risk, since one study found reviewer agents raised spoilers and rule violations. The design that avoids both: a subagent whose tools reach only the chapter file, the reader-safe character pages and the event records, given the new block and the one-line turn it was written to, and asked narrow questions that each demand a quotation. Did the turn happen, and where? What did the protagonist attempt? Does any sentence contradict the records? Does each evidence quote pay the item it is attached to? Does anything read as known that the page has never established? Because it holds only canon, the last question is the paraphrase check the build cannot do, and its report is reader-safe by construction. Run it at low effort for two weeks without acting on it, count how many findings were real, and only then let the writer revise flagged spans; never carry its notes forward as rules. For item 6, the measured support is for scanning finished text, so move the banned-phrase list into `saga.py reread`, which already counts the contrast construction, add the measured stock names, and leave the guide with definitions and a few varied passages in the target voice, rotated so the previous block is not the only exemplar. Rewrite the densest skill steps as ordered steps with each exception and its reason, critical steps first. Then test the pruned guide on alternating days for one chapter and rate each scene the next morning without knowing which guide wrote it: the player is the only expert judge who matters here, and blind ratings are the one measurement no outside study can supply. Item 7 closes the brief's blind spots with three small additions. Item 8 turns a reminder into a check by having the record state, per companion in the block, that their notes were changed or needed no change, and schedules the one job the 1M window is suited to: the whole chronicle is on the order of 125,000 tokens (an estimate from its word count), small enough for a subagent to read whole and return a short list of contradictions with quotations.

**Experiment and check (9 to 12).** Before building a pressure clock, find out in a GM-side session whether the arc already has one. If antagonist pressure advances only where the plan says so, test a single clock that the engine ticks at chapter close, whose next step must be shown on the page before its last step may land. It must tick on story time and on what the protagonist chose to oppose, never on missed real-world days, or it becomes the punishment mechanic the repository forbids and the research warns against. For item 10, the book-opening step already folds unrun plan items and lapses expired callbacks; add a list of every open thread with a required disposition of paid, carried with a reason, or released on the page. A one-word arrow per record would let the engine flag a run of scenes that all end upward, the measured bias. For item 11, the evidence leans toward removing the free-play budget, not enforcing it: the player starts those scenes, regard income from them is already bounded by the engine, and open input is where discovery-minded players create the most ([arXiv 2404.17027](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17027)). Whichever way it goes, the skill should describe free play as it is now played, and gain two or three short examples of the world declining in voice, which is Anthropic's "common scenarios and expected responses" applied to the one rule with no check. Item 12 is two questions. If the phone client shows the brief's output, the plan is on screen at every scene; because each session already starts cold and re-reads its voice from files, running brief, write and record inside a subagent should hide it at little cost to the prose, which is an inference worth one week's trial. And the survey flagged at least one story reward whose size depends on real-world timeliness; whether that counts as a penalty under the repository's own no-penalty rule is a design decision worth making explicitly.

## Conclusion

The research did not find a better architecture than the one this repository runs; it found the same architecture arrived at independently by people working from failure, and less of it. What the comparison changes is where the remaining risk sits. It has moved off memory, which the records and the brief handle, and onto two things the engine cannot see: whether a scene is good, and whether the instructions that surround the brief still say what the engine does. Both are problems of accretion. The one detailed play log on a current Claude model traced several of its failures to rules that were followed literally and were wrong, and Anthropic's guidance for the Claude 5 family says the same in general terms, so each new guardrail from here should be either code or a deletion, never another paragraph.

The second implication is about evidence. Almost nothing in this field has been measured on a current Claude model, and nothing at all on a campaign of this length with a real-world clock. This repository is in a position to produce that evidence for itself at low cost: a reader run in shadow mode, a pruned guide on alternating days, and a player who rates what he reads without knowing which version wrote it.
